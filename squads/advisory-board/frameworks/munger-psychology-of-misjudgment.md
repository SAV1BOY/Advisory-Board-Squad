# Munger Psychology of Human Misjudgment

## Purpose

Based on Charlie Munger's famous talk "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment," this framework catalogs the 25 cognitive biases that most reliably cause intelligent people to make bad decisions. Understanding these biases does not make you immune to them — but it provides a checklist for identifying when a bias may be distorting your judgment or the judgment of others. For advisory board members, this is the most practical psychology framework available: it explains why smart people make predictable mistakes and provides concrete countermeasures.

## When to Use

- When a decision "feels" right but you cannot articulate why with logic
- When diagnosing organizational dysfunction or repeated errors
- When negotiating, selling, or being sold to
- When evaluating why a team or individual made a clearly suboptimal decision
- During board discussions where emotions or social dynamics may be distorting analysis
- When designing systems, processes, or incentive structures (to bias-proof them)
- When you suspect groupthink, anchoring, or overconfidence in a discussion

## Core Model

### The 25 Tendencies (Grouped by Category)

**Incentive-Related Biases:**
1. **Reward and Punishment Super-Response** — People do what they are rewarded for, avoid what they are punished for, far more reliably than any other behavioral driver.
2. **Self-Interest Bias** — People unconsciously distort their views to align with their self-interest while genuinely believing they are objective.

**Social Biases:**
3. **Liking Tendency** — We are more easily persuaded by people we like. We distort facts to favor people we like.
4. **Disliking Tendency** — We distort facts to disfavor people we dislike. We reject good ideas from people we distrust.
5. **Social Proof** — When uncertain, we look at what others are doing and copy them. This works when the crowd is wise; it fails catastrophically when the crowd is wrong.
6. **Authority Bias** — We defer to authority figures even when they are wrong. The more confident the authority, the more we defer.
7. **Envy/Jealousy** — Comparison with others drives irrational behavior: overpaying, over-competing, sabotaging.

**Consistency Biases:**
8. **Commitment and Consistency** — Once we take a position, we defend it beyond reason. The more publicly we've committed, the harder it is to reverse.
9. **Sunk Cost Fallacy** — We continue investing in losing propositions because we've already invested. The past investment is irrelevant to the future decision.
10. **Status Quo Bias** — We prefer the current state, even when change would be beneficial. The default option has an enormous psychological advantage.

**Pattern and Belief Biases:**
11. **Confirmation Bias** — We seek, interpret, and remember information that confirms our existing beliefs. We actively avoid or dismiss contradicting evidence.
12. **Anchoring** — The first number or frame we encounter heavily influences all subsequent estimates, even when the anchor is arbitrary.
13. **Availability Bias** — We overweight information that is easily recalled (recent, vivid, emotional) and underweight information that is hard to recall.
14. **Overconfidence** — We systematically overestimate our knowledge, abilities, and the precision of our predictions.

**Loss and Risk Biases:**
15. **Loss Aversion** — Losses feel approximately 2x worse than equivalent gains feel good. This causes excessive risk aversion and bad decision-making around sunk costs.
16. **Denial** — When reality is painful, we refuse to acknowledge it. The more painful the truth, the stronger the denial.

**Reasoning Errors:**
17. **Cause-Seeking Tendency** — We find patterns and causes even in random data. We create narratives for events that have no meaningful pattern.
18. **Contrast Effect** — We evaluate things relative to what we've just seen, not in absolute terms. A $50 option looks cheap after seeing a $500 option.
19. **Stress-Influence** — Under stress, we revert to simpler, more emotional, less analytical thinking.
20. **Reciprocity** — We feel compelled to return favors, even inappropriate ones. This is exploited in sales, negotiation, and politics.

**Complex Biases:**
21. **Association Bias** — We associate messengers with messages. We shoot the messenger. We love the person who delivers good news.
22. **Simple Pain-Avoiding Denial** — Rather than face a painful reality, we construct alternative explanations that avoid the pain.
23. **Excessive Self-Regard** — We overvalue ourselves and our ideas relative to others and their ideas.
24. **Deprival Super-Reaction** — We react more intensely to losing something we have than to not getting something we don't have.
25. **Lollapalooza Effect** — When multiple biases align in the same direction, the effect is not additive but multiplicative. This is where catastrophic misjudgments occur.

## Steps

1. **Memorize the key biases.** Not all 25 need to be at your fingertips, but the top 10 (incentives, social proof, confirmation, anchoring, loss aversion, overconfidence, commitment/consistency, authority, sunk cost, availability) should be automatic.

2. **Before any major decision, scan for the top 10 biases.** Ask: "Which of these biases could be affecting my judgment right now?"

3. **When diagnosing others' behavior, check biases before assuming irrationality.** People who seem irrational are usually responding to incentives (bias #1) or social proof (bias #5) or loss aversion (bias #15).

4. **Design processes that counteract biases.** Silent idea generation counters social proof. Pre-commitment to criteria counters anchoring. External base rates counter overconfidence.

5. **Watch for the Lollapalooza effect.** When you see multiple biases pointing in the same direction, extreme caution is warranted. This is where the biggest mistakes happen.

6. **Build a bias journal.** Record instances where you catch yourself or others being influenced by a bias. Over time, you develop faster pattern recognition.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Board dynamics are especially susceptible to authority bias (deferring to the most prominent board member), social proof (going along with apparent consensus), and commitment/consistency (defending positions taken in previous meetings).
- When a board is about to make a unanimous decision quickly, pause. Unanimity often signals social proof, not genuine agreement. Appoint a devil's advocate.
- Help founders recognize when they are in denial about a failing product, a bad hire, or a deteriorating market position. Denial is the most destructive bias for startup founders.

### For Organizational Design
- Build bias countermeasures into processes, not just training. Training awareness of biases is necessary but insufficient — people still fall prey to biases they know about.
- Blind review processes counter liking/disliking bias. Pre-committed criteria counter anchoring. External audits counter self-interest bias.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Believing awareness prevents bias.** It doesn't. Biases operate unconsciously. Structural countermeasures (processes, checklists, external review) are more effective than awareness alone.

2. **Seeing biases only in others.** The bias blind spot: we see biases in others but not in ourselves. The most dangerous biases are the ones affecting you right now.

3. **Overusing bias labels as dismissals.** "That's just confirmation bias" is not an argument. Identify the bias, then provide the counter-evidence.

4. **Ignoring the Lollapalooza.** When multiple biases combine, the compound effect is devastating. Individual bias-checking misses the combinatorial danger.
