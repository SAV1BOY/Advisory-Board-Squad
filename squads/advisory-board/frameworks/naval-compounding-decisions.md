# Naval Compounding Decisions Framework

## Purpose

Naval Ravikant emphasizes that life and business outcomes are determined not by individual decisions but by the compound effect of many decisions over time. Like financial compound interest, good decisions compound — each good decision improves the conditions for the next. Bad decisions compound negatively. This framework helps leaders understand the compounding nature of decisions and prioritize the decisions that have the highest compound return.

## When to Use

- When prioritizing which decisions deserve the most attention and rigor
- When evaluating the long-term impact of seemingly small decisions
- When building habits, processes, or cultures (which are compounding decision systems)
- When a founder is focused on big, dramatic decisions while neglecting the daily ones that compound
- When coaching on personal or organizational development

## Core Model

### The Compounding Principle
The value of a decision = immediate impact + compound value of all future decisions it enables or improves.

Most people evaluate decisions by their immediate impact. The compounding framework evaluates them by their total lifetime impact, including the chain of improved decisions they enable.

### High-Compound Decisions
These are decisions that improve all future decisions:
- **Hiring great people** — they make better decisions in their domain, which compounds
- **Building good habits** — daily compounding of small positive actions
- **Investing in learning** — each new mental model improves all future analyses
- **Building trust** — trust compounds into better relationships, deals, and information access
- **Creating systems** — a good process improves thousands of future outcomes

### Low-Compound Decisions
These have immediate impact but do not improve future decisions:
- One-time optimizations
- Tactical responses to specific situations
- Decisions driven by urgency rather than importance

### The Compound Decision Formula
Long-term outcome = (Quality of daily decisions)^(Number of decisions over time)

A 1% improvement in average decision quality, compounded over thousands of decisions, produces a dramatically different outcome than a single brilliant decision.

## Steps

1. **Identify your highest-compound decisions.** Which decisions, if improved by 10%, would have the greatest long-term impact? These are usually: who you hire, what you learn, what habits you build, and what systems you create.

2. **Invest disproportionate time in compound decisions.** Spend 80% of your decision-making energy on the 20% of decisions that compound. Spend minimal time on one-time decisions.

3. **Build compounding systems.** Convert individual decisions into repeatable systems: hiring frameworks, decision memos, learning routines, feedback loops.

4. **Protect the compounding chain.** One catastrophically bad decision can destroy years of compounded value. Identify decisions that could break the chain and apply maximum rigor to them.

5. **Be patient.** Compounding is back-loaded. The first year of compounding produces modest results. The tenth year produces extraordinary results. Do not give up early.

6. **Track compound metrics.** Measure things that compound: customer retention (not just acquisition), employee tenure and growth, knowledge accumulation, process improvement rates.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Help founders shift attention from dramatic one-time decisions to the daily compounding decisions that determine long-term outcomes.
- Evaluate companies on their compounding metrics: customer retention rate, employee learning velocity, decision quality improvement over time.
- The most valuable advice often relates to compound decisions: "Who should you hire?" matters more than "What should you build?"

## Common Mistakes

1. **Optimizing for drama over compound.** Big, visible decisions feel important. Daily habits that compound are invisible but far more impactful.
2. **Impatience.** Compounding requires time. Abandoning a compounding strategy after 6 months is the most common and most costly mistake.
3. **Ignoring negative compounding.** Bad habits, toxic culture, and technical debt compound negatively just as powerfully.
4. **Single-decision thinking.** Evaluating each decision in isolation rather than as part of a compounding chain.
