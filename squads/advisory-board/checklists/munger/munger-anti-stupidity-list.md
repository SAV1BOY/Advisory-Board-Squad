# Munger Anti-Stupidity List Checklist

## Purpose
Implements Munger's insight that avoiding stupidity is more profitable than seeking brilliance. Most organizations spend their energy pursuing clever strategies while leaving the door open to predictable, avoidable mistakes. This checklist catalogs the most common failure modes in business decision-making and verifies that basic checks are in place before pursuing any ambitious initiative. It is easier to stay out of trouble than to get out of trouble.

## When to Use
- Before any major decision as a final sanity check.
- When reviewing a proposal that feels rushed or overconfident.
- During board review of management recommendations.
- When building a culture of decision quality.
- Quarterly to review whether the organization is making avoidable mistakes.

## Gate Criteria

### Cognitive Bias Protection
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Confirmation bias guard: evidence against the proposed action has been actively sought.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Sunk cost guard: the decision is evaluated based on future costs and benefits, not past investment.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Anchoring guard: initial estimates have been challenged with alternative starting points.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Overconfidence guard: confidence intervals for projections are wide enough to capture uncertainty.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Social proof guard: the decision is evaluated on its own merits, not because others are doing it.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Authority bias guard: the quality of the argument matters more than who is making it.

### Structural Stupidity Avoidance
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The initiative does not bet the company on a single outcome.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The worst-case scenario is survivable.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The initiative does not require everything to go right to succeed.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The team has asked: "Is there a simpler way to achieve the same objective?"
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The initiative does not violate basic unit economics that are unlikely to improve with scale.

### Historical Pattern Avoidance
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Similar initiatives by other companies have been studied, including failures.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The organization's own history of similar decisions has been reviewed for lessons.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Industry-standard failure modes for this type of initiative are known and guarded against.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The team can name at least three companies that failed attempting something similar and explain why this will be different.

### Decision Hygiene
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The decision is not being made under artificial time pressure that prevents adequate analysis.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The decision-makers are not emotionally compromised (excited, angry, afraid) in ways that impair judgment.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Multiple perspectives have been sought, including at least one skeptic.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The decision has been stated in simple language that a non-expert could evaluate.

## Red Flags
- "This time is different" is the primary justification for overriding historical patterns.
- The team cannot name a way the initiative could fail.
- Everyone agrees enthusiastically with no dissent or questioning.
- The analysis is complex enough that no single person fully understands it.
- The initiative requires sustained execution excellence with no margin for error.
- The downside scenario is described as "unlikely" without quantification.

## Escalation Triggers
- A decision violates one or more items on this checklist and proceeds anyway without documented justification.
- A pattern of avoidable mistakes occurs despite the anti-stupidity checks being nominally in place.
- The same mistake occurs twice within 12 months.
- A team member raises a concern from this checklist and is overruled without adequate explanation.
- The organization incurs a material loss from an avoidable error that this checklist addresses.
