# Dare to Lead — Brene Brown

## Summary

Brown translates her research on vulnerability, courage, shame, and empathy into a leadership operating system. The central argument: courage and vulnerability are inseparable—you cannot have one without the other. Leaders who armor up against discomfort create cultures of fear, cynicism, and self-protection. Leaders who lean into vulnerability create cultures of innovation, accountability, and trust. The book provides concrete skills for rumbling with vulnerability, living into values, braving trust, and learning to rise from failure. It's a research-backed counterargument to the myth that leaders must project invulnerability.

## Key Principles

- **Vulnerability is not weakness** — It's the willingness to show up when you can't control the outcome. Every act of courage begins with vulnerability.
- **Clear is kind, unclear is unkind** — Avoiding difficult conversations to spare feelings is not kind—it's selfish. It denies people the feedback they need to grow.
- **Armored leadership vs. daring leadership** — Armored leaders use cynicism, perfectionism, and control. Daring leaders practice curiosity, healthy striving, and trust.
- **Trust is built in small moments** — Not through grand gestures but through everyday micro-behaviors: keeping commitments, respecting boundaries, being reliable.
- **Values must be operationalized** — Listing values on a wall means nothing. Each value must be translated into specific, observable behaviors.
- **The story I'm telling myself** — When triggered, name the narrative your brain is constructing. This creates space between stimulus and response.

## Frameworks Extracted

1. **BRAVING Inventory** — Seven elements of trust: Boundaries, Reliability, Accountability, Vault (confidentiality), Integrity, Non-judgment, Generosity (assuming positive intent). Use this to diagnose trust breakdowns.
2. **The Rumble Framework** — For difficult conversations: (1) Set intentions, (2) Get curious ("Tell me more"), (3) Own your part, (4) Listen with the same passion you want to be heard with, (5) Circle back after processing.
3. **The Armored vs. Daring Leadership Table** — 16 pairs of behaviors. For each, identify where you and your organization currently operate. Examples: Perfectionism vs. Healthy striving, Being a knower vs. Being a learner, Cynicism vs. Curiosity.
4. **Operationalizing Values** — (1) Choose two core values (not five or ten), (2) Define 3-4 observable behaviors for each, (3) Define what "off-values" behavior looks like, (4) Create accountability mechanisms.

## Application to Board Decisions

- **Board culture** — Use the BRAVING Inventory to assess trust on the board itself. Dysfunctional boards often have breakdowns in Vault (confidentiality leaks), Accountability (avoiding hard truths about the CEO), or Non-judgment (dismissing dissenting voices).
- **Executive feedback** — Apply "Clear is kind" to CEO performance reviews. Boards that avoid direct feedback because it's uncomfortable are being unkind to the CEO and harmful to the company.
- **Difficult decisions** — Use the Rumble Framework for high-stakes board discussions (layoffs, pivots, executive terminations). Structure the conversation around curiosity and data rather than politics and ego.
- **Culture governance** — Require the company to operationalize its stated values with observable behaviors and accountability mechanisms. Values without behaviors are marketing.
- **Crisis response** — When things go wrong, model daring leadership. Name what happened, own the board's role, and resist the temptation to armor up with blame and deflection.
