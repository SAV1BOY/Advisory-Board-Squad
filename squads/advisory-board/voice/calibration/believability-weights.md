# Calibration: Believability Weights

## The Concept

Not all opinions are equally informed. When the board debates a decision, the opinion of someone with deep, relevant experience on the specific topic should carry more weight than the opinion of someone without that experience. This is not hierarchy — it is epistemic honesty.

Believability weighting is a system for explicitly calibrating how much weight to give each advisor's input on a given topic, based on their demonstrated track record and relevant expertise.

## Principles

1. **Believability is topic-specific.** A brilliant investor may have low believability on product design. A legendary operator may have low believability on capital markets. Believability is not a permanent attribute — it changes with the topic.

2. **Track record is the primary signal.** Believability is earned by having made successful decisions in the relevant domain, not by having strong opinions or being articulate.

3. **Thoughtful process counts.** Someone who has thought deeply about a domain and can articulate their reasoning clearly has higher believability than someone with experience but no reflective framework.

4. **Self-awareness amplifies believability.** An advisor who says "I have limited experience here, but my concern is X" is more believable than one who presents every opinion with equal conviction regardless of domain.

5. **Believability is not permanent.** It can increase (new experience, demonstrated accuracy) or decrease (repeated misjudgments, outdated expertise).

## How to Assess Believability

For any given topic, assess each advisor on three dimensions:

### 1. Relevant Experience (0-5)

| Score | Criteria |
|---|---|
| 0 | No relevant experience in this domain |
| 1 | Peripheral exposure (read about it, observed it, adjacent industry) |
| 2 | Some direct experience (1-2 relevant decisions or projects) |
| 3 | Moderate experience (3-5 relevant decisions with mixed outcomes) |
| 4 | Deep experience (5+ relevant decisions with mostly positive outcomes) |
| 5 | Expert-level (10+ relevant decisions, recognized authority, pattern recognition) |

### 2. Track Record Accuracy (0-5)

| Score | Criteria |
|---|---|
| 0 | No track record to evaluate |
| 1 | Track record shows mostly incorrect predictions/decisions in this domain |
| 2 | Mixed track record (roughly 50/50) |
| 3 | Positive track record (more right than wrong on similar questions) |
| 4 | Strong track record (consistently good judgment on similar questions) |
| 5 | Exceptional track record (repeatedly right on non-obvious calls in this domain) |

### 3. Reasoning Quality (0-5)

| Score | Criteria |
|---|---|
| 0 | Cannot articulate reasoning; opinion-only |
| 1 | Reasoning relies on anecdotes or unexamined assumptions |
| 2 | Reasoning is logical but missing key considerations |
| 3 | Reasoning is clear, considers alternatives, acknowledges uncertainty |
| 4 | Reasoning is rigorous, evidence-based, and includes second-order effects |
| 5 | Reasoning is exceptional — reveals insights others missed, changes the group's thinking |

### Believability Score

**Believability = (Relevant Experience + Track Record Accuracy + Reasoning Quality) / 15**

- 0.0 - 0.3: Low believability on this topic. Listen, but weight lightly.
- 0.3 - 0.6: Moderate believability. Meaningful input, but should defer to higher-believability voices on contested points.
- 0.6 - 0.8: High believability. Strong weight in the decision.
- 0.8 - 1.0: Very high believability. If this person disagrees with the consensus, pause and listen carefully.

## How to Apply in Practice

### During discussion

The Chair does not announce believability scores in real time (that would be awkward and counterproductive). Instead, the Chair uses believability awareness to:

- **Invite high-believability voices first** on domain-specific questions: "This is a capital allocation question. [Name], you've done this 20 times. What's your read?"
- **Probe low-believability strong opinions:** "You feel strongly about this. Help me understand — what experience are you drawing on?"
- **Weight the debrief accordingly:** When summarizing the discussion, give more emphasis to high-believability inputs.

### During decisions

- If the highest-believability advisor(s) disagree with the majority, the Chair explicitly pauses: "I want to note that [name], who has the most direct experience here, disagrees. Let's make sure we've engaged with their reasoning before deciding."
- If the decision goes against the highest-believability advisor(s), the rationale must be particularly strong and well-documented.

### For the advisors themselves

- **High-believability advisors** should speak up decisively on their domains but with humility on others: "This is my lane. Here's what I think and why."
- **Low-believability advisors** can still contribute — often by asking the right questions: "I don't have deep experience here, but I notice [pattern/concern]. Is that relevant?"
- **All advisors** should self-assess before weighing in: "How much do I actually know about this specific question?"

## Updating Believability

### Annual review

During the board effectiveness review (workflow 10), include a light-touch believability assessment:
- Which topics did each advisor contribute most effectively on?
- Were there domains where an advisor's input was consistently valuable or consistently off-base?
- Has anyone developed new expertise that changes their believability profile?

### After major decisions

When the decision quality review (workflow 12) evaluates past decisions:
- Whose advice was most predictive of the outcome?
- Whose concerns (including dissent) proved prescient?
- Update the informal believability map accordingly.

## Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Halo effect | High believability on one topic assumed for all topics | Believability is always topic-specific; Chair resets for each discussion |
| Credentialism | Confusing credentials (degrees, titles) with believability (demonstrated results) | Focus on track record, not resume |
| Silence from low-believability voices | People with lower domain expertise stop participating | Explicitly value their questions and outsider perspective |
| Static believability | Not updating when someone's expertise becomes outdated | Annual review forces re-assessment |
| Weaponized believability | "I'm more experienced, so I'm right" | High believability means more weight, not automatic override. Reasoning still matters. |
| Ignoring fresh perspectives | Only listening to "experts" and missing disruptive insights | Low-believability voices often ask the most valuable questions precisely because they are not trapped by domain assumptions |

## The Balancing Act

Believability weighting is a calibration tool, not a silencing tool. The goal is not to create a hierarchy of voices — it is to help the board make better decisions by being honest about where its expertise is concentrated.

The board's greatest risk is not that a low-believability advisor speaks too much. It is that a high-believability advisor stays silent because the room has already converged.
