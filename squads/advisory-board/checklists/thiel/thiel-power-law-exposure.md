# Thiel Power Law Exposure Checklist

## Purpose
Ensures the organization understands and positions itself for power law dynamics: the reality that a small number of bets, relationships, or assets will generate the vast majority of value. Thiel observes that the power law governs venture returns, product portfolios, and market outcomes. This checklist prevents the failure of spreading resources equally across opportunities when concentration on the best opportunities yields dramatically better results.

## When to Use
- During portfolio strategy and resource allocation.
- When evaluating which initiatives to double down on versus which to defund.
- When designing investment strategy for new ventures or products.
- During annual reviews of where value is actually generated.
- When the organization is spreading too thin across too many opportunities.

## Gate Criteria

### Power Law Awareness
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The organization's value creation is analyzed for power law distribution (are a few things generating most of the value).
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Revenue concentration is known: what percentage of revenue comes from the top 10% of customers or products.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Talent concentration is understood: which individuals or teams generate disproportionate value.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The organization acknowledges that equal allocation across opportunities is likely suboptimal.

### Concentration Strategy
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The organization identifies its highest-potential opportunities and allocates disproportionate resources.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Kill criteria exist for underperforming initiatives so resources can be reallocated to winners.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The highest-potential initiatives have the best talent assigned, not just adequate talent.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The temptation to diversify prematurely (before winning in the core) is resisted.

### Upside Optionality
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The organization maintains exposure to high-upside, non-linear outcomes.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Small bets on potentially transformative opportunities are included in the portfolio.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The organization does not optimize solely for average returns at the expense of outlier potential.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Follow-on investment capacity exists to double down on breakout successes.

### Risk of Misapplication
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The organization does not confuse power law thinking with reckless concentration.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Downside scenarios for concentrated positions are modeled.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The difference between power law bets (long-term, asymmetric payoff) and gambling is understood.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Non-power-law activities (operations, compliance, people management) are still resourced adequately.

## Red Flags
- Resources are allocated equally across all initiatives regardless of potential.
- The organization has twenty initiatives and none are clearly the most important.
- Underperforming products or initiatives are kept alive indefinitely because no one wants to kill them.
- The highest-potential opportunity has the same resources as the lowest-potential one.
- The organization diversifies into many areas before dominating one.
- Portfolio reviews focus on average performance rather than distribution of outcomes.

## Escalation Triggers
- A competitor concentrates resources on a single opportunity and wins the market the organization was spreading across.
- The organization's best opportunities are under-resourced because resources are locked into underperformers.
- Talent allocation does not match opportunity quality, with top performers on second-tier projects.
- The board approves a portfolio strategy with no mechanism for reallocating to winners.
- An initiative that should be killed continues to consume resources due to political rather than strategic reasons.
