# Never Split the Difference — Chris Voss

## Summary

Former FBI hostage negotiator Voss argues that negotiation is not a rational exercise—it's an emotional one. Traditional principled negotiation (Getting to Yes) assumes rational actors. Voss provides tactical tools for the real world: calibrated questions, tactical empathy, mirroring, labeling emotions, and strategic use of "no." The approach is particularly powerful in high-stakes, emotionally charged situations—exactly the kind of negotiations boards face with investors, acquirers, co-founders, and executives in crisis.

## Key Principles

- **Tactical empathy** — Understand the other side's feelings and mindset, and demonstrate that understanding. This is not agreement—it's acknowledgment that disarms defensiveness.
- **Labeling** — Name the other party's emotions out loud ("It seems like you're frustrated with..."). Labeling diminishes negative emotions and reinforces positive ones.
- **Mirroring** — Repeat the last 1-3 words someone said. This creates rapport and encourages them to elaborate, revealing more information.
- **"No" is the start, not the end** — Getting the other side to say "no" gives them a feeling of safety and control. "That's right" is the breakthrough moment, not "yes."
- **Calibrated questions** — Open-ended questions beginning with "how" or "what" that give the other side the illusion of control while directing the conversation. "How am I supposed to do that?" is more powerful than "I can't do that."
- **The Black Swan** — Unknown unknowns that can completely change the negotiation when discovered. The best negotiators are relentless about uncovering Black Swans.

## Frameworks Extracted

1. **The Accusation Audit** — Before a difficult conversation, list every negative thing the other party might think about you or the situation. Address them proactively. This preempts defensiveness.
2. **The Calibrated Question Set** — Key questions: "How am I supposed to do that?", "What about this is important to you?", "How would you like me to proceed?", "What is it that brought us to this situation?" These give the other side control while advancing your agenda.
3. **The Ackerman Model** — For price negotiation: Set target price, make first offer at 65%, increase to 85%, 95%, then 100% (non-round number). Each increase is paired with empathy and calibrated questions.
4. **The "That's Right" Technique** — Summarize the other party's position so accurately that they respond with "That's right." This signals genuine understanding and creates willingness to move forward.

## Application to Board Decisions

- **M&A negotiations** — Use the Accusation Audit before any acquisition negotiation. Proactively addressing the seller's concerns about the acquirer builds trust and accelerates deals.
- **Difficult conversations** — When the board must deliver hard messages (CEO underperformance, strategic disagreement), use tactical empathy and labeling to maintain the relationship while addressing the substance.
- **Investor negotiations** — Apply calibrated questions in fundraising discussions. "How would this structure work for the existing investors?" is more effective than "we can't accept those terms."
- **Crisis communication** — In reputational or operational crises, use labeling and tactical empathy with all stakeholders. Acknowledging emotions before presenting solutions dramatically improves reception.
- **Executive termination** — Use the "That's Right" technique to ensure the departing executive feels genuinely understood before discussing terms. This prevents litigation and preserves reputation.
