# Principal-Agent Framework

## Purpose

This framework provides a systematic method for identifying, analyzing, and resolving principal-agent problems — situations where one party (the agent) acts on behalf of another (the principal) but has different information, incentives, or risk preferences. Principal-agent misalignment is the root cause of most organizational dysfunction, governance failures, and trust breakdowns. This framework helps leaders design relationships, contracts, and governance structures that minimize agency costs and align agent behavior with principal interests.

## When to Use

- When designing executive compensation and performance metrics
- When structuring board governance and advisory relationships
- When negotiating with vendors, partners, or service providers
- When delegating significant authority or resources
- When diagnosing why a team, division, or partner is not producing expected results
- When designing marketplace or platform incentives
- When evaluating fund managers, financial advisors, or other fiduciaries

## Core Model

### The Principal-Agent Problem
A principal-agent problem exists whenever:
1. The principal delegates authority to an agent
2. The agent has information the principal lacks (information asymmetry)
3. The agent has different incentives or risk preferences than the principal
4. The principal cannot perfectly monitor the agent's behavior

### Three Types of Agency Problems

**1. Moral Hazard (Hidden Action)**
The agent takes actions the principal cannot observe. Example: An employee who works less when not supervised. A fund manager who takes excessive risk because the downside is borne by investors.

**2. Adverse Selection (Hidden Information)**
The agent has information before the relationship begins that the principal lacks. Example: A job candidate who overstates their abilities. A company that hides problems during due diligence.

**3. Hold-Up (Hidden Intention)**
After the relationship begins and investments are made, one party exploits the other's dependency. Example: A vendor raises prices after the client has become dependent. A co-founder demands more equity after becoming indispensable.

### Agency Cost Components
- **Monitoring costs**: What the principal spends to observe agent behavior (audits, reviews, reporting)
- **Bonding costs**: What the agent spends to demonstrate alignment (certifications, escrow, guarantees)
- **Residual loss**: The remaining value destruction from imperfect alignment despite monitoring and bonding

### Alignment Mechanisms

| Mechanism | How It Works | Best For |
|-----------|-------------|----------|
| **Equity/ownership** | Agent becomes co-owner | Long-term alignment |
| **Performance pay** | Compensation tied to outcomes | Measurable outputs |
| **Reputation** | Agent's future depends on current behavior | Repeat relationships |
| **Monitoring** | Direct observation of agent behavior | High-stakes, low-trust |
| **Transparency** | Full information sharing | Building trust |
| **Competition** | Multiple agents compete | Vendor selection |
| **Culture** | Shared values reduce divergence | Internal teams |
| **Contracts** | Legal obligations and penalties | External relationships |

## Steps

1. **Identify the principal-agent relationships.** Map every significant delegation relationship in your organization or deal. Founder→employee, investor→founder, board→CEO, company→vendor.

2. **For each relationship, assess the three asymmetries:**
   - Information: What does the agent know that the principal doesn't?
   - Incentives: Where do the agent's interests diverge from the principal's?
   - Risk: Does the agent bear the same downside as the principal?

3. **Classify the agency problem type.** Is it moral hazard, adverse selection, hold-up, or a combination?

4. **Estimate the agency cost.** How much value is being destroyed by misalignment? This can be measured as: excess spending, underperformance vs. potential, value-destructive decisions, or wasted monitoring effort.

5. **Select alignment mechanisms.** Choose from the menu above. Multiple mechanisms are usually needed. The goal is to make the agent's self-interest coincide with the principal's interest.

6. **Design the monitoring system.** What will you observe, how often, and what triggers intervention? Monitoring should be proportional to the stakes and the trust level.

7. **Implement with transparency.** Explain the alignment mechanisms to the agent. Hidden monitoring destroys trust. Transparent monitoring builds it.

8. **Review and adjust.** Agency problems evolve. Incentives that were aligned at one stage may diverge as circumstances change. Review annually.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- The board-CEO relationship is a classic principal-agent problem. The board (principal) delegates operational authority to the CEO (agent). Design governance to align this relationship.
- Advisor compensation should create alignment. Cash-only compensation creates an advisor who is incentivized to keep the engagement going. Equity compensation creates an advisor incentivized to increase company value.
- When a company's performance diverges from expectations, check for agency problems before assuming strategic failure. The strategy may be fine; the agents may be misaligned.

### For Founder-Investor Relationships
- Investors (principals) and founders (agents) have overlapping but non-identical interests. Investors want returns; founders want returns AND autonomy, mission, lifestyle. Structure the relationship to align on the overlap.
- Vesting schedules are an alignment mechanism: they ensure founders are committed long-term before receiving their full equity stake.
- Board seats are a monitoring mechanism: they give investors visibility into company operations.

### For Hiring and Management
- Every hire creates a principal-agent relationship. Design the role, compensation, and management structure to minimize agency costs.
- Remote work increases information asymmetry. Compensate with clearer output metrics, more frequent check-ins, and transparent communication tools.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Trusting without verifying.** "We hire good people and trust them" is not an alignment strategy. Good people in bad incentive structures still produce bad outcomes.

2. **Over-monitoring.** Excessive monitoring is expensive, demoralizing, and signals distrust. Find the minimum effective monitoring level.

3. **Ignoring the principal's agency problems.** The principal is often also an agent for someone else. A CEO is the board's agent. The board is the shareholders' agent. Map the full chain.

4. **One-dimensional alignment.** Using only financial incentives to solve a multi-dimensional problem. Social, cultural, and intrinsic alignment mechanisms are often more powerful and cheaper.

5. **Static alignment.** Incentives that aligned at seed stage may diverge at growth stage. Revisit alignment as the company evolves.

6. **Confusing alignment with control.** The goal is not to control agents but to create conditions where agents acting in their own interest also serve the principal's interest.
