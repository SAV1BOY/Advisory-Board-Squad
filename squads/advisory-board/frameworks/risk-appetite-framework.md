# Risk Appetite Framework

## Purpose

Risk appetite defines the amount and type of risk an organization is willing to accept in pursuit of its strategic objectives. Without a clearly articulated risk appetite, boards oscillate between excessive caution (missing opportunities) and reckless aggression (courting existential threats). This framework provides a structured method for defining, communicating, and monitoring organizational risk appetite so that management and the board share a common language for risk-taking decisions.

## When to Use

- During annual strategic planning to calibrate risk thresholds for the year ahead
- Before entering new markets, launching new products, or making significant capital commitments
- When evaluating M&A opportunities or strategic partnerships
- After a risk event to reassess whether appetite levels remain appropriate
- During fundraising to communicate risk posture to investors
- When onboarding new board members to align on risk philosophy

## Core Model

### Risk Appetite Hierarchy

1. **Risk Capacity** — The maximum risk the organization can absorb before threatening solvency or viability. This is an objective ceiling determined by balance sheet strength, liquidity, and contractual obligations.
2. **Risk Appetite** — The aggregate amount and type of risk the board is willing to accept. This is a board-level strategic choice that sits below capacity.
3. **Risk Tolerance** — The acceptable variation around specific objectives. These are operational boundaries set by management within the board's appetite.
4. **Risk Limits** — Specific quantitative thresholds for individual risk categories, business units, or transactions.

### Risk Appetite Statement Structure

A well-constructed risk appetite statement has five components:

| Component | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Risk Category | The domain of risk | Financial — Revenue concentration |
| Appetite Level | Averse / Cautious / Open / Hungry | Open |
| Quantitative Threshold | Measurable boundary | No single customer >15% of revenue |
| Rationale | Why this level is appropriate | Diversification protects against churn shock |
| Escalation Trigger | When to alert the board | Any customer approaches 12% of revenue |

### Appetite Levels Defined

| Level | Description | Appropriate When |
|---|---|---|
| **Averse** | Avoid this risk entirely; zero tolerance for exposure | Existential risks, legal/ethical violations, safety |
| **Cautious** | Accept minimal risk only where necessary; prefer proven approaches | Core operations, data security, regulatory compliance |
| **Open** | Accept moderate risk where balanced with reward; willing to experiment with controls | Growth initiatives, market expansion, product innovation |
| **Hungry** | Actively seek risk for outsized returns; accept significant volatility | Venture-stage bets, disruptive innovation, land-grab markets |

## Steps to Define Risk Appetite

1. **Map strategic objectives.** Risk appetite derives from strategy. Begin by listing the top 3-5 strategic priorities for the planning period.

2. **Identify risk categories.** For each objective, identify the relevant risk categories: financial, operational, strategic, people, technology, legal/compliance, reputational.

3. **Assess current risk profile.** Document where the organization currently sits on the appetite spectrum for each category using historical data and incident records.

4. **Set target appetite levels.** For each category, the board decides the appropriate appetite level based on strategic context, competitive dynamics, and organizational maturity.

5. **Define quantitative thresholds.** Translate appetite levels into measurable limits. Examples: maximum acceptable cash burn rate, minimum runway months, maximum customer concentration, acceptable defect rate.

6. **Establish escalation triggers.** For each threshold, define the point at which management must inform the board. Triggers should fire before limits are breached, not after.

7. **Document and communicate.** Publish the risk appetite statement to all relevant stakeholders. Ensure management understands the boundaries within which they operate.

8. **Monitor and report.** Include risk appetite compliance in quarterly board reporting. Track actual exposure against defined thresholds.

9. **Review and recalibrate annually.** Risk appetite should evolve with strategy. A Series A company and a Series C company have different appetites.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Setting appetite too broadly.** "We have moderate risk appetite" is meaningless. Appetite must be specific to each risk category.
2. **Confusing appetite with tolerance.** Appetite is a strategic choice; tolerance is an operational parameter. The board sets appetite; management sets tolerance within appetite.
3. **Static appetite in a dynamic environment.** Risk appetite should be reviewed when strategy changes, market conditions shift, or after significant risk events.
4. **All categories at the same level.** A startup should be hungry on product risk but cautious on compliance risk. Uniform appetite signals lack of strategic thinking.
5. **No quantitative thresholds.** Qualitative labels without numbers are unenforceable. Every appetite level needs at least one measurable boundary.

## Related Frameworks

- **Pre-Mortem Framework** — Use pre-mortems to test whether appetite levels are realistic
- **Scenario Planning** — Stress-test appetite thresholds against multiple future scenarios
- **Expected Value and Asymmetry** — Calibrate appetite based on asymmetric payoff profiles
- **Reversible-Irreversible Decisions** — Higher appetite is appropriate for reversible risks
- **Second-Order Thinking** — Trace downstream effects of risk appetite choices
