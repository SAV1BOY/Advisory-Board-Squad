# Case: Boeing 737 MAX Crisis

## Overview

Two crashes of Boeing 737 MAX aircraft — Lion Air Flight 610 (October 2018) and Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 (March 2019) — killed 346 people and triggered the longest grounding of a commercial aircraft in aviation history. The crisis exposed systemic failures in engineering culture, regulatory capture, and corporate governance that accumulated over two decades.

## What Happened

### The MCAS System
Boeing introduced the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS) to compensate for aerodynamic changes caused by larger engines mounted further forward on the 737 MAX. MCAS automatically pushed the nose down based on a single angle-of-attack sensor reading.

### Critical Design Failures
- MCAS relied on a single sensor with no redundancy — a design choice that would have been unacceptable in most safety-critical systems
- The system could activate repeatedly, overpowering pilot inputs
- Pilots were not informed about MCAS in their training materials
- Boeing classified MCAS as a minor system change to avoid triggering new training requirements and additional regulatory scrutiny

### The Crashes
In both crashes, a faulty angle-of-attack sensor fed erroneous data to MCAS, which repeatedly forced the aircraft's nose down. Pilots, unaware of MCAS and untrained in its failure modes, were unable to regain control.

## Root Causes

### 1. Financial Pressure Over Engineering Culture
Boeing's 1997 merger with McDonnell Douglas shifted the company's center of gravity from engineering excellence to financial performance. Headquarters moved from Seattle (near engineering) to Chicago (near finance).

- Cost reduction became the dominant metric
- Engineering concerns were subordinated to schedule and budget pressures
- The mantra shifted from "build the best airplane" to "deliver shareholder returns"
- Internal engineers who raised MCAS safety concerns were overruled or sidelined

### 2. Regulatory Capture
Boeing's relationship with the FAA had evolved from oversight to partnership through the Organization Designation Authorization (ODA) program.

- Boeing employees served as FAA-authorized inspectors of their own work
- The FAA relied on Boeing's internal assessments rather than conducting independent analysis
- Budget constraints at the FAA reduced the agency's capacity for independent oversight
- Boeing actively lobbied to expand self-certification authority

### 3. Competitive Urgency
Airbus's A320neo family was winning orders with fuel-efficient new engines. Rather than design a new aircraft (a 10+ year, $15B+ program), Boeing chose to re-engine the existing 737 — a decision driven by speed to market and cost avoidance.

- The re-engining approach created the aerodynamic problems MCAS was designed to solve
- Boeing marketed the 737 MAX as requiring no new pilot training (a key selling point vs. a new aircraft type)
- This marketing commitment constrained engineering options and prevented proper disclosure of MCAS

### 4. Board Oversight Failure
Boeing's board failed to exercise adequate oversight of safety culture and engineering decisions.

- The board lacked members with deep aerospace engineering expertise
- Safety metrics were not a standing board agenda item
- The board relied on management representations about safety without independent verification
- Post-crisis investigations revealed the board was unaware of MCAS-related concerns raised internally

## Consequences

- 346 lives lost
- 20-month global grounding of the 737 MAX fleet
- Over $20B in direct costs (compensation, penalties, lost orders, production disruption)
- Criminal charges against Boeing (deferred prosecution agreement)
- CEO Dennis Muilenburg fired in December 2019
- Permanent damage to Boeing's reputation as the gold standard in aviation safety
- Congressional investigations revealing systemic cultural and governance failures

## Lessons for Advisors

### On Engineering Culture
- When financial metrics override engineering judgment in safety-critical domains, catastrophic failure is not a possibility — it is an inevitability
- "Move fast" culture is incompatible with industries where failure kills people
- Internal dissent must have a protected channel to reach the board, bypassing management

### On Regulatory Relationships
- Self-regulation is not regulation. Companies must maintain genuine independence between their engineering teams and the regulatory process
- Regulatory capture creates the illusion of oversight while eliminating its substance
- Advisors should question any arrangement where the regulated entity is also the regulator

### On Governance
- Boards of safety-critical companies must include members with deep domain expertise
- Safety must be a standing board-level metric, not a management-reported assurance
- When a company's competitive strategy depends on minimizing safety disclosure, the strategy itself is the risk

### On Corporate Priorities
- The Boeing case is a cautionary tale about the long-term cost of prioritizing short-term financial performance
- Companies that sacrifice their core competency (in Boeing's case, engineering excellence) for financial optimization eventually lose both

## The Advisory Question

> "What is the equivalent of MCAS in your organization — a known risk that has been rationalized, minimized, or hidden because addressing it would slow growth or increase costs?"
