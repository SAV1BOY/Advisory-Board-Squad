# Delegation Pattern

## Pattern Name

Delegation — structured transfer of decision authority with appropriate guardrails, accountability, and support.

## When to Use

- A leader is bottlenecking decisions that others have the context to make.
- A team member needs to grow into greater responsibility.
- The decision type recurs frequently and does not require senior judgment each time.
- A leader wants to preserve their attention for higher-leverage activities.
- Organizational scaling demands that authority be distributed, not concentrated.

Do NOT use when:
- The decision is existential or irreversible and requires the most experienced judgment available.
- The delegate lacks the foundational competence to make the decision (delegation is not abdication).
- The delegate does not want the authority and has not been developed to accept it.
- Regulatory or legal requirements mandate a specific decision-maker.

## Structure

```
1. DEFINE    → Specify what is being delegated and its boundaries
2. SELECT    → Choose the right delegate for the right reasons
3. EQUIP     → Provide context, constraints, and resources
4. AGREE     → Establish mutual expectations and reporting
5. RELEASE   → Step back and let the delegate own it
6. REVIEW    → Evaluate outcomes and calibrate for next time
```

## Steps

### Step 1: Define the Delegation Scope
- State clearly what is being delegated:
  - **Decision authority:** "You can approve expenses up to $10K without my sign-off."
  - **Outcome ownership:** "You own the Q2 product launch — all decisions within scope are yours."
  - **Process ownership:** "You run the weekly team meeting including agenda setting and follow-up."
- Define the boundaries explicitly:
  - **In scope:** What the delegate controls.
  - **Out of scope:** What remains with the delegator.
  - **Escalation triggers:** Specific conditions that should come back to the delegator (dollar thresholds, risk levels, stakeholder conflicts, policy exceptions).
- Use the delegation authority levels:
  - **Level 1 — Investigate:** Research and report options. Decision stays with delegator.
  - **Level 2 — Recommend:** Recommend a course of action. Decision stays with delegator.
  - **Level 3 — Decide and inform:** Make the decision and report it. Delegator can override within a window.
  - **Level 4 — Decide and act:** Make the decision and execute. Report on outcomes at agreed cadence.
  - **Level 5 — Full ownership:** Own it entirely. Delegator is informed only at review intervals.

### Step 2: Select the Right Delegate
- Selection criteria:
  - **Competence:** Does this person have the skills to succeed? (If not, this is development, not delegation — provide training first.)
  - **Context:** Does this person have or can they quickly acquire the necessary context?
  - **Capacity:** Does this person have the bandwidth to take this on without sacrificing other commitments?
  - **Motivation:** Does this person want this responsibility?
- Match the delegation level to the delegate's readiness:
  - New to the domain: Start at Level 1-2
  - Competent but new to authority: Level 3
  - Proven and trusted: Level 4-5

### Step 3: Equip the Delegate
- Provide:
  - **Strategic context:** Why this work matters and how it connects to broader goals.
  - **Decision history:** Relevant past decisions and their rationale.
  - **Stakeholder map:** Who cares about this and what they expect.
  - **Constraints:** Budget limits, timeline requirements, policy boundaries.
  - **Resources:** Tools, budget, team support, and access to expertise.
  - **Support commitment:** "You can come to me for counsel at any time — asking for advice is not failure."
- Do NOT provide:
  - A script of exactly what to do (that is tasking, not delegating).
  - Your preferred answer (that biases the delegate and defeats the purpose).

### Step 4: Agree on Expectations
- Document mutual expectations:
  - **Success criteria:** What a good outcome looks like.
  - **Reporting cadence:** How often the delegate updates the delegator (weekly check-in, monthly report, etc.).
  - **Reporting format:** What information is shared (key decisions made, risks encountered, help needed).
  - **Override protocol:** Under what circumstances the delegator will intervene and how.
  - **Timeline:** Duration of the delegation (permanent, project-scoped, or trial period).
- Both parties confirm understanding. This is a two-way commitment.

### Step 5: Release and Resist Interference
- The delegator's job after delegation:
  - Be available when asked for counsel.
  - Provide air cover with stakeholders ("This is Maria's decision to make").
  - Resist the urge to step in when the delegate makes choices differently than you would.
  - Only intervene when escalation triggers from Step 1 are hit.
- Common temptation to resist: "Let me just handle this one thing" — this undermines the entire delegation.
- If the delegate makes a mistake within the delegated scope, support them through the correction rather than reclaiming authority.

### Step 6: Review and Calibrate
- At the agreed review interval:
  - Evaluate outcomes against success criteria.
  - Discuss what went well and what was difficult.
  - Assess whether the delegation level should be adjusted (up or down).
  - Capture lessons for the delegate's development and for organizational delegation practices.
- If delegation succeeded: consider expanding scope or increasing authority level.
- If delegation struggled: diagnose whether the issue was scope, support, competence, or capacity — and address the root cause rather than simply reclaiming authority.

## Anti-Patterns

| Anti-Pattern | Description | Consequence |
|-------------|-------------|-------------|
| Delegation without authority | "You own this but check with me on everything" | Delegate has responsibility without power; frustration and bottleneck persist |
| Abdication disguised as delegation | Dumping work without context, support, or follow-up | Delegate fails; delegator blames delegate |
| Yo-yo delegation | Delegating, then reclaiming at the first sign of difficulty | Delegate never develops; trust erodes |
| Delegation to the group | "The team owns this" without a single accountable person | No one owns it; nothing happens |
| Upward delegation | Delegate repeatedly brings decisions back to the delegator for approval | Authority never actually transfers; bottleneck persists |
| Delegation mismatch | High-stakes delegation to an unprepared delegate | Predictable failure; delegate's confidence damaged |

## Examples

### Example 1: Budget Authority Delegation

**Define:** VP Marketing delegates advertising spend decisions up to $15K per campaign to Marketing Manager. Above $15K requires VP approval. Escalate if: CPL exceeds 2x target for more than 1 week, or any compliance-sensitive content.

**Equip:** Share quarterly budget, historical CPL data, brand guidelines, list of pre-approved channels, and compliance checklist.

**Agree:** Weekly 15-minute check-in. Monthly spend report. Override only if escalation triggers are hit.

**Review:** After one quarter — Marketing Manager made 23 spend decisions, all within guidelines. CPL improved 8%. Delegation level increased: threshold raised to $25K.

### Example 2: Hiring Authority Delegation

**Define:** CEO delegates hiring decisions for individual contributor roles to VP Engineering (Level 4 — decide and inform). VP-level hires and above remain with CEO (Level 2 — recommend).

**Equip:** Headcount plan, compensation bands, scorecard templates, diversity targets, and offer approval workflow.

**Agree:** VP Eng reports new hires weekly. Monthly hiring pipeline review with CEO. Escalation triggers: any offer above band, any role not in headcount plan, any candidate with competitor non-compete concerns.

**Review:** After 6 months — 14 IC hires made, 12 meeting 90-day expectations (86% success rate). Two regrettable hires analyzed in postmortem. Delegation maintained with improved reference check process.
