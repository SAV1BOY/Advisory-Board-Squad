# Contrarian Decision Memos

## Overview
The most valuable decisions are often contrarian—they go against the prevailing consensus. These examples illustrate the pattern of contrarian reasoning and provide templates for boards facing decisions where the right answer is unpopular.

## Patagonia's "Don't Buy This Jacket" Campaign (2011)

### The Contrarian Logic
- **Consensus view:** Companies should encourage customers to buy more.
- **Contrarian position:** A company built on environmental values should discourage unnecessary consumption, even of its own products.
- **The memo reasoning:** (1) Our mission is environmental responsibility, (2) Overconsumption is the core problem, (3) Asking customers not to buy unless they need it builds trust and brand loyalty, (4) The customers who respond to this message are our most valuable long-term customers.
- **Outcome:** The ad went viral. Sales increased. Brand loyalty deepened. Patagonia proved that authenticity is the ultimate marketing strategy.

### Swipeable Principle
When your values and conventional business logic conflict, choosing values can be the best business decision—but you must have the courage to absorb the short-term risk.

## Berkshire's Decision to Hold Cash During the Dot-Com Boom (1999-2000)

### The Contrarian Logic
- **Consensus view:** Technology is the future. Not investing means falling behind.
- **Contrarian position:** We don't understand tech valuations. The prices are irrational. We'll sit on cash until opportunities emerge in our circle of competence.
- **The memo reasoning:** (1) We don't understand these businesses well enough to invest, (2) Prices imply growth rates that are mathematically impossible for most companies, (3) Cash is not "doing nothing"—it's a position that preserves optionality, (4) We will be criticized but our job is long-term returns, not short-term reputation.
- **Outcome:** The dot-com crash validated the discipline. Berkshire deployed cash at distressed prices during and after the crash.

### Swipeable Principle
Doing nothing is a valid strategic decision when the alternatives are overpriced or poorly understood. The social pressure to act is not a strategic reason to act.

## Stripe's Decision to Ignore Enterprise Initially (2010-2015)

### The Contrarian Logic
- **Consensus view:** Payments is an enterprise sales business. Go after the biggest customers first.
- **Contrarian position:** Developers are the real decision-makers for payments integration. Serve them first.
- **The memo reasoning:** (1) The existing payments infrastructure is developer-hostile, (2) Developers have growing influence over technology purchasing decisions, (3) A developer-first product will organically move upmarket as startup customers grow, (4) Enterprise sales is expensive and slow; developer adoption is cheap and fast.
- **Outcome:** Stripe became the dominant payments infrastructure company by growing with its customers from startup to enterprise.

### Swipeable Principle
The conventional target market is sometimes wrong. Identifying the true decision-maker or the underserved segment can create a more defensible growth path than competing directly for established customers.

## Template for Contrarian Decision Memos

1. **What does the consensus believe?** State it clearly and fairly.
2. **Why is the consensus wrong (or incomplete)?** Specific reasoning with evidence.
3. **What is our contrarian thesis?** Clear, testable, and specific.
4. **What are the risks of being wrong?** Honestly assessed.
5. **What evidence would change our mind?** Define falsification criteria upfront.
6. **What is the time horizon?** Contrarian bets often take longer to pay off.
7. **How will we manage the social cost?** Being contrarian invites criticism. Plan for it.
