# Marketplace Industry Benchmarks

## Key Findings

1. **Marketplace liquidity is the foundational metric.** A marketplace is healthy when supply utilization exceeds 30% and buyer-side search-to-transaction conversion exceeds 5%. Below these thresholds, the marketplace has a liquidity problem that no amount of marketing spend can solve — it requires structural product changes.
2. **Take rates are converging by category.** Services marketplaces command 15-25% take rates, product marketplaces 8-15%, and B2B marketplaces 3-8%. Take rates above category norms signal either a monopoly position or an unsustainable margin that invites competition.
3. **The chicken-and-egg problem is overstated.** Successful marketplaces almost always start by constraining supply (curated, high-quality supply in a single geography or vertical) rather than by attempting to build both sides simultaneously. The board should challenge any growth strategy that tries to scale both sides at once.
4. **Disintermediation risk is highest between Year 2 and Year 5.** Once buyers and sellers have established relationships through the platform, they have incentive to transact off-platform. Retention requires continuous value creation beyond the initial match — payments, insurance, dispute resolution, data analytics.
5. **GMV growth without unit economics improvement is a warning sign.** Marketplaces that subsidize transactions to grow GMV often discover that removing subsidies causes volume to collapse. The board should track organic (unsubsidized) transaction growth separately.

## Implications for Board

- **Liquidity metrics over vanity metrics.** The board should prioritize supply utilization, match rate, and repeat transaction rate over GMV and registered user counts. A marketplace with $10M GMV and 45% repeat rate is healthier than one with $50M GMV and 10% repeat rate.
- **Take rate trajectory.** Monitor take rate trends quarterly. A declining take rate without strategic justification (e.g., entering a new category) signals competitive pressure or disintermediation.
- **Geographic and category density.** Marketplaces win through density, not breadth. The board should challenge premature geographic or category expansion before core market liquidity thresholds are met.
- **Subsidy accounting.** Require management to report subsidized vs. organic transaction volume separately. The board should set a sunset date for acquisition subsidies and monitor organic retention.
- **Disintermediation defense.** The board should review the company's disintermediation defense strategy annually — what value does the platform provide beyond the initial match that keeps transactions on-platform?
