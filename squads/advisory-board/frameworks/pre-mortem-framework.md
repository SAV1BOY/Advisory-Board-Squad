# Pre-Mortem Framework

## Purpose

The Pre-Mortem Framework, developed by psychologist Gary Klein, is a prospective hindsight technique that asks teams to imagine that a project has already failed and then work backward to identify the causes. Unlike risk assessment (which asks "What could go wrong?"), the pre-mortem asks "Imagine it DID go wrong — why?" This subtle reframing unlocks dramatically more honest and creative identification of risks because it gives people permission to voice concerns they would otherwise suppress.

## When to Use

- Before launching any major initiative, product, or campaign
- Before making irreversible (Type 1) decisions
- When a team is exhibiting excessive optimism or groupthink
- Before fundraising rounds to anticipate investor objections
- Before major hires, partnerships, or acquisitions
- When stakes are high and failure would be costly
- As a standard step in any project kickoff

## Core Model

### The Psychological Mechanism
In a standard risk assessment, people are asked to criticize a plan they have already committed to. This triggers cognitive dissonance — the same plan they invested in is now the thing they must attack. The result: people identify surface-level risks and suppress deep concerns.

The pre-mortem reverses this. By stating "the project has failed" as a given, it removes the social cost of dissent. People are not criticizing — they are explaining. This distinction unlocks:
- Concerns that junior team members were afraid to voice
- Risks that the leader's enthusiasm had suppressed
- Failure modes that require domain-specific knowledge only certain team members have
- "Uncomfortable truths" that everyone senses but nobody says

### The Pre-Mortem Protocol

**Setup:** "It is [date 12 months from now]. The project has failed spectacularly. Not a minor miss — a complete failure. Take 5 minutes of silence and write down every reason it could have failed."

**Key rules:**
- The failure is stipulated — no arguing that "it won't fail"
- Individual generation first, group discussion second
- Every failure reason is valid — no filtering during brainstorming
- The more specific and vivid the failure reason, the more useful it is

## Steps

1. **Set the stage.** Assemble the project team. The facilitator reads the setup: "The project has failed. It's a disaster. Write down why."

2. **Silent individual generation (5-10 minutes).** Each person writes their own list of failure reasons. No talking. No sharing. This prevents groupthink and ensures diverse perspectives.

3. **Round-robin sharing.** Go around the room. Each person shares one failure reason per round. Continue until all unique reasons are surfaced. No discussion during sharing — just capture.

4. **Consolidate and cluster.** Group related failure reasons into themes. Common clusters: execution/resourcing, market/customer, technology, people/culture, external/macro, timing.

5. **Prioritize by plausibility and impact.** Vote on which failure modes are most likely AND most damaging. Focus on the top 5-7.

6. **For each priority failure mode, assign a mitigation owner.** The owner is responsible for designing a specific mitigation and presenting it within one week.

7. **Integrate mitigations into the project plan.** Pre-mortem outputs are not a separate document — they modify the project plan, budget, and timeline.

8. **Revisit at midpoint.** Run a mini pre-mortem at the project midpoint. New failure modes will have emerged as the project progressed.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Before any board vote on a major decision, run a 15-minute pre-mortem. "We approved this investment and it failed — why?" The quality of board risk assessment will improve dramatically.
- Use pre-mortems to surface concerns that board members are reluctant to voice in front of the founder. The framework makes dissent productive, not confrontational.
- When reviewing a founder's plan, ask them to present their own pre-mortem. A founder who has done one is more prepared and trustworthy than one who hasn't.

### For Founders
- Run pre-mortems before fundraising. The failure reasons your team identifies will overlap significantly with investor objections. Prepare for them proactively.
- Make pre-mortems part of your product launch process. Every launch plan should have a pre-mortem appendix.
- Use pre-mortems to give your team permission to disagree with you. The framework creates structured safety for dissent.

### For Project Managers
- Pre-mortems are the highest-ROI 30 minutes you can spend on risk management. They consistently identify 2-3x more risks than traditional risk assessment.
- Combine with the Inversion Framework for maximum coverage: inversion provides structural failure analysis; pre-mortem provides narrative, human-centered failure analysis.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Running it as a group brainstorm.** The silent individual generation phase is non-negotiable. Group brainstorms produce fewer, less diverse, and less honest failure reasons.

2. **The leader goes first.** If the CEO shares their failure reasons first, everyone else's reasons will cluster around the CEO's. Leaders should go last or not share at all.

3. **Being too abstract.** "We might fail because of competition" is too vague. "We fail because Competitor X releases a free version in Q2 and our sales cycle is too long to respond" is useful.

4. **Stopping at the list.** The pre-mortem list is the beginning, not the end. Every critical failure mode needs a specific, owned mitigation.

5. **Doing it once and filing it away.** The pre-mortem should be a living document that is revisited as the project evolves.

6. **Using it to kill projects.** The pre-mortem is not a veto mechanism. It is a strengthening mechanism. Every project has failure modes — the question is whether they can be mitigated.

7. **Skipping it because "we already know the risks."** The pre-mortem consistently surfaces risks that experienced teams did not identify through conventional risk assessment.
