# Reversible vs Irreversible Decision Quality Checklist

## Purpose
Ensure the advisory board correctly classifies decisions by their reversibility and applies proportional rigor. Irreversible decisions (one-way doors) demand exhaustive analysis and broad consensus. Reversible decisions (two-way doors) should be made quickly with bias toward action. Misclassifying either type wastes time or creates unnecessary risk.

## When to Use
- At the start of any decision process to determine the appropriate level of analysis.
- When a team is spending excessive time on a decision that appears easily reversible.
- When a team is rushing a decision that may have irreversible consequences.
- During decision post-mortems to assess whether classification was correct.

## Gate Criteria

### Classification Accuracy
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The decision has been explicitly classified as reversible, partially reversible, or irreversible with rationale documented.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The classification considers financial reversibility (can we recover spent capital?).
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The classification considers reputational reversibility (can we undo public perception damage?).
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The classification considers talent reversibility (will we lose key people who cannot be rehired?).
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The classification considers contractual reversibility (are there binding commitments with exit costs?).
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The classification considers competitive reversibility (will competitors exploit a reversal as weakness?).

### Irreversible Decision Gates
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: For irreversible decisions, at least three alternative options have been evaluated.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: For irreversible decisions, a pre-mortem has been conducted imagining the decision failed.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: For irreversible decisions, the decision-maker has slept on it for at least 48 hours after the analysis is complete.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: For irreversible decisions, at least one dissenting voice has been actively sought and heard.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: For irreversible decisions, legal and financial review has been completed.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: For irreversible decisions, the board or appropriate governance body has formally approved.

### Reversible Decision Gates
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: For reversible decisions, a decision has been made within the standard decision timeline (not overanalyzed).
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: For reversible decisions, rollback criteria and process are defined before execution.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: For reversible decisions, the cost of delay has been weighed against the cost of being wrong.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: For reversible decisions, a single decision-maker is empowered (not sent to committee).

### Partially Reversible Decisions
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: For partially reversible decisions, the irreversible components are identified and receive higher scrutiny.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: For partially reversible decisions, the reversible components are separated and can proceed independently if needed.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: For partially reversible decisions, the cost and timeline of reversal for each component is estimated.

### Process Calibration
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The level of analysis invested matches the reversibility classification (not overthinking two-way doors).
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The number of approvers matches the reversibility classification (fewer for reversible, more for irreversible).
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The timeline for decision matches the reversibility classification (faster for reversible, deliberate for irreversible).
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Historical decisions are reviewed to validate the organization's classification accuracy.

## Red Flags
- Every decision is treated as irreversible, leading to analysis paralysis and slow execution.
- Every decision is treated as reversible, leading to reckless commitments that cannot be unwound.
- The team classifies a decision as reversible because they want to move fast, not because it genuinely is.
- Sunk cost fallacy prevents reversal of a decision that should be unwound.
- The organization lacks a defined process for actually executing a reversal.
- Exit costs or contractual lock-ins make a theoretically reversible decision practically irreversible.
- No one has asked "what would it take to undo this?" before committing.

## Escalation Triggers
- A decision classified as reversible turns out to be irreversible, causing material harm.
- A pattern emerges of systematically misclassifying decisions in one direction.
- An irreversible decision is being rushed through without the required gates.
- The team cannot articulate the reversal process for a decision classified as reversible.
- Material exit costs are discovered after a commitment is made.
