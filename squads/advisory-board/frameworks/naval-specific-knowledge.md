# Naval Specific Knowledge Framework

## Purpose

Naval Ravikant defines "specific knowledge" as knowledge that cannot be trained for — it is found by pursuing your genuine curiosity and passion. It is the unique combination of skills, insights, and capabilities that makes you irreplaceable. Specific knowledge is what remains valuable when everything else can be automated, outsourced, or commoditized. This framework helps individuals and organizations identify, develop, and deploy specific knowledge as the foundation for durable competitive advantage.

## When to Use

- When a founder is trying to identify their unique competitive advantage
- When building a team and defining what each person uniquely contributes
- During career coaching to help someone find their highest-leverage role
- When evaluating whether a company's advantage is durable or commoditizable
- When deciding what to insource vs. outsource (insource specific knowledge)
- When a company's advantage seems to be eroding due to competition or automation

## Core Model

### Characteristics of Specific Knowledge
- **Cannot be trained for**: It is learned through apprenticeship, curiosity, and obsession, not courses
- **Feels like play to you, work to others**: If you find it effortless and others find it grueling, it may be your specific knowledge
- **Highly technical or creative**: Often at the intersection of multiple domains
- **Tied to your unique combination of experiences**: Your specific life path created it
- **Not easily described in a job posting**: If you can write a job description for it, it is not specific enough

### Where Specific Knowledge Lives
- At the intersection of two or more skills (coding + psychology = behavioral product design)
- In deep domain expertise developed over years of obsessive focus
- In pattern recognition built from unique experiences
- In relationships and trust networks that took years to build
- In taste and judgment that cannot be quantified or taught

### Specific Knowledge vs. General Knowledge
| Aspect | General Knowledge | Specific Knowledge |
|--------|------------------|-------------------|
| Source | School, courses, books | Experience, curiosity, obsession |
| Replicability | High (anyone can learn) | Low (unique to you) |
| Value trend | Declining (commoditizing) | Increasing (scarce) |
| Automation risk | High | Low |
| Leverage potential | Low | High |

## Steps

1. **Identify your specific knowledge.** Ask: What do people come to me for that they don't go to others for? What feels like play to me but work to others? What unique combination of skills do I have?

2. **Validate it.** Specific knowledge must be valuable to others, not just interesting to you. Test: Are people willing to pay for this knowledge? Does it solve important problems?

3. **Double down.** Once identified, invest disproportionately in deepening your specific knowledge. The more specific it becomes, the more valuable it is.

4. **Apply leverage to it.** Specific knowledge alone is valuable but limited. Specific knowledge + leverage (code, media, capital, labor) = wealth and impact.

5. **Build accountability around it.** Put your name and reputation behind your specific knowledge. Accountability is what converts knowledge into economic reward.

6. **Resist the urge to generalize.** The market rewards specialists who go deep, not generalists who go wide. Stay in your lane and deepen it.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Help founders articulate their specific knowledge and ensure the business strategy is built on top of it, not beside it.
- Evaluate founding teams by their collective specific knowledge. Does the team's combined knowledge create an unfair advantage?
- When a company is struggling, check whether they've drifted away from their specific knowledge into areas where they have no unique advantage.

### For Founders
- Your company's specific knowledge is its soul. Every strategic decision should strengthen it. Diversifying away from specific knowledge is dilution.
- Hire people whose specific knowledge complements yours. Two founders with the same specific knowledge are redundant; two with complementary specific knowledge are powerful.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing credentials with specific knowledge.** An MBA is general knowledge. The insight you developed from building three failed marketplaces is specific knowledge.
2. **Trying to develop specific knowledge through courses.** Specific knowledge comes from doing, failing, and reflecting — not from structured learning.
3. **Copying someone else's specific knowledge.** By definition, this produces general knowledge. Your specific knowledge must be uniquely yours.
4. **Not applying leverage.** Specific knowledge without leverage produces a comfortable living. Specific knowledge with leverage produces outsized returns.
