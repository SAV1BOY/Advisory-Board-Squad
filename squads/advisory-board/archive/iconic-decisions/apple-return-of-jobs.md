# Apple: The Return of Steve Jobs

## Context

By 1996, Apple was 90 days from bankruptcy. Market share had collapsed from 16% to 4%. The product line was bloated with dozens of overlapping products. Three CEOs in succession had failed to reverse the decline. The board was desperate and the company was losing $1B annually. Apple acquired NeXT Software for $427M, bringing Steve Jobs back to the company he had been fired from 11 years earlier.

## Decision/Event

Jobs made a series of brutal, high-conviction decisions within his first year:
- **Killed 70% of the product line.** Reduced dozens of products to a simple 2x2 matrix: Consumer/Pro x Desktop/Portable. Four products.
- **Killed the Newton.** Cancelled Apple's PDA despite significant sunk costs and a devoted team.
- **Accepted a $150M investment from Microsoft.** The enemy. In exchange for a commitment to develop Office for Mac and settle patent disputes. The crowd at Macworld booed.
- **Installed a new board.** Removed all but two existing directors and replaced them with allies.
- **Focused on design and user experience** as the core differentiator, hiring Jony Ive as head of design.

## What Happened

Apple went from near-bankruptcy to the world's most valuable company within 15 years. The iMac (1998), iPod (2001), iPhone (2007), and iPad (2010) each created or redefined a product category. Apple's market cap grew from approximately $2B in 1997 to over $3T.

The product line reduction was particularly instructive: by eliminating 70% of products, Apple improved engineering focus, reduced operational complexity, strengthened brand clarity, and freed capital for the few products that mattered.

## Lessons

1. **In turnarounds, subtraction is more powerful than addition.** The instinct is to add new products or markets. Jobs's genius was eliminating almost everything to focus on what Apple could do better than anyone.

2. **Conviction decisions require conviction leaders.** The Microsoft deal, the product kills, and the board overhaul were all deeply unpopular. They required a leader willing to be hated in the short term for the right long-term outcome.

3. **Board composition determines organizational trajectory.** Jobs's first move was reshaping the board. A board that tolerates decline will continue to get it. A board that demands excellence enables it.

4. **Sunk cost must be ignored, especially when survival is at stake.** The Newton had consumed years of investment. Killing it was the right call because its future expected value was negative regardless of past investment.

5. **Design and simplicity can be a moat.** Apple proved that user experience, when elevated to a strategic priority rather than a cosmetic concern, creates a brand moat that commands premium pricing for decades.

## Frameworks That Apply

- `lib/patterns/kill-decision-pattern.md` — The product line reduction is a textbook kill decision at massive scale. Jobs separated sunk costs from forward value.
- `lib/patterns/consensus-vs-conviction-pattern.md` — Nearly every early decision was conviction-based. Jobs had concentrated knowledge from his years at NeXT and Pixar.
- `lib/components/strategy-blocks.md` — The 2x2 product matrix is a masterclass in strategic choice: where to play and, critically, where NOT to play.
- `lib/taxonomies/risk-types.md` — Apple's existential risk was resolved by addressing concentration risk (too many products diluting focus) and strategic risk (no clear market position).

## What Board Would Have Caught

A well-functioning advisory board would have:
- Identified the product line bloat as the core operational problem years earlier, before the crisis demanded drastic action.
- Challenged earlier CEOs on strategic clarity using strategy-blocks.md — Apple lacked a guiding policy.
- Flagged the sunk cost fallacy keeping the Newton alive.
- Insisted on a turnaround brief with kill criteria for underperforming products.
- Recognized earlier that the board itself was part of the problem (board-effectiveness-rubric.md would have scored the pre-Jobs board as dysfunctional).
