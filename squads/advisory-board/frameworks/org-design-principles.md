# Organizational Design Principles Framework

## Purpose

Organizational design is the deliberate structuring of roles, teams, decision rights, information flows, and incentives to execute strategy. Poor org design creates friction, confusion, and politics that no amount of talent or culture work can overcome. This framework provides principles and a process for making organizational design decisions that align structure with strategy, enabling speed without sacrificing coordination.

## When to Use

- When scaling from one stage to the next (e.g., 20 to 50, 50 to 150, 150 to 500 people)
- After a strategic pivot that changes what the organization must be good at
- When cross-functional friction, duplicated effort, or decision bottlenecks emerge
- During M&A integration when combining two organizational structures
- When a founder-CEO transitions to a professional CEO and the org must professionalize
- During annual planning to assess whether structure still serves strategy

## Core Principles

### Principle 1: Structure Follows Strategy
Design the organization around what must be true for the strategy to succeed. If the strategy requires speed-to-market, optimize for autonomous teams with minimal handoffs. If it requires deep integration, optimize for coordination and shared services. Never reorganize for symmetry or neatness alone.

### Principle 2: Minimize Coordination Cost
Every handoff, approval layer, and cross-team dependency adds latency and error. Design for the fewest handoffs needed to deliver value. Two-pizza teams (Bezos) exist because small, autonomous units minimize coordination overhead.

### Principle 3: Align Authority with Accountability
The person accountable for an outcome must have the authority and resources to achieve it. Accountability without authority creates frustration. Authority without accountability creates recklessness. Map every key outcome to a single accountable owner.

### Principle 4: Design for the Work, Not the People
Resist the temptation to design roles around current individuals. Define what the organization needs, then find or develop the people to fill those roles. Person-shaped roles create fragility and succession problems.

### Principle 5: Information Should Flow to Decision Makers
Design reporting lines and communication channels so that the people making decisions have direct access to the information they need. Every relay point introduces delay and distortion.

### Principle 6: Span of Control Is a Strategic Choice
Narrow spans (3-5 reports) enable deep coaching but add management layers. Wide spans (8-12 reports) flatten hierarchy but require more autonomous reports. Choose based on the maturity of the team and the complexity of the work.

### Principle 7: Manage the Tradeoffs Explicitly
Every design choice involves tradeoffs. Functional structures build deep expertise but create silos. Product/business-unit structures improve speed but duplicate capabilities. Matrix structures balance both but add complexity. Name the tradeoff you are making and design mitigations for the downside.

## Common Organizational Models

| Model | Best For | Weakness | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional | Early-stage, expertise-driven orgs | Silos, slow cross-functional work | Cross-functional squads, shared OKRs |
| Divisional | Multi-product or multi-market companies | Duplicated resources, inconsistency | Shared services, platform teams |
| Matrix | Complex orgs needing both expertise and speed | Confusion, dual reporting conflict | Clear decision rights (RACI), strong culture |
| Squad/Tribe | Product-driven, high-autonomy cultures | Coordination gaps, knowledge silos | Guilds, chapters, internal open-source |
| Network | Highly dynamic, project-based work | Lack of career paths, instability | Strong project management, talent marketplace |

## Design Process

1. **Clarify strategic priorities.** What must the organization be exceptionally good at in the next 12-24 months? List no more than three capabilities.

2. **Map value streams.** Trace how value flows from customer need to delivered solution. Identify every handoff, approval, and dependency.

3. **Identify design criteria.** Based on strategy, define what the org design must optimize for: speed, quality, cost efficiency, innovation, customer intimacy, or coordination.

4. **Generate options.** Develop 2-3 structural options. For each, map the tradeoffs against design criteria.

5. **Stress-test with scenarios.** For each option, ask: Does this structure work if we double in size? If we enter a new market? If a key leader leaves?

6. **Define decision rights.** For every key decision category, specify who decides, who is consulted, and who is informed. Use RACI or a similar framework.

7. **Plan the transition.** Organizational change requires communication, retraining, and patience. Plan a 90-day transition with clear milestones.

8. **Measure and iterate.** Track leading indicators of org health: decision speed, employee engagement, cross-team friction, and delivery velocity. Adjust within 6 months if indicators are negative.

## Board Application

- Challenge management to articulate how the organizational structure supports strategy, not just what the org chart looks like.
- Review org design during annual strategy sessions and after any major strategic shift.
- Monitor key person dependencies and single points of failure in the structure.
- Assess whether the organization is designed for its current size or its next stage of growth.
- Use succession planning to test whether the structure is resilient to leadership changes.

## Related Frameworks

- **Lencioni Organizational Health** — Culture and org design are complementary; health requires both
- **Principal-Agent Framework** — Org design must align incentives between principals and agents
- **Incentives Design** — Structure and incentives must reinforce each other
- **Strategy Choice Cascade** — Org design is a "management systems" choice in the cascade
- **Hiring Scorecard Framework** — Role design precedes hiring; get the design right first
