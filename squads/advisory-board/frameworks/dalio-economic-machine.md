# Dalio Economic Machine Framework

## Purpose

Ray Dalio's Economic Machine framework provides a mental model for understanding how the economy works as a system of simple, mechanical transactions driven by three primary forces: productivity growth, the short-term debt cycle, and the long-term debt cycle. For advisory board members, understanding these macroeconomic mechanics is essential for timing fundraising, setting growth expectations, advising on financial strategy, and preparing companies for different economic environments.

## When to Use

- When advising on fundraising timing relative to economic cycles
- When setting growth expectations and financial plans
- When evaluating whether a company's challenges are company-specific or macro-driven
- When stress-testing business models against different economic scenarios
- During strategic planning to account for macroeconomic shifts
- When explaining economic headwinds or tailwinds to founders

## Core Model

### The Economy as a Machine
The economy is the sum of all transactions. A transaction occurs when a buyer exchanges money or credit for goods, services, or financial assets. Total spending drives the economy, and total spending = money + credit.

### Three Forces Driving the Economy

**Force 1: Productivity Growth (Long-term trend)**
- Output per unit of input grows over time through innovation, education, and efficiency
- This is the fundamental driver of long-term living standard improvements
- Grows at ~2% per year historically — steady, slow, and non-cyclical
- This is the line around which the cycles oscillate

**Force 2: Short-term Debt Cycle (5-8 years)**
- Credit expansion drives spending above productivity → boom
- Central banks raise interest rates to cool inflation → contraction
- Spending falls below productivity → recession
- Central banks lower rates → recovery → next boom
- Each cycle takes 5-8 years

**Force 3: Long-term Debt Cycle (75-100 years)**
- Over many short-term cycles, total debt levels grow faster than income
- Eventually debt burdens become unsustainable → deleveraging
- Deleveraging includes: austerity, debt restructuring, wealth transfers, money printing
- A "beautiful deleveraging" balances all four tools; an ugly one over-relies on one

### The Credit Cycle and Startups
- **Early expansion:** Easy credit, risk appetite high, venture funding abundant, valuations rise
- **Late expansion:** Credit overextended, quality declines, marginal companies funded
- **Contraction:** Credit tightens, funding dries up, weak companies fail, strong companies gain share
- **Recovery:** Survivors emerge stronger, new cycle of funding begins

## Steps

1. **Identify where we are in the short-term cycle.** Examine: interest rates (rising/falling?), credit growth (expanding/contracting?), unemployment (falling/rising?), inflation (accelerating/decelerating?).

2. **Assess the long-term debt position.** Look at: total debt-to-GDP ratios, household/corporate/government debt levels, interest rate room (is monetary policy constrained?).

3. **Map the implications for the company.** How does the current economic position affect: customer spending, funding availability, talent market, input costs, competitive dynamics?

4. **Build economic scenarios into strategic plans.** Don't plan for one economic environment. Plan for the current environment AND at least one alternative (recession scenario, inflation scenario).

5. **Time fundraising to the cycle.** Raise capital when credit is expanding and investor risk appetite is high. Build reserves before contraction hits.

6. **Adjust growth expectations.** In expansion, organic growth is boosted by macro tailwinds. In contraction, even good execution produces slower growth. Set expectations accordingly.

7. **Prepare for regime changes.** When interest rates shift from falling to rising (or vice versa), asset valuations, customer behavior, and funding dynamics all change. Prepare for transitions.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Help founders distinguish between company-specific performance and macro-driven performance. A company growing 50% in a booming market may be underperforming. A company growing 10% in a recession may be outperforming.
- Use the economic machine model to set fundraising strategy. The difference between raising in early expansion vs. late contraction can be the difference between survival and death.
- When macro conditions change, help founders adapt strategy rather than doubling down on a plan designed for a different environment.

### For Financial Planning
- Build multiple financial scenarios that correspond to different economic environments. At minimum: expansion continues, mild recession, severe recession.
- Maintain 12-18 months of runway regardless of cycle position. In contractions, this becomes 18-24 months.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Ignoring macro entirely.** First-time founders often build plans as if the economy is constant. It is not. Macro conditions affect every business.

2. **Mistaking a macro tailwind for company excellence.** Rising tide lifts all boats. Ensure you are measuring performance relative to the environment, not in absolute terms.

3. **Fighting the cycle.** Trying to grow aggressively during a contraction by burning cash is usually fatal. Adapt to the cycle; don't ignore it.

4. **Assuming this time is different.** Cycles have occurred for centuries. The specific triggers change; the pattern does not.

5. **Timing obsession.** Trying to time the cycle precisely is a fool's errand. Instead, build resilience for any phase and take advantage of extremes.

## Related Frameworks

- **Scenario Planning** — Use the economic machine to build macro scenarios
- **Base Rates and Reference Class** — Economic cycle position provides base rates for growth expectations
- **Expected Value and Asymmetry** — Cycle position affects the probability distribution of outcomes
- **Fundraising Timeline Framework** — Timing fundraising to the cycle
- **Competitive Response Model** — Economic conditions affect competitor behavior
- **Power Law Thinking** — Economic contractions concentrate market share in winners
