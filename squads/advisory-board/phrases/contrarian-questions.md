# Contrarian Questions

Phrases that challenge consensus, reveal hidden assumptions, and test whether the group is thinking or merely agreeing. Use these when the room converges too quickly or when you suspect groupthink.

## Challenging Consensus

1. **"Everyone seems to agree. That makes me nervous. What are we not seeing?"**
   Use when unanimous agreement arrives suspiciously fast. Genuine agreement is fine; reflexive agreement is dangerous.

2. **"What is the consensus view in our industry, and why might it be wrong?"**
   Use to test whether the board is following the herd. If our strategy matches everyone else's, it is not a strategy — it is a default.

3. **"What would we do differently if we assumed the opposite were true?"**
   Use to invert assumptions. If the analysis rests on "the market is growing," explore what happens if the market is shrinking. The inversion often reveals hidden risks.

4. **"Who benefits most from maintaining the current approach? Is that person in this room?"**
   Use to surface vested interests that may be distorting the discussion. Status quo bias often has a beneficiary.

5. **"Is this the best use of this capital, or is it just the use that's on the table?"**
   Use during capital allocation to prevent "choice from the menu" thinking. The best use of capital might not be any of the options currently being discussed.

## Questioning Fundamentals

6. **"If we were starting this company today, would we make this same choice?"**
   Use to strip away legacy bias. Sunk costs, organizational momentum, and historical commitments should not drive future decisions.

7. **"What would need to be true for the opposite of our strategy to be correct?"**
   Use to test the robustness of strategic choices. If the conditions for the opposite strategy are plausible, our strategy has a vulnerability.

8. **"Which of our current advantages is most likely to evaporate in the next three years?"**
   Use during strategy discussions to combat complacency. Advantages are temporary unless actively defended.

9. **"Are we building a moat, or are we building a sandcastle?"**
   Use to distinguish durable competitive advantages from temporary ones. Sandcastles look impressive but dissolve with the first wave.

10. **"What would our most capable competitor do with our resources?"**
    Use to benchmark ambition. If the answer is "something more aggressive than what we're doing," we may be underperforming our potential.

## Testing for Depth

11. **"You said this is a big opportunity. How big, specifically? Show me the math."**
    Use when enthusiasm is outpacing evidence. Quantification is the antidote to hype.

12. **"What evidence would change your mind?"**
    Use to test intellectual honesty. If nothing could change the person's mind, they are not reasoning — they are rationalizing.

13. **"Is this our conviction, or is this what we heard at the last conference?"**
    Use to distinguish original thinking from borrowed ideas. The best strategies are not widely known.

14. **"Three years ago, what did we believe that has proven wrong?"**
    Use to build humility and calibrate confidence. If we can't name past mistakes, we're not learning.

15. **"Are we solving a real problem, or are we solving the problem we wish we had?"**
    Use when strategic discussions feel detached from reality. The most important problems are often the most uncomfortable to confront.

## Provoking Better Thinking

16. **"What is the unpopular decision that we all know is right but nobody wants to make?"**
    Use to surface the elephant in the room. The most important decisions are often the ones the group is avoiding.

17. **"If we had unlimited resources, would we still choose this path? If yes, it's strategic. If no, it's a compromise."**
    Use to separate strategic choices from resource constraints. Know which you are making.

18. **"What are we optimizing for? And is that the right thing to optimize for?"**
    Use to challenge the objective function. Optimizing the wrong metric is worse than not optimizing at all.

19. **"Who is our real customer? Not the one we want — the one who actually pays."**
    Use to ground strategy in economic reality. Aspirational customer profiles are useful for planning but dangerous for decision-making.

20. **"What would the board that replaces us criticize about this decision?"**
    Use as a future-backward thought experiment. If a future board would immediately reverse this decision, it might not be the right one.

## Handling the Response

When contrarian questions create discomfort — which they should — follow up with:

- "I'm not saying I believe the opposite. I'm saying we should be able to answer the question clearly before we commit."
- "The goal isn't to be contrarian. The goal is to make sure our conviction is earned, not assumed."
- "If we can't answer this question, that's data. It tells us where our thinking needs more work."
