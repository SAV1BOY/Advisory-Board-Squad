# Channel Guide: Internal Memo

## When to Use

The internal memo is a structured written communication from leadership to the broader organization. It carries more weight than a Slack message or email — it signals that the content is important, considered, and official. Use it for:

- Strategic direction changes or major announcements.
- Post-board-meeting summaries for the executive team.
- Policy changes or new processes.
- Addressing organizational concerns (after engagement surveys, after departures, during uncertainty).
- Communicating difficult decisions (restructuring, budget cuts, pivots).

## Format

### Structure

```
INTERNAL MEMO
To: [Audience — e.g., All Employees, Executive Team, Engineering]
From: [Author — e.g., CEO, Board-Chair, VP Engineering]
Date: [YYYY-MM-DD]
Subject: [Clear, specific subject — not clever, not vague]
Confidentiality: [Internal Only / Management Only / Open]

---

[Opening: Why you're writing this memo]

[Context: What happened or what's changing]

[Details: The specifics]

[Impact: What this means for the reader]

[What happens next: Concrete next steps]

[Closing: Tone-appropriate sign-off]
```

### Length
- Standard memo: 1-2 pages (400-800 words).
- Major announcement: 2-3 pages (800-1200 words).
- Never exceed 3 pages. If the topic requires more, use a memo + appendix structure.

## Writing Guidance by Section

### Opening (1-2 sentences)
State why this memo exists. The reader should know the purpose before the second paragraph.

**Yes:** "I'm writing to share the board's decision on our Q2 strategic priorities and what it means for each team."
**Yes:** "This memo explains the organizational changes we're making effective April 1, and why."
**No:** "As you know, we've been going through a period of transformation and I wanted to take a moment to reflect on where we are and where we're headed..."

### Context (1 paragraph)
Provide the minimum context the reader needs. Do not assume they have the same information you do. But don't lecture — be efficient.

### Details (the core)
This is the substance. Present it in the format that makes it easiest to absorb:
- **For decisions:** State the decision, the options considered, and the rationale.
- **For changes:** State what is changing, when, and why.
- **For announcements:** State the news, the context, and the implications.

Use bullet points for lists. Use paragraphs for reasoning. Use tables for comparisons.

### Impact (1-2 paragraphs)
Answer the reader's first question: "What does this mean for me?"

- Be specific about what changes for the reader's daily work.
- If different teams are affected differently, call it out.
- Acknowledge disruption if it exists. Don't pretend changes are easy when they're not.

### What Happens Next (bullets)
- Specific next steps with owners and dates.
- Where to ask questions (town hall, Q&A doc, manager, specific person).
- Timeline for implementation.

### Closing (1-2 sentences)
Match the tone to the content:
- For positive news: energizing, forward-looking.
- For difficult news: empathetic, resolute.
- For neutral updates: brief, professional.

## Tone by Memo Type

### Good news / strategic direction
- Energized but grounded. Excitement is appropriate; hype is not.
- Connect the news to the team's work: "This is possible because of what you built."
- Be specific about what success looks like.

### Difficult decisions
- Direct and empathetic. Do not bury the lead.
- Acknowledge the human impact: "I know this creates uncertainty."
- Explain the reasoning honestly: "Here is why we made this decision."
- Provide concrete support: "Here is what we are doing for affected colleagues."
- Do not be cheerful about painful decisions. It is disrespectful.

### Post-board meeting summary
- Informative and transparent (within appropriate boundaries).
- Share what the board discussed and decided.
- Do not share confidential board dynamics or individual advisor views.
- Connect board decisions to team-level implications.

### Policy changes
- Clear and unambiguous. Policy memos should not require interpretation.
- State the old policy, the new policy, and the reason for the change.
- State the effective date.
- State who to contact with questions.

## Distribution

### Before sending
- Have at least one other person read it for clarity and tone.
- For major announcements, the Board-Chair or a peer executive reviews.
- For sensitive content, legal reviews before distribution.

### Sequencing
- **Affected parties first.** If the memo announces layoffs, affected individuals learn before the all-hands memo.
- **Managers before ICs.** Give managers 2-4 hours to prepare for questions from their teams.
- **Internal before external.** Employees should never learn company news from the press.

### Follow-up
- Schedule a live Q&A (town hall, team meeting) within 48 hours of any significant memo.
- Create a written FAQ document and update it as questions come in.
- The author of the memo should be available for questions — do not delegate availability.

## Anti-Patterns

| Anti-Pattern | Problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Burying the lead | Opening paragraph is vague context; real news is on page 2 | First sentence states why you're writing |
| Corporate voice | "Pursuant to our strategic initiative..." | Write like a human talking to other humans |
| Happy-spin on bad news | "This exciting restructuring creates opportunities..." | Difficult news deserves honest, empathetic delivery |
| No "what happens next" | Reader finishes and doesn't know what to do | Always end with specific next steps |
| Over-communication | Memos about everything; memo fatigue | Reserve the memo format for topics that genuinely warrant it |
| Under-communication | Major change communicated in a 3-line Slack message | If it matters, write a proper memo |
