# Thinking, Fast and Slow — Daniel Kahneman

## Summary

Nobel laureate Kahneman presents decades of research on the two systems that drive human thought. System 1 is fast, intuitive, emotional, and automatic. System 2 is slow, deliberate, logical, and effortful. Most decisions—including high-stakes board decisions—are driven by System 1, which is prone to systematic biases. The book catalogs these biases (anchoring, availability, loss aversion, framing, overconfidence) and provides frameworks for recognizing when System 1 is leading you astray. It is the foundational text for understanding why smart people make predictable errors.

## Key Principles

- **System 1 and System 2** — Two modes of thinking that operate simultaneously but with different characteristics. System 1 is always on; System 2 is lazy and engages only when forced.
- **What You See Is All There Is (WYSIATI)** — System 1 constructs the best story from available information without seeking what's missing. This creates overconfidence and narrative bias.
- **Anchoring** — Initial information disproportionately influences subsequent judgments. The first number mentioned in a negotiation, the first option presented in a board meeting, sets the anchor.
- **Loss aversion** — Losses are felt roughly twice as intensely as equivalent gains. This biases decision-making toward the status quo and against calculated risks.
- **Prospect theory** — People evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point, not in absolute terms. Framing the same choice as a gain or a loss changes the decision.
- **Overconfidence** — The most pervasive bias. Experts consistently overestimate their knowledge and the precision of their predictions. Planning fallacy is a specific case.

## Frameworks Extracted

1. **Pre-mortem Technique** — Before committing to a decision, imagine it has failed. Each participant independently writes reasons for the failure. This activates System 2 and surfaces risks that optimism suppresses.
2. **Reference Class Forecasting** — Instead of estimating from the inside (how this specific project will go), start from the outside (how similar projects have gone historically). Dramatically reduces planning fallacy.
3. **The Bias Checklist for Decisions** — Before any major decision, check for: anchoring (what was the first number?), availability (are we overweighting recent/vivid events?), framing (how is this being presented?), loss aversion (are we avoiding risk irrationally?), overconfidence (how wide are our error bars?).
4. **Two-System Decision Audit** — For important decisions: (1) What is System 1 telling me? (2) Have I engaged System 2? (3) What information am I missing (WYSIATI)? (4) What would an outside observer with no emotional stake conclude?

- "A reliable way to make people believe in falsehoods is frequent repetition, because familiarity is not easily distinguished from truth."
- "The confidence that individuals have in their beliefs depends mostly on the quality of the story they can tell about what they see, even if they see little."
- "Losses loom larger than gains."

## Application to Board Decisions

- **Decision quality** — Implement the pre-mortem technique for every major board decision. It counteracts the natural tendency toward overconfidence and groupthink.
- **Financial forecasting** — Require reference class forecasting for revenue projections and project timelines. Inside-view estimates are consistently optimistic.
- **Negotiation preparation** — Be aware of anchoring in all negotiations (M&A, fundraising, executive compensation). The party that sets the first number has disproportionate influence.
- **Risk tolerance** — Recognize loss aversion in board discussions. Boards often reject positive expected-value opportunities because the potential loss looms larger than the potential gain.
- **Framing awareness** — When management presents options, notice how they're framed. "95% survival rate" and "5% mortality rate" are the same statistic with different decision outcomes.
