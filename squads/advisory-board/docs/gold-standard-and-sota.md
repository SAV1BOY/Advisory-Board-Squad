# Gold Standard & State of the Art

## Purpose

This document defines what "excellent" looks like for the Advisory Board — the gold standard for each major activity. It also captures the current state of the art in board governance, decision science, and strategic advisory, so the board can continuously calibrate against best practices.

## Gold Standard by Activity

### Board Meetings

**Gold standard:** Every meeting produces at least two substantive decisions, stays within ±10 % of planned duration, includes genuine dissent on at least one topic, and generates minutes distributed within 24 hours.

**What "excellent" looks like:**
- Pre-reads are distributed on time, read by all advisors, and generate written questions before the meeting.
- The Chair facilitates with time discipline, ensures every voice is heard, and closes each decision with a formal record.
- Discussion focuses on the 2-3 highest-leverage questions, not administrative updates.
- Action items from the prior meeting are reviewed and ≥80 % are complete.
- The meeting would be valuable even if the company were performing perfectly — the board adds strategic insight, not just oversight.

### Decision Quality

**Gold standard:** ≥65 % of decisions produce outcomes within 30 % of projections. Every decision is recorded with rationale, dissent, and review date. Systematic biases are identified and reducing year-over-year.

**What "excellent" looks like:**
- The board distinguishes between decision quality and outcome quality. Good processes are celebrated even when outcomes disappoint.
- Pre-mortems are conducted for every Type 1 decision.
- Dissent is recorded in ≥20 % of decisions (indicating healthy debate).
- The annual decision quality review produces specific, actionable framework changes.
- Calibration improves over time — projection accuracy trends upward.

### Strategic Advisory

**Gold standard:** The board's strategic input is demonstrably different from (and better than) what the executive team would produce without board involvement.

**What "excellent" looks like:**
- The board brings perspectives the executive team does not have: cross-industry pattern recognition, contrarian views, and long-time-horizon thinking.
- Strategy discussions start from first principles, not from last year's plan.
- The board's "what we chose not to do" list is as valuable as the "what we chose to do" list.
- The strategy refresh produces genuine choices (where the company will play, how it will win), not a restatement of the status quo.

### Risk Management

**Gold standard:** No material risk materializes that was not already in the risk register. Mitigations are in place and effective for all critical risks.

**What "excellent" looks like:**
- The risk register is a living document updated monthly, not an annual compliance exercise.
- The board spends 15 minutes every quarter on risk, and the discussion is specific and actionable.
- The annual black swan exercise identifies at least one risk the board had not considered.
- When risks materialize, the pre-planned mitigations reduce impact measurably.

### Accountability

**Gold standard:** ≥80 % of action items completed by deadline. Follow-through scorecards are published at T+21 after every meeting. Overdue items never exceed 10 % of the total.

**What "excellent" looks like:**
- Commitments are made with realistic timelines, not optimistic ones.
- Blocked items are escalated immediately, not held for the next meeting.
- The CEO and executive team view the board's accountability function as valuable, not burdensome.
- The board holds itself to the same accountability standard it applies to management.

## State of the Art: External Benchmarks

### Board Governance Best Practices

Based on leading governance research and practice:

1. **Separation of oversight and management.** The board advises and governs; it does not operate. Clear decision-rights prevent role confusion.

2. **Structured decision processes.** The highest-performing boards use consistent decision frameworks rather than ad-hoc discussion. Expected-value analysis, pre-mortems, and disagree-and-commit are standard tools.

3. **Active dissent culture.** Boards that value and protect dissent make better decisions. The data is clear: diverse perspectives improve outcomes if they are genuinely heard.

4. **Decision review cadence.** Leading boards systematically review past decisions for accuracy and bias, typically quarterly (light) and annually (deep).

5. **Believability-weighted input.** The most effective boards calibrate input based on domain expertise, not seniority or volume of speech.

6. **Board effectiveness self-assessment.** Annual self-assessment with external benchmarking is standard for high-performing boards.

### Decision Science

Current state of the art in decision quality:

1. **Probabilistic thinking.** Replacing binary predictions ("this will work") with calibrated probabilities ("I assign 70 % probability to success"). Calibration training improves accuracy measurably.

2. **Cognitive bias awareness.** Understanding anchoring, confirmation bias, sunk cost fallacy, and groupthink is necessary but not sufficient. Procedural debiasing (structured processes that reduce bias) is more effective than awareness alone.

3. **Pre-mortem methodology.** Prospective hindsight (imagining future failure) produces more complete risk identification than traditional risk analysis. Systematically improves project success rates.

4. **Reference class forecasting.** Using base rates from similar past decisions to anchor predictions, rather than building forecasts from internal assumptions alone.

5. **Decision journals.** Recording predictions with confidence levels and revisiting them systematically improves calibration over time.

### Strategic Advisory

Current best practices in advisory board effectiveness:

1. **Structured advisory, not open-ended discussion.** Advisory boards that follow playbooks produce more consistent value than those that operate conversationally.

2. **Preparation-intensive model.** The ratio of preparation time to meeting time for effective boards is at least 3:1 for advisors and 10:1 for the chair.

3. **Written culture.** Boards that require written pre-reads and produce written decisions consistently outperform boards that operate verbally.

4. **Cross-functional composition.** The most effective advisory groups combine domain expertise with outsider perspectives. Homogeneous groups produce fewer insights.

5. **Feedback loops to execution.** The weakest link in most advisory relationships is the translation from advice to action. Structured handoffs (workflow 15) close this gap.

## Continuous Improvement

The board improves by:
1. Measuring itself against the gold standards in this document.
2. Benchmarking against the external state of the art.
3. Conducting annual effectiveness reviews (workflow 10) with explicit reference to these standards.
4. Updating this document as the state of the art evolves.

This document is reviewed and updated annually by the Board-Chair and Strategy-Analyst.
