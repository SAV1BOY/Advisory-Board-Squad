# Language Guide: Conflict Language

## Purpose

This guide provides language patterns for navigating conflict within and around the Advisory Board — disagreements between advisors, between the board and management, or within the executive team when the board is mediating. The goal is productive conflict that improves decisions, not destructive conflict that damages relationships.

## Principles

1. **Conflict is signal, not noise.** Disagreement means people care and are thinking critically. Suppress it and you get groupthink.
2. **Attack the idea, not the person.** Every statement should target the argument, the data, or the logic — never the individual's character, competence, or motives.
3. **Curiosity before judgment.** Understand before you evaluate. Ask "help me understand" before you say "I disagree."
4. **Name the type of conflict.** Not all disagreements are the same. Is this a factual disagreement, a values disagreement, or a priorities disagreement? Each requires different resolution.
5. **Resolve or commit.** Every conflict ends with either resolution (we agree) or commitment (we disagree but we align on the decision and support it publicly).

## Language for Entering Conflict

### Opening a disagreement
- "I see this differently, and I want to explain why."
- "I have a concern about this approach. Can I walk through it?"
- "My reading of the data leads me to a different conclusion. Let me share it."
- "I want to push back on [specific point], not because I doubt your intent, but because I think the logic has a gap."

### Inviting disagreement
- "I've shared my view. I want someone to tell me why I'm wrong."
- "What's the strongest case against what I just said?"
- "If you were advising a competitor, how would you attack this strategy?"
- "I notice everyone is nodding. That worries me. Who disagrees?"

### When you're the facilitator
- "We have a disagreement between [position A] and [position B]. Both positions have merit. Let's examine them."
- "Before we try to resolve this, let me make sure both sides feel heard. [Name A], state your position. [Name B], state yours."
- "I want to name what's happening: we have a genuine disagreement. That's healthy. Let's work through it."

## Language for Navigating Conflict

### Clarifying the disagreement
- "Help me understand: is your concern about the analysis, the recommendation, or the underlying assumptions?"
- "Are we disagreeing about what is true (facts), what is important (priorities), or what is right (values)?"
- "Let me restate your position to make sure I understand it. Tell me if I get it wrong."
- "What would have to be true for you to change your mind?"

### De-escalating without retreating
- "I hear the frustration, and I think it's because we both care about getting this right."
- "Let's slow down. I think we're talking past each other. Let me try to find where we agree."
- "The heat in this conversation tells me we're touching something important. Let's stay with it, but let's stay respectful."
- "I'm going to pause and check: are we still trying to solve the problem, or are we trying to win the argument?"

### Challenging without attacking
- "I respect your experience on this, and I want to test the reasoning because the stakes are high."
- "The conclusion may be right, but the argument has a step I can't follow. Can you walk me through [specific step]?"
- "I think there's a hidden assumption here: [state the assumption]. If that assumption is wrong, the conclusion changes."
- "The data supports [X], but I can see how the same data could support [Y]. What makes you confident it's [X]?"

### Acknowledging the other side
- "That's a fair point, and I hadn't considered it."
- "You're right about [specific point]. My concern is about [different specific point]."
- "I can see why someone with your experience would read the situation that way."
- "Let me update my view: given what you've said, I'm now less certain about [X], but I still believe [Y]."

## Language for Resolving Conflict

### Finding common ground
- "It sounds like we agree on the goal but disagree on the path. Let me see if there's a third option that addresses both concerns."
- "Where we agree: [list]. Where we disagree: [list]. The disagreement is narrower than it felt 10 minutes ago."
- "Both options have merit. Can we design an approach that captures the upside of both while mitigating the risks each of you identified?"

### Disagree and commit
- "We've heard both sides. I don't think we're going to agree. Here's what I propose: we go with [decision], and we review in [timeframe]. If [name]'s concern proves valid, we pivot."
- "[Name], I hear your dissent and I want to record it explicitly. Can you commit to supporting this decision publicly even though you disagree?"
- "Disagree-and-commit means your dissent is honored and documented, and your support for the decision is expected. Are you in?"

### When resolution isn't possible today
- "We're stuck. That's okay. The missing piece is [specific information or time to reflect]. Let's reconvene in [timeframe]."
- "I want to table this, but not indefinitely. We revisit on [specific date] with [specific new information]."
- "Let's each write a one-page argument for our position and circulate it before the next meeting. Fresh eyes might break the deadlock."

## Language to Avoid

| Avoid | Why | Use Instead |
|---|---|---|
| "You're wrong." | Triggers defensiveness | "I see it differently. Here's why." |
| "That doesn't make sense." | Implies the person is illogical | "Help me follow the logic from step 2 to step 3." |
| "We already decided this." | Shuts down re-examination | "We decided this in [month]. Has something changed that warrants reopening?" |
| "Let's agree to disagree." | Avoids resolution without committing | "Let's either resolve this or explicitly disagree-and-commit." |
| "I think everyone agrees." | Manufactures false consensus | "Let me check: does everyone agree, or is someone holding back?" |
| "That's not realistic." | Dismissive without evidence | "What would it take to make that realistic? I see [specific obstacles]." |
| "You always..." / "You never..." | Absolutes that provoke | "In the last two meetings, I noticed [specific pattern]." |

## After the Conflict

- **Document the dissent.** If someone disagreed, record it in the minutes. Dissent that is documented is dissent that is respected.
- **Check in privately.** After a heated exchange, the Chair checks in with both parties within 24 hours. "How are you feeling about that discussion?"
- **Follow through on review dates.** If the resolution included a review, honor it. Nothing erodes trust faster than promising to revisit and then forgetting.
- **Acknowledge when the dissenter was right.** If the outcome proves the minority view correct, say so publicly. This builds trust in the dissent process.
