# Conflict Resolution Approaches

## Overview
Conflict is inevitable in boards, leadership teams, and stakeholder relationships. The quality of conflict resolution determines whether disagreements produce better decisions or organizational damage. This file provides frameworks for resolving conflicts at the board level.

## Conflict Types

### Task Conflict
- Disagreement about what to do (strategy, resource allocation, priorities)
- **Generally productive** when managed well—leads to better decisions
- **Becomes destructive** when it shifts to personal attacks

### Process Conflict
- Disagreement about how to do things (decision-making process, roles, communication)
- **Moderately productive** when addressed early
- **Becomes destructive** when it creates ongoing frustration and paralysis

### Relationship Conflict
- Personal friction, mistrust, or dislike between individuals
- **Almost always destructive** — must be addressed directly or it poisons everything else
- **Key distinction:** Relationship conflict masquerading as task conflict is common and insidious

## Resolution Frameworks

### The Interest-Based Approach (Fisher & Ury)
1. Separate the people from the problem
2. Focus on interests, not positions
3. Generate options for mutual gain
4. Use objective criteria for evaluation
- **Best for:** Task and process conflicts where parties share an underlying goal

### The Empathy Bridge (Voss/Brown)
1. Label the emotion ("It seems like you're concerned about...")
2. Validate the feeling (you don't have to agree with the position to validate the emotion)
3. Explore underlying interests once defensiveness has diminished
4. Co-create solutions
- **Best for:** Conflicts with strong emotional components

### The Structural Approach
1. Identify the structural source of conflict (role ambiguity, competing incentives, unclear authority)
2. Redesign the structure to eliminate the conflict's root cause
3. Define clear decision rights, responsibilities, and escalation paths
- **Best for:** Recurring conflicts that stem from organizational design rather than personality

### The Mediated Conversation
1. A neutral third party (board chair, independent director, external mediator) facilitates
2. Each party states their perspective uninterrupted
3. Mediator reflects back and identifies common ground
4. Parties jointly generate solutions; mediator helps evaluate
- **Best for:** Relationship conflicts and high-stakes disagreements where direct conversation has failed

## Common Board Conflicts and Resolutions

### Founder vs. Investor on Growth vs. Profitability
- **Structural fix:** Agree on explicit financial milestones that trigger strategy shifts
- **Interest exploration:** Both want the company to succeed; disagree on the path. What evidence would change each party's mind?

### Board Member vs. CEO on Performance
- **Process:** Use the formal evaluation framework. Move from subjective ("I feel the CEO isn't performing") to objective (measured against agreed criteria)
- **If unresolvable:** A board member who has lost confidence in the CEO should push for a formal board discussion, not lobby privately

### Board Members Disagreeing on Strategy
- **Process:** Return to first principles. What does the data say? What are the assumptions? Use the strategy kernel (Rumelt) to diagnose the real disagreement.
- **If persistent:** Conduct a time-boxed experiment to test the competing hypotheses

## Board Application
- Don't avoid conflict—manage it. Avoidance leads to festering resentment and eventually to crises
- Match the resolution approach to the conflict type
- The board chair is the primary conflict manager; invest in this capability
- When the same conflict recurs, look for structural causes rather than blaming individuals
