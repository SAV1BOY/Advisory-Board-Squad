# Decision Log Framework

## Purpose

A Decision Log is the institutional memory of an organization's choices. It records what was decided, why, by whom, and what the expected and actual outcomes were. Over time, the decision log becomes the most valuable learning asset an organization possesses — revealing patterns in decision quality, calibrating confidence levels, and preventing the re-litigation of settled decisions. Organizations without decision logs repeat mistakes and forget the reasoning behind their most important choices.

## When to Use

- For every significant decision (Type 1 and high-stakes Type 2)
- When decisions are being revisited without new information
- When new leaders need context on past strategic choices
- When building institutional learning systems
- When calibrating organizational decision-making quality over time
- During board meetings to document formal decisions

## Core Model

### Decision Log Entry Structure
Each entry captures:
1. **Decision:** What was decided (one sentence)
2. **Date:** When it was decided
3. **Decision-maker:** Who made the call
4. **Context:** What situation prompted the decision
5. **Options considered:** What alternatives were evaluated
6. **Rationale:** Why this option was chosen
7. **Dissent:** What the strongest counterargument was
8. **Expected outcome:** What we predicted would happen
9. **Review date:** When we will evaluate the outcome
10. **Actual outcome:** What actually happened (filled in later)
11. **Learning:** What we learned from the gap between expected and actual

### The Calibration Loop
Over time, the log enables:
- Tracking prediction accuracy (are we overconfident? underconfident?)
- Identifying decision patterns (do we consistently underestimate timelines? overestimate market size?)
- Building institutional judgment (new leaders can learn from documented reasoning)
- Preventing re-litigation (when someone wants to revisit a decision, the log shows the full context)

## Steps

1. **Create a shared, searchable decision log.** Use a simple tool: spreadsheet, Notion database, or dedicated tool.
2. **Log every significant decision immediately.** Don't wait — memory degrades within days.
3. **Include the full context.** Future readers won't know what you knew. Write enough context that someone in 2 years could understand the reasoning.
4. **Set review dates.** Every decision should have a date when you will compare expected to actual outcomes.
5. **Fill in actual outcomes.** The most neglected step. Without outcome data, the log is just a diary.
6. **Analyze patterns quarterly.** What types of decisions do we make well? Poorly? What biases are recurring?
7. **Use the log in onboarding.** New leaders should read the decision log for their domain as part of their first-week onboarding.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Every board decision should be logged in a formal decision log, separate from meeting minutes.
- Use the log to hold yourself accountable. Track your advisory recommendations and their outcomes.
- When a founder wants to revisit a decision, pull up the log first. Often the original reasoning is still valid.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Not logging.** The most common failure. Decisions are made and immediately forgotten.
2. **Logging decisions but not outcomes.** The log's value is in the comparison. Without outcomes, there's no learning.
3. **Over-logging.** Log significant decisions, not every minor choice. The log should contain 1-3 entries per month, not 50.
4. **Private logs.** Decision logs should be shared. Private logs create information silos.
