# Capital Allocation

## The Capital Allocation Framework

### The Hierarchy (Buffett-Inspired)
1. **Reinvest in the business** — Only if internal returns exceed the cost of capital
2. **Acquire other businesses** — Only at fair prices with clear strategic logic
3. **Buy back equity** — Only when trading below intrinsic value
4. **Pay dividends / return capital** — When no better use exists
5. **Hold cash** — When optionality is more valuable than deployment

### The Key Question
For every dollar of capital: what is the expected return on THIS use vs. the NEXT BEST alternative use? This is the opportunity cost principle applied to capital allocation.

## Reinvestment Decisions

### Growth Investment
- **Organic growth:** Product development, sales capacity, marketing, geographic expansion
- **Test:** What is the incremental return on the next dollar of investment? Plot the marginal return curve.
- **Warning:** Many companies reinvest past the point of diminishing returns because growth is psychologically satisfying

### Maintenance Investment
- **Technical debt reduction, infrastructure, compliance, security**
- **Test:** What is the cost of NOT making this investment? (Often higher than the investment itself)
- **Warning:** Under-investing in maintenance creates hidden fragility

## M&A Capital Allocation

### Acquisition Discipline
- **Strategic fit:** Does this strengthen our competitive position in a way organic investment cannot?
- **Valuation:** Are we paying a fair price, or are we paying a premium for the excitement of the deal?
- **Integration:** Do we have a realistic plan for integration? Most acquisition value is destroyed in integration.
- **Alternatives:** Could we build this capability organically for less money and less risk?

### The M&A Trap
- 70-80% of acquisitions fail to create value for the acquirer
- The primary reasons: overpayment, integration failure, and cultural mismatch
- Boards should apply a higher burden of proof to acquisitions than to organic investments

## Share Buybacks

### When to Buy Back
- Stock is trading below intrinsic value (not just because cash is available)
- No better internal or external investment opportunities exist
- The buyback is funded from operating cash flow, not debt

### When NOT to Buy Back
- At or above intrinsic value (transfers wealth from remaining shareholders to departing ones)
- When funded by debt (adds risk without creating value)
- To meet EPS targets (financial engineering, not value creation)
- When management has undisclosed negative information (insider trading risk)

## Cash Reserves

### How Much Cash to Hold
- Minimum: 6-12 months of operating expenses (survival buffer)
- Additional: enough to pursue 1-2 strategic opportunities without needing external capital
- In uncertain environments: bias toward more cash, not less. Optionality has value.
- Buffett's rule: "Cash is like oxygen—you don't notice it until it's gone"

## Board Application
- Review capital allocation decisions against the hierarchy at every board meeting
- Challenge the CEO when capital is being allocated to low-return activities out of habit
- Require explicit opportunity cost analysis for every major capital deployment
- Monitor the marginal return on growth investment to detect diminishing returns
- Maintain a capital allocation policy document that codifies the board's framework
