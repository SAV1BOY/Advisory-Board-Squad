# Conflict Types Taxonomy

## Types

1. **Task Conflict** — Disagreement about the content of the work: what should be done, how it should be done, or what the priorities are.
2. **Process Conflict** — Disagreement about how decisions should be made, who has authority, or how work should be organized.
3. **Relationship Conflict** — Interpersonal friction driven by personality differences, past grievances, or eroded trust.
4. **Values Conflict** — Disagreement rooted in different fundamental beliefs about what is important or right.
5. **Status Conflict** — Conflict over relative standing, influence, recognition, or organizational hierarchy.
6. **Resource Conflict** — Competition for scarce resources — budget, headcount, leadership attention, or strategic priority.
7. **Information Conflict** — Disagreement arising from different information, assumptions, or interpretations of the same data.
8. **Interest Conflict** — Misaligned incentives where one party's success comes at another's expense.
9. **Structural Conflict** — Conflict arising from organizational design — overlapping responsibilities, unclear authority, or misaligned metrics.
10. **Identity Conflict** — Conflict that threatens how someone sees themselves or their role in the organization.

## Definitions

| Type | Root Cause | Constructive Potential | Destructive Potential | Primary Resolution Path |
|------|-----------|----------------------|----------------------|------------------------|
| Task | Different views on work content | High — diverse views improve decisions | Low if managed; high if it becomes personal | Evidence, data, structured decision process |
| Process | Unclear or contested decision-making norms | Medium — surfaces needed governance clarification | Medium — erodes trust if not resolved | Clarify authority, document decision rights |
| Relationship | Interpersonal friction, trust deficit | Low — rarely produces useful outcomes | High — poisons team dynamics, spreads | Direct conversation, mediation, separation if necessary |
| Values | Different fundamental beliefs | Medium — surfaces important organizational questions | High — values gaps rarely close with discussion alone | Clarify organizational values, accept if irreconcilable may mean parting ways |
| Status | Perceived disrespect or hierarchy violation | Low — status games rarely produce value | High — triggers defensive behavior, undermines collaboration | Explicit role clarity, recognition systems, direct conversation |
| Resource | Scarcity of budget, people, or attention | Medium — forces prioritization discipline | Medium — can become political if allocation is opaque | Transparent allocation framework, strategic prioritization |
| Information | Different data, assumptions, or interpretations | High — resolving information gaps improves decisions | Low — usually resolves once shared information is established | Share information, align on facts before debating conclusions |
| Interest | Misaligned incentives | Medium — surfaces incentive design flaws | High — misaligned interests create persistent conflict | Redesign incentives, acknowledge trade-offs openly |
| Structural | Organizational design flaws | Medium — reveals org design problems | High — structural conflict recurs until the structure changes | Redesign roles, reporting lines, or metrics |
| Identity | Threat to self-concept or professional identity | Low — emotionally charged, hard to discuss | High — triggers strong defensive reactions | Empathy, reframing, coaching, time |

## Examples

| Conflict Type | Scenario | Diagnosis Clue |
|--------------|---------|----------------|
| Task | VP Product and VP Engineering disagree on whether to build feature A or B | Disagreement is about the work, not the people. Both parties have data supporting their view. |
| Process | Team members are frustrated because the CEO overrides decisions made in team meetings | The complaint is "that is not how we should make decisions" rather than "that is the wrong decision." |
| Relationship | Two team leads avoid working together after a perceived slight at an offsite | Conflict persists across different topics. It is about the people, not the issue. |
| Values | Co-founders disagree on whether to prioritize growth at the expense of work-life balance | Both positions are internally consistent and stem from different worldviews. No amount of data resolves it. |
| Status | Senior IC is upset about a recent hire with a more senior title doing similar work | The conflict is about titles, recognition, and standing — not the work itself. |
| Resource | Marketing and Sales both want the additional headcount allocated in the budget | Both teams have legitimate needs. The conflict is about allocation, not disagreement on value. |
| Information | Board member challenges the revenue forecast based on data the CFO does not have | Once both parties see the same data, the conflict dissolves. |
| Interest | Sales team opposes new pricing model because it reduces commission on existing accounts | The conflict is rational — their compensation is threatened by the change. |
| Structural | Two teams keep building overlapping features because their mandates are not clearly delineated | The conflict recurs with different people in the same roles. It is the structure, not the individuals. |
| Identity | Long-tenured engineer resists new architectural patterns proposed by a new hire | The resistance is disproportionate to the technical merits. The engineer's identity as the technical authority feels threatened. |

## When to Use Each

**To diagnose conflict correctly:** Most conflict is initially perceived as task conflict ("we disagree about what to do") but is actually one of the other types underneath. Use this taxonomy to ask: "Is this really about the task, or is it about process, status, resources, or values?"

**To choose the right resolution approach:**
- Task and Information conflicts respond well to data, evidence, and structured decision processes.
- Process and Structural conflicts require governance and organizational design changes.
- Resource conflicts require transparent prioritization frameworks.
- Interest conflicts require incentive redesign.
- Relationship, Values, Status, and Identity conflicts require interpersonal intervention (mediation, coaching, direct conversation) — throwing data at these conflicts makes them worse.

**To prevent conflict escalation:** Unresolved task conflict becomes relationship conflict over time. Unresolved process conflict becomes status conflict. The key is to resolve conflict at its original type before it metastasizes.

**For board-level awareness:** Boards should be attuned to structural, values, and interest conflicts in the leadership team. These conflict types indicate organizational design or cultural issues that will not self-resolve.

**Escalation guidance:**
- Task and Information conflicts should be resolved at the team level.
- Process and Resource conflicts should escalate to the next level of leadership if not resolved within one decision cycle.
- Relationship, Status, and Identity conflicts should involve HR or a mediator if direct conversation fails.
- Values conflicts at the leadership level should be surfaced to the board — they often indicate a need for organizational clarity that only the top of the organization can provide.
- Structural conflicts should escalate to the person with authority to change the structure.
- Interest conflicts should be escalated to whoever owns incentive design.
