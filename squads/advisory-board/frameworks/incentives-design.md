# Incentives Design Framework

## Purpose

This framework provides a systematic approach to designing, auditing, and repairing incentive structures within organizations. The core principle: never attribute to malice or stupidity what can be explained by incentives. People respond to the incentives they face, not the incentives you intend. Every dysfunctional behavior in an organization can be traced to a misaligned incentive. This framework helps you find and fix those misalignments before they cause damage.

## When to Use

- When designing compensation, bonus, or equity structures
- When a team or individual is consistently producing unwanted outcomes
- Before launching a new program, partnership, or vendor relationship
- When diagnosing organizational dysfunction ("Why does this keep happening?")
- During board reviews of executive compensation and performance metrics
- When designing marketplace mechanics, referral programs, or customer incentives
- When evaluating M&A targets (what incentives drove their reported performance?)

## Core Model

### The Incentive Stack
Every actor in a system faces multiple simultaneous incentives arranged in a hierarchy:

1. **Financial incentives**: Compensation, bonuses, equity, commissions
2. **Career incentives**: Promotion, title, resume value, optionality
3. **Social incentives**: Status, recognition, peer approval, belonging
4. **Intrinsic incentives**: Autonomy, mastery, purpose, meaning
5. **Negative incentives**: Fear of loss, punishment, shame, exclusion

### Key Concepts

**Agency Problems**: When the agent (employee, advisor, vendor) has different interests from the principal (company, board, customer). Every delegation creates an agency problem.

**Moral Hazard**: When one party takes risks because someone else bears the costs. Insurance creates moral hazard. Guaranteed salaries create moral hazard. Bailout expectations create moral hazard.

**Adverse Selection**: When incentive structures attract the wrong people. Low pay attracts low performers. Commission-only attracts aggressive sellers. Equity-heavy attracts risk-seekers.

**Cobra Effect**: When the incentive designed to solve a problem makes the problem worse. (The British government paid for dead cobras in India; people started breeding cobras.)

**Goodhart's Law**: When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. If you incentivize lines of code, you get verbose code. If you incentivize customer count, you get unprofitable customers.

### The Incentive Audit Matrix

For any role or relationship, map:
- What behavior do we want?
- What behavior does the current incentive structure reward?
- What is the gap?
- How do we close it?

## Steps

1. **Identify the actors.** Who are the key players in this system? Employees, managers, customers, vendors, investors, regulators. Each has their own incentive stack.

2. **Map existing incentives for each actor.** What are they actually rewarded for? Not what the policy says — what the lived reality is. Talk to people. Watch behavior. Behavior reveals incentives.

3. **Identify desired behaviors.** What do you need each actor to do for the system to work? Be specific and observable.

4. **Find the gaps.** Where are incentives misaligned with desired behaviors? These gaps explain most organizational dysfunction.

5. **Design aligned incentives.** For each gap:
   - Change the financial incentives (compensation redesign)
   - Change the measurement system (new metrics)
   - Change the social incentives (recognition, visibility)
   - Change the information flow (transparency)
   - Change the structure (reporting lines, authority)

6. **Stress-test for gaming.** For every incentive you design, ask: "If someone were trying to game this, how would they do it?" Then close the loopholes.

7. **Implement with monitoring.** Roll out incentive changes with explicit metrics and a 90-day review. Incentive effects are often non-linear and delayed.

8. **Iterate.** No incentive system is perfect on the first attempt. Build in feedback loops and adjustment mechanisms.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Every board meeting should include an incentive audit of key decisions. Ask: "Who benefits from this recommendation, and how?"
- Review executive compensation through the lens of incentive alignment, not market benchmarks. Market-rate pay that incentivizes the wrong behavior is worse than below-market pay that incentivizes the right behavior.
- Be especially vigilant about incentives in fundraising. Investors, founders, and employees often have divergent incentives around timing, valuation, and exit.

### For Organizational Design
- When two departments are in conflict, look at their incentive structures first. Sales and engineering fight because sales is incentivized on bookings and engineering is incentivized on shipping. Align the metrics.
- Design incentives at the team level, not just the individual level. Individual incentives create competition; team incentives create collaboration.

### For Vendor and Partner Relationships
- Always ask: "How does our vendor make money, and does that align with our goals?" If the vendor makes money when you spend more, they will always recommend spending more.
- Structure partnerships with shared upside and shared downside. If only one party bears the risk, the other party will be reckless.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Assuming people will do the right thing.** They will do the incentivized thing. Design systems for humans as they are, not as you wish they were.

2. **Incentivizing inputs instead of outputs.** Hours worked, meetings attended, reports written — these are inputs. Revenue generated, customers retained, problems solved — these are outputs.

3. **Creating incentives with no downside.** Upside-only incentives (bonuses but no clawbacks) encourage excessive risk-taking. Include consequences for bad outcomes.

4. **Ignoring second-order effects.** A sales commission accelerates revenue but may also accelerate bad deals, customer churn, and team burnout.

5. **Designing for the average case.** Incentive systems will be tested at the extremes. Design for the person who will try hardest to game the system.

6. **Changing incentives too frequently.** People need stability to plan. Changing incentives quarterly creates short-term thinking and erodes trust.

7. **Measuring only what is easy to measure.** The most important behaviors (collaboration, mentorship, long-term thinking) are hard to measure. Design qualitative incentives alongside quantitative ones.
