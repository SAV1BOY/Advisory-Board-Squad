# Kill Decision Memos — Examples of Deciding to End Products & Initiatives

## Overview

Killing a product or initiative is one of the hardest decisions a leader makes. These memo examples illustrate how to frame, justify, and communicate the decision to shut something down.

## Example 1: Killing a Product That Still Has Users

### Memo: Recommendation to Sunset [Product X] Mobile App

**From:** VP of Product
**To:** Executive Team
**Date:** [Quarter] Strategic Review

**Recommendation:** Discontinue [Product X] mobile application within 90 days.

**Context:** [Product X] launched 18 months ago to extend our platform to mobile users. It currently serves 12,000 monthly active users.

**Why we should kill it:**
- Customer acquisition cost is 4.7x our core product ($340 vs $72)
- Retention at 90 days is 11%, compared to 68% for our core product
- Engineering maintenance consumes 15% of mobile team capacity for 2% of revenue
- Mobile users who convert to our core product do so regardless of the app's existence
- Competitive mobile offerings have surpassed our feature set with dedicated teams

**What we tried:** Redesign in Q2, push notification optimization in Q3, partnership bundle in Q4. None moved retention above 15%.

**Migration plan:** 60-day notice to users, export tools for data, redirect to mobile web experience, personal outreach to top 50 accounts.

**Resources freed:** 3 engineers, $180K annual infrastructure costs, 20% of QA capacity.

**Risk if we don't kill it:** Continued resource drain accelerates while competitive gap widens. Team morale suffers from maintaining a product everyone knows is failing.

## Example 2: Killing an Internal Initiative

### Memo: Recommendation to End Project Atlas

**From:** Chief Strategy Officer
**To:** CEO and Board

**Recommendation:** Terminate Project Atlas (international expansion initiative) and reallocate resources to domestic growth.

**Original thesis:** Entering three European markets would diversify revenue and reduce domestic concentration risk.

**What we learned:**
- Regulatory compliance costs exceeded projections by 280%
- Local competitors have entrenched relationships and 5-8 year head starts
- Our product requires significant localization that dilutes core platform investment
- Pilot market achieved 30% of Year 1 revenue target after 14 months

**Sunk cost acknowledgment:** We have invested $4.2M in Project Atlas. This memo recommends against investing further to "protect" that spend. The $4.2M is gone regardless of our decision today.

**Opportunity cost of continuing:** Every quarter we extend Atlas delays our domestic enterprise push by approximately one quarter. The enterprise opportunity is 6x larger with 3x better unit economics.

**Decision framework applied:**
- Would we start this project today knowing what we know? **No.**
- Is there a realistic path to the original business case? **No.**
- Are we continuing because of evidence or because of ego? **Ego.**

## Example 3: Killing a Feature Before Launch

### Memo: Recommendation to Cancel AI Chat Feature Before Release

**From:** Head of Product
**To:** VP Engineering, CEO

**Recommendation:** Do not ship the AI chat feature currently in final QA.

**Why this is hard:** The team has worked on this for four months. It is technically functional. Canceling it will be demoralizing.

**Why we must cancel:**
- Three competitors shipped similar features in the past 60 days — we are no longer first or differentiated
- User research conducted last week shows our target segment views AI chat as a commodity, not a differentiator
- The feature creates support burden we are not staffed to handle (estimated 200+ tickets/week)
- Shipping a "me too" feature sends the wrong signal about our innovation capabilities

**What we should do instead:** Redirect the team to the predictive analytics feature where we have genuine technical advantage and zero competition.

**How to communicate to the team:** Acknowledge the quality of their work. Frame this as strategic discipline, not a judgment on execution. Ensure the team leads the pivot to the replacement initiative.

## Kill Decision Principles

1. **Sunk costs are irrelevant** — Only future costs and benefits matter
2. **Ask the "clean sheet" question** — Would you start this today?
3. **Measure opportunity cost** — What are you not doing because of this?
4. **Speed matters** — A slow kill wastes more resources than a fast one
5. **Communicate with respect** — The team that built it deserves honesty, not blame
6. **Document the learning** — Every killed initiative should produce institutional knowledge
7. **Celebrate the discipline** — Killing well is a sign of strategic maturity, not failure
