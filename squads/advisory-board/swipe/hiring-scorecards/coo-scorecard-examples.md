# COO Scorecard Examples

## COO Evaluation Dimensions

### Operational Excellence (Weight: 30%)
- Builds and scales operational systems that maintain quality while growing throughput
- Establishes clear KPIs and accountability frameworks across all operational functions
- Drives continuous improvement in unit economics, cycle times, and error rates
- Manages vendor relationships and supply chain with rigor and leverage
- **Rating scale:** 1 (operations are firefighting-driven) to 5 (operations are a recognized competitive advantage)

### Organizational Scaling (Weight: 20%)
- Designs org structure that supports current needs and anticipates next-stage requirements
- Builds management layers that increase leverage without adding bureaucracy
- Creates hiring systems that maintain quality at higher volume
- Develops and retains operational leaders who can own functions independently
- **Rating scale:** 1 (organization bottlenecked on COO personally) to 5 (self-sustaining operational leadership at every level)

### Cross-Functional Execution (Weight: 20%)
- Translates strategic goals into operational plans with owners, timelines, and milestones
- Breaks down silos between departments and resolves cross-functional conflicts
- Runs effective operating cadence (weekly, monthly, quarterly reviews)
- Ensures information flows efficiently between teams and up to leadership
- **Rating scale:** 1 (departments operate independently, conflicts unresolved) to 5 (seamless cross-functional execution with proactive coordination)

### CEO Partnership (Weight: 15%)
- Frees the CEO to focus on strategy, fundraising, and external representation
- Provides candid operational perspective to balance CEO's strategic optimism
- Manages down and across so the CEO does not have to
- Maintains alignment with CEO on priorities while flagging disagreements constructively
- **Rating scale:** 1 (duplicates CEO's work or creates friction) to 5 (CEO's force multiplier, trusted to run the company day-to-day)

### Process and Systems Design (Weight: 10%)
- Implements systems (ERP, project management, reporting) that scale with the business
- Documents critical processes so they are not person-dependent
- Balances standardization with flexibility — enough process to ensure consistency, not so much that it strangles speed
- Drives adoption of tools and processes across the organization
- **Rating scale:** 1 (tribal knowledge, no systems) to 5 (institutional-grade systems with high adoption)

### Financial and Resource Management (Weight: 5%)
- Manages operational budgets within targets while maintaining service quality
- Makes resource allocation tradeoffs transparently and with data
- Identifies and eliminates waste without cutting muscle
- **Rating scale:** 1 (budget overruns, opaque spending) to 5 (disciplined resource allocation with clear ROI)

## Stage-Specific COO Criteria

### Early-Stage COO (Series A-B)
- Willingness to be hands-on: the COO is often the first person to build a process before hiring someone to run it
- Speed over perfection: build systems that are 80% right and iterate
- Comfort with ambiguity and frequent priority changes
- Ability to recruit operational talent into a company that has not yet built an employer brand

### Growth-Stage COO (Series C+)
- Scaling existing systems without breaking them
- Building middle management that maintains culture and standards
- Managing increasing regulatory and compliance requirements
- International operations expansion and multi-site coordination

### Pre-IPO / Public COO
- Institutional-grade operational rigor and SOX compliance
- Investor relations support with operational metrics and narratives
- Risk management and business continuity planning
- Board reporting on operational performance and capital efficiency

## Board Application
- The CEO-COO relationship is the most important dyad in the company — evaluate fit and complementarity, not just individual competence
- COO is the hardest executive role to define because it varies by company — insist on a written scope document before hiring
- Evaluate whether the company needs a COO or whether the gap is actually a VP of Operations, VP of Finance, or Chief of Staff
- Warning sign: if the COO is doing work the CEO should be doing, the company has a CEO problem, not a COO solution
