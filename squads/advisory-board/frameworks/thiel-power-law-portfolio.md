# Thiel Power Law Portfolio

## Purpose

Peter Thiel's Power Law Portfolio framework applies the mathematical reality of power law distributions to investment and strategic decision-making. In a power law world, the best investment in a fund outperforms all others combined. This has profound implications for how portfolios should be constructed, how time should be allocated, and how bets should be sized. It is the mathematical argument against diversification in venture-style environments.

## When to Use

- When constructing an investment portfolio (venture, angel, or internal product bets)
- When deciding how to allocate time across portfolio companies as an advisor
- When evaluating whether to double down on winners or spread resources
- When a startup is deciding between multiple product bets
- When evaluating fund strategy and portfolio construction

## Core Model

### The Power Law in Venture Capital
- The best investment in a successful fund equals or exceeds the value of all other investments combined
- The second-best investment equals or exceeds the third through last combined
- Most investments return 0
- This is not an anomaly — it is the fundamental structure of venture returns

### Implications
1. **Every investment must have the potential to return the entire fund.** If it can't, it shouldn't be in the portfolio.
2. **You cannot diversify your way to great returns.** In a power law environment, concentration in winners produces better outcomes than broad diversification.
3. **Double down on winners.** When you identify a power-law winner, invest more — don't rebalance.
4. **Time allocation follows the same law.** The company that will return the most value deserves the most of your time as a board member or advisor.
5. **Portfolio companies are not equal.** Treating all portfolio companies equally is a mathematical error in a power law world.

## Steps

1. **Accept the power law.** Most bets will fail. A few will succeed enormously. Design your portfolio for this reality.
2. **Ensure every bet has 10x-100x potential.** If the upside is capped at 2-3x, it cannot be a power-law winner.
3. **Identify winners early.** Look for: exponential growth curves, strong product-market fit, expanding TAM, compounding advantages.
4. **Concentrate resources on winners.** Time, capital, attention, and connections should flow disproportionately to the winners.
5. **Cut losers without guilt.** In a power law world, time and money spent on losers is stolen from winners.
6. **Size bets based on conviction, not equality.** Your highest-conviction bets should be your largest positions.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Allocate your advisory time according to the power law: spend the most time with your highest-potential company, not equally across all.
- When a portfolio company is clearly a power-law winner, increase your involvement. When one is clearly not, reduce it.
- Help founders understand that their startup IS a single bet in a power-law portfolio. The only acceptable outcome is a big win — modest success is the same as failure in venture math.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Equal allocation.** Splitting time and resources equally across portfolio companies ignores the power law.
2. **Supporting losers at the expense of winners.** Emotional attachment to struggling companies steals resources from winners.
3. **Capping upside.** Taking profits too early on winners is the most expensive mistake in power law investing.
4. **Too many small bets.** Diversification in venture is often a sign of low conviction.
