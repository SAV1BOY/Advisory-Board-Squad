# Case: Uber's Cultural Crisis (2017)

## Overview

In 2017, Uber experienced a cascade of cultural, ethical, and leadership crises that culminated in the forced resignation of founder-CEO Travis Kalanick. The crisis exposed how a toxic culture, left unchecked by governance, can threaten the survival of even the most valuable private companies.

## Timeline of Crisis

### February 2017: Susan Fowler Blog Post
Former engineer Susan Fowler published a detailed account of systemic sexual harassment, HR failures, and a culture that protected high-performing predators. The blog post went viral and triggered a public reckoning.

### March 2017: Greyball Revelation
The New York Times revealed Uber's "Greyball" tool, used to deceive regulators and law enforcement in markets where Uber operated illegally. The tool deliberately showed fake information to government officials attempting to enforce regulations.

### March 2017: Waymo Lawsuit
Alphabet's Waymo sued Uber for trade secret theft, alleging that a former Waymo engineer brought stolen autonomous driving files to Uber. The lawsuit exposed reckless acquisition practices.

### June 2017: Eric Holder Report
The law firm Covington & Burling, led by former Attorney General Eric Holder, delivered a damning 13-page report recommending sweeping cultural and governance changes.

### June 2017: Kalanick Resignation
Investor pressure, led by Benchmark Capital, forced Kalanick to resign as CEO. He initially resisted, but a letter signed by five major investors demanded his departure.

### August 2017: Dara Khosrowshahi Hired
Former Expedia CEO Dara Khosrowshahi was hired as the new CEO, chosen specifically for his reputation as a collaborative, ethical leader — the antithesis of the "win at all costs" culture.

## Root Causes

### 1. Values That Enabled Excess
Uber's original cultural values included "Always Be Hustlin'," "Meritocracy and Toe-Stepping," and "Principled Confrontation." These values, intended to drive competitive intensity, instead provided cover for bullying, rule-breaking, and ethical violations.

### 2. Governance Vacuum
- The board was composed largely of investors with financial alignment but limited governance experience
- Kalanick held supervoting control that made board oversight largely symbolic
- No independent directors with the authority or willingness to challenge the CEO
- HR functioned as a tool of management, not an independent check

### 3. Growth as the Only Metric
The relentless focus on growth metrics (rides, cities, revenue) drowned out every other signal. Employee complaints, regulatory violations, and ethical concerns were treated as obstacles to growth rather than existential risks.

### 4. "Brilliant Jerk" Tolerance
The culture explicitly protected high performers regardless of their behavior. Fowler's account described HR declining to discipline a repeat offender because he was a "high performer." This pattern was systemic, not isolated.

## The Recovery Playbook

### What Khosrowshahi Did Right
1. **Rewrote the values.** Replaced toxic value statements with new norms including "We Do the Right Thing" and "We Value Ideas Over Hierarchy"
2. **Hired experienced governance leaders.** Brought in a Chief Legal Officer, Chief People Officer, and Chief Ethics & Compliance Officer with real authority
3. **Settled fights.** Resolved the Waymo lawsuit and multiple regulatory disputes rather than continuing Kalanick-era legal battles
4. **Changed the tone.** Publicly acknowledged Uber's failures and committed to a different standard of behavior
5. **Rebuilt relationships.** Personally met with regulators, drivers, and employees to listen and demonstrate change
6. **Maintained the business.** Navigated the cultural transformation without losing operational momentum

## Lessons for Advisors

### On Culture
- Values that celebrate aggression without guardrails will attract and produce unethical behavior
- HR must be structurally independent from the CEO, not a compliance function that serves management
- The "brilliant jerk" tolerance is always a cultural cancer, regardless of the individual's output

### On Governance
- Supervoting structures that prevent board oversight create existential governance risk
- Independent directors with governance experience are not optional, even for private companies
- Investors who sit on boards must prioritize governance, not just returns

### On Crisis Response
- The cover-up instinct (suppress, minimize, deflect) accelerates every crisis
- External investigations (like the Holder report) create credibility that internal reviews cannot
- New leadership is sometimes the only credible signal of genuine change
- Cultural transformation requires years of consistent behavior, not a single announcement

### On Prevention
- Regular culture audits by independent parties surface problems before they become crises
- Anonymous reporting channels with visible follow-through build trust
- Board-level oversight of culture, ethics, and people practices should be a standing agenda item

## The Advisory Question

> "If a journalist obtained complete access to your company's internal communications, HR files, and decision-making processes, what would they find — and would it be consistent with your stated values?"
