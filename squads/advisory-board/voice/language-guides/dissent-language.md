# Language Guide: Dissent Language

## Purpose

This guide provides language for expressing, inviting, protecting, and resolving dissent within the Advisory Board. Healthy dissent is the board's primary value-add — a board that always agrees is a board that adds nothing. This guide ensures dissent is productive, respected, and honored.

## Principles

1. **Dissent is a duty, not a right.** If you disagree and stay silent, you are not doing your job.
2. **The quality of dissent matters.** "I disagree" is not dissent. "I disagree because X, and the evidence is Y" is dissent.
3. **Dissent has a shelf life.** Once the decision is made and dissent is recorded, the dissenter commits to support the decision publicly.
4. **Dissent is about the idea.** Never about the person, their experience, or their intentions.
5. **Recorded dissent builds trust.** When dissent is documented and later proven right, it strengthens the board's decision-making culture.

## Language for Expressing Dissent

### Clear, direct dissent
- "I disagree with the recommendation. Here's why: [specific reasoning with evidence]."
- "I see a flaw in the logic between step 2 and step 3 of this argument. [State the flaw.]"
- "The data supports a different conclusion than the one in the memo. Specifically: [cite the data]."
- "I think we're solving the wrong problem. The real question isn't [stated question] — it's [alternative question]."

### Partial dissent
- "I agree with the direction but not the timeline. Here's what I think is more realistic and why."
- "The analysis is strong. My concern is with one specific assumption: [name the assumption]."
- "I support the strategy but I think the resource allocation is wrong. Here's how I'd redistribute."
- "I'm aligned on the 'what' but not the 'how.' Can I propose an alternative approach?"

### Dissent when you're uncertain
- "I'm not confident enough to disagree outright, but something feels off. Let me try to articulate it."
- "I can't find the flaw in the logic, but my experience with similar situations suggests this will play out differently than projected. Here's what I've seen before."
- "I assign a higher probability to the downside scenario than the memo suggests. My reasoning is [reasoning]."
- "I want to register a caution, not a formal dissent. If [condition] proves true, this decision fails."

### Dissent as the designated Devil's Advocate
- "In my role as Devil's Advocate, here is the strongest case against this recommendation."
- "Let me argue the other side for a moment. Even if I don't fully believe it, these are the most dangerous counter-arguments."
- "If I were advising against this decision, here are the three things I would say."

## Language for Inviting Dissent

### From the Chair
- "Before we converge, I want to hear from anyone who sees this differently. This is the moment."
- "We've heard three voices in favor. I specifically want to hear a voice against. Who will take that position?"
- "[Name], you've been quiet on this. I know you have relevant experience. What's your read?"
- "Unanimous agreement this quickly makes me nervous. What are we not seeing?"
- "I'm going to wait 30 seconds. If nobody objects, I'll take that as genuine agreement — not politeness."

### Creating safety for dissent
- "I want to remind everyone: disagreeing with the majority position is not just acceptable — it's expected."
- "The strongest boards are the ones where minority views are heard, recorded, and taken seriously."
- "If you disagree and you don't say it here, you lose the right to say 'I told you so' later."
- "I commit to never punishing dissent. If you disagree, the worst that happens is we have a better discussion."

## Language for Protecting Dissent

### When dissent is being dismissed
- "Hold on. [Name] raised a legitimate point. Let's engage with it before moving on."
- "I notice we moved past [name]'s objection without addressing it. [Name], can you restate it?"
- "The fact that only one person disagrees doesn't make them wrong. What specifically is wrong with their argument?"
- "Let's steelman this dissent. Who can make [name]'s case even stronger?"

### When dissent is being punished (subtly or overtly)
- "I want to name something: I saw [name]'s dissent met with [sighing/eye-rolling/interruption]. That's not how we operate."
- "Dissent is how this board adds value. If we make it costly to disagree, we become a rubber stamp."
- "Let me be clear: the person who disagrees with the popular view is the person this board needs most right now."

## Language for Resolving Dissent

### Disagree and commit
- "We've heard the dissent. The decision is [X]. [Name], will you disagree and commit?"
- "Disagree-and-commit means: your dissent is recorded in the minutes, you support the decision publicly, and we revisit if [condition] arises."
- "I'm recording your dissent explicitly: [name] disagreed because [reason]. The review date is [date]."

### When the dissenter was right
- "I want to acknowledge that [name] raised [concern] during the original discussion. That concern has materialized. [Name], thank you for pushing back."
- "This is why we record dissent. [Name]'s minority view from three months ago is now the majority understanding."
- "Let's update our decision framework based on this outcome. The dissent identified [specific insight] that the majority missed."

### When the dissenter was wrong
- "The outcome doesn't validate [name]'s concern. That's fine — the dissent was still valuable because it forced us to examine [specific aspect] more carefully."
- "Being wrong about a specific dissent does not reduce the value of dissenting. Keep doing it."

## Documenting Dissent

Every formal dissent is recorded in the meeting minutes and decision log:

```
Decision: [Decision made]
Rationale: [Why the majority chose this]
Dissent: [Name] disagreed because [specific reason with evidence]
Review date: [When to check if the dissent concern materialized]
Resolution: [How the dissent will be tracked and revisited]
```

## Anti-Patterns

| Anti-Pattern | Problem | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Silent dissent | Disagreeing in the hallway after the meeting | Chair explicitly invites dissent before closing every decision |
| Dissent without substance | "I just don't feel good about it" | Require specific reasoning — "Help me understand what specifically concerns you" |
| Pile-on suppression | One person disagrees and five people immediately counter | Chair protects the dissenter: "Let's hear the full argument before responding" |
| Performative dissent | Disagreeing to look independent, not because of genuine concern | Over time, these become visible. Chair has a private conversation about signal vs. noise |
| Dissent without commitment | Disagreeing and then undermining the decision | Disagree-and-commit is a contract. If someone can't commit, escalate before the decision is finalized |
| Punished dissent | Dissenter is excluded, marginalized, or given less airtime | Chair monitors and intervenes. This is a board culture issue that must be addressed immediately |
