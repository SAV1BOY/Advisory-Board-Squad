# Failed Decision Memos

## Overview
Studying failed decisions is more valuable than studying successful ones because failures reveal the biases, blind spots, and structural errors that persist across contexts. These case studies examine decisions that seemed reasonable at the time but failed, extracting lessons for board-level governance.

## Kodak's Decision to Protect Film Business (1990s-2000s)

### The Decision
- Kodak invented the digital camera in 1975. Leadership chose to suppress and underinvest in digital to protect the enormously profitable film business.
- **The internal logic:** Film generates 70% gross margins. Digital photography cannibalizes our core business. We should manage the transition gradually.

### Why It Failed
- The transition wasn't gradual—it was exponential. Digital quality improved faster than Kodak's models predicted.
- Protecting the film business meant Kodak didn't build digital capabilities while competitors did.
- When digital became unavoidable, Kodak lacked the technology, talent, and culture to compete.
- **Root bias:** Status quo bias, loss aversion, and the Innovator's Dilemma (good management of the existing business prevented investment in the disrupting technology).

### Board Lesson
- When a disruptive technology threatens your core business, the question is not "if" but "when." Boards must pressure management to invest in cannibalization before competitors do it for them.

## WeWork's Decision to Pursue IPO at $47B Valuation (2019)

### The Decision
- WeWork filed for IPO at a $47B valuation despite negative unit economics, massive losses, and governance concerns around CEO Adam Neumann.

### Why It Failed
- The S-1 filing exposed: conflicted self-dealing transactions, no path to profitability, governance structures that concentrated power in the CEO, and a valuation disconnected from fundamentals.
- The IPO was pulled. Valuation collapsed to $8B. CEO was removed.
- **Root bias:** Overconfidence, narrative fallacy (the "future of work" story obscured the economics), and authority bias (SoftBank's $47B valuation was treated as validation rather than questioned).

### Board Lesson
- The board failed to challenge the CEO and the lead investor's valuation. Independent governance was absent. When a board cannot say "no" to the CEO, it is not functioning.

## Yahoo's Decision to Reject Microsoft's $44.6B Acquisition Offer (2008)

### The Decision
- Microsoft offered $44.6B to acquire Yahoo. CEO Jerry Yang and the board rejected it, believing Yahoo was worth more.

### Why It Failed
- Yahoo's competitive position continued to deteriorate. No alternative at a comparable or higher valuation materialized.
- The company eventually sold its core business to Verizon for $4.5B in 2016—a 90% decline from the rejected offer.
- **Root bias:** Overconfidence in Yahoo's standalone value, loss aversion (accepting the offer felt like "losing"), and anchoring (internal valuation anchored on past peak rather than future trajectory).

### Board Lesson
- When evaluating acquisition offers, the board must assess the company's realistic standalone trajectory, not just the offered price. The alternative to accepting isn't the status quo—it's the future competitive landscape.

## Template for Post-Decision Analysis (Failure Mode)

1. **What was the decision?** State it factually.
2. **What was the reasoning at the time?** (Without hindsight contamination)
3. **What information was available?** What was genuinely unknowable?
4. **What biases were likely at work?** (Use the bias checklist)
5. **What structural/governance failures enabled the bad decision?**
6. **What would have changed the outcome?** (Process changes, not just "decide differently")
7. **What is the transferable lesson?** (Applicable beyond this specific case)
