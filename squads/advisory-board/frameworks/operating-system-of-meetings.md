# Operating System of Meetings

## Purpose

This framework transforms meetings from default time-fillers into a deliberate operating system with distinct meeting types, each with a defined purpose, cadence, format, and output. Most organizations have too many meetings that all look the same — a mix of updates, discussion, and deferred decisions. This framework separates meetings by function, ensuring that the right people are in the right room at the right time for the right purpose.

## When to Use

- When building or restructuring an organization's meeting cadence
- When meetings feel unproductive, too frequent, or poorly attended
- When decisions are not getting made or are being revisited repeatedly
- When information is not flowing to the right people at the right time
- During periods of rapid growth when coordination complexity increases
- When onboarding new leaders who need to understand the governance rhythm

## Core Model

### Three Meeting Types (and Only Three)

#### 1. Decision Meetings
**Purpose:** Make irreversible or high-stakes decisions with the right people in the room.

- **Frequency:** As needed (not scheduled in advance — called when a decision is ripe)
- **Duration:** 60-90 minutes
- **Attendees:** Decision maker + people with relevant information + affected stakeholders (max 6-8)
- **Input:** Pre-read Decision Memo distributed 48 hours in advance
- **Process:** Silent reading (10 min) → Clarifying questions (10 min) → Debate (30-40 min) → Decision (10 min) → Action items and communication plan (10 min)
- **Output:** Written decision with rationale, dissent record, owner, timeline, and review trigger
- **Rule:** No decision meeting without a pre-read. No pre-read, no meeting.

#### 2. Execution Meetings
**Purpose:** Coordinate work in progress, remove blockers, and maintain accountability.

- **Frequency:** Weekly (same time, same day, never rescheduled)
- **Duration:** 30-45 minutes maximum
- **Attendees:** Working team (5-8 people)
- **Input:** Updated status on each person's commitments from last week
- **Process:** Round-robin status (2 min each) → Blockers (flagged, not solved in the meeting) → Commitments for next week
- **Output:** Written list of blockers (assigned to owners for resolution) + next week's commitments
- **Rules:** No discussion of strategy. No debate. No tangents. If an item needs more than 2 minutes, it becomes a separate conversation.

#### 3. Alignment Meetings
**Purpose:** Share context, build shared understanding, calibrate direction, and maintain culture.

- **Frequency:** Monthly or quarterly
- **Duration:** 90 minutes to half-day
- **Attendees:** Broader team (can be full company at early stage)
- **Input:** Strategic updates, key metrics, learning from the period
- **Process:** Context setting (30 min) → Discussion / Q&A (30 min) → Breakout work if applicable (30 min)
- **Output:** Updated priorities, shared understanding of direction, documented questions and answers
- **Rule:** This is not a decision meeting. Decisions flagged here should be routed to Decision Meetings.

### Meeting Types NOT to Have
- **Status meetings with more than 8 people.** Use async updates instead.
- **Brainstorming meetings without pre-work.** Distribute the prompt 48 hours ahead and collect ideas before the meeting.
- **Meetings to discuss meetings.** If your meta-meeting rate is above zero, your system is broken.
- **Recurring meetings without a clear type.** Every recurring meeting must be explicitly classified as Decision, Execution, or Alignment.

## Steps

1. **Audit all current meetings.** List every recurring meeting in the organization. For each, document: name, frequency, duration, attendees, stated purpose, actual purpose, and output.

2. **Classify each meeting.** Is it Decision, Execution, or Alignment? If it is none of these, question whether it should exist.

3. **Eliminate or merge.** Kill meetings that serve no clear purpose. Merge meetings that serve the same purpose for overlapping groups.

4. **Redesign remaining meetings.** For each surviving meeting, assign the correct format, duration, pre-work requirement, and output format.

5. **Establish the rhythm.** Create a visual calendar showing the meeting cadence for each team and the organization. Ensure there is protected deep-work time between meetings.

6. **Train facilitators.** Each meeting type requires a different facilitation style. Decision meetings need a devil's advocate. Execution meetings need a strict timekeeper. Alignment meetings need a storyteller.

7. **Implement a 90-day trial.** Run the new system for 90 days. Collect feedback. Adjust.

8. **Review quarterly.** Meeting systems decay. New meetings creep in. Quarterly audits keep the system clean.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Board meetings should be classified: which agenda items are Decision, which are Alignment? Most boards mix the two, which means decisions get deferred and alignment is superficial.
- Push for pre-reads on all decision items. Boards that read materials for the first time in the meeting are wasting their most expensive resource (board member time).
- Advisory sessions should be pure Decision or Alignment — never status updates. If the board is hearing operational status, governance is miscalibrated.

### For Founders/CEOs
- Your meeting calendar is a reflection of your management philosophy. If you spend most of your time in Execution meetings, you are managing. If you spend most in Decision meetings, you are leading.
- Protect at least 40% of your calendar for non-meeting work. A CEO who is in meetings all day is not thinking, and a CEO who is not thinking is not leading.

### For Teams
- Every meeting should end with: "What did we decide? Who owns what? When is it due?" If you cannot answer these questions, the meeting failed.

## Common Mistakes

1. **One meeting type for everything.** The weekly staff meeting that mixes status updates, strategic discussion, and attempted decisions is the most common meeting failure mode.

2. **Too many people in decision meetings.** Every additional person reduces decision quality and speed. The "two pizza rule" is a maximum, not a guideline.

3. **No pre-reads.** Meetings where the first 30 minutes are spent bringing people up to speed are a sign that pre-reads are not being distributed or not being read.

4. **Skipping the output.** A meeting without a written output (decisions, action items, commitments) is a conversation, not a meeting. Conversations are fine, but don't put them on the calendar.

5. **Making execution meetings too long.** If your weekly standup takes more than 30 minutes for a team of 8, you are solving problems in the meeting instead of flagging them.

6. **Letting meetings expand.** Meetings are gases — they expand to fill available time. Set aggressive time limits and enforce them.
