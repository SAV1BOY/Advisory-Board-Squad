# Leadership & Succession Crisis Playbook Examples

## Overview

Leadership crises — sudden CEO departures, founder breakdowns, succession failures — are among the most destabilizing events a company can face. These examples provide actionable patterns for navigating them.

## Crisis Type 1: Sudden CEO Departure

### Apple — Steve Jobs Health Crisis (2009-2011)
**Situation:** CEO's declining health created uncertainty about company's future direction.

**Playbook moves:**
- Quietly elevated Tim Cook through increasingly visible operational roles years in advance
- Board maintained confidential succession plan that predated public awareness
- Communicated continuity of strategy, not just continuity of leadership
- Stock price stabilized because the market saw a credible successor already performing

**Lesson:** Succession planning is not a document — it is years of visible capability-building in the successor.

### McDonald's — Two CEO Deaths in Seven Months (2004-2005)
**Situation:** CEO Jim Cantalupo died of a heart attack; successor Charlie Bell diagnosed with cancer within months.

**Playbook moves:**
- Board had a three-deep succession plan already documented
- Jim Skinner appointed within hours, preventing leadership vacuum
- Consistent strategic direction maintained through transitions
- External communication emphasized institutional strength over individual dependence

**Lesson:** Succession plans must go at least three levels deep. One backup is not enough.

## Crisis Type 2: Founder Conflict

### Uber — Travis Kalanick Forced Resignation (2017)
**Situation:** Board forced founder-CEO out amid cultural scandals, lawsuits, and investor revolt.

**Playbook moves:**
- Investor coalition (Benchmark) applied formal pressure through legal channels
- Hired Eric Holder for independent investigation to create objective basis for action
- Interim leadership committee managed operations during CEO search
- Dara Khosrowshahi hired as "clean hands" external CEO to signal cultural reset

**Lesson:** Removing a founder requires an evidence base, legal framework, and credible replacement ready to go.

## Crisis Type 3: Succession Failure

### General Electric — The Welch Succession Fallout
**Situation:** Jack Welch's succession process elevated Jeff Immelt while losing Jim McNerney and Bob Nardelli to competitors.

**Playbook moves that failed:**
- "Horse race" format created internal competition that damaged collaboration
- Losing candidates immediately departed to lead competitors
- Selected successor inherited strategy assumptions that were already outdated
- Board deferred excessively to outgoing CEO's preference

**What should have been done:**
- Evaluate candidates against future strategic needs, not past performance
- Structure the process to retain losing candidates in meaningful roles
- Ensure the board owns the decision independently of the departing CEO

## Crisis Type 4: Incapacitated Leader

### When a CEO Cannot Serve but Will Not Leave
**Playbook template:**
1. **Invoke governance provisions** — Review bylaws for incapacity clauses
2. **Engage legal counsel** — Understand fiduciary obligations of the board
3. **Appoint interim authority** — COO or board chair assumes operational control
4. **Communicate with stakeholders** — Customers, investors, and employees need clarity
5. **Protect the leader's dignity** — Frame the transition with respect and care
6. **Set a timeline** — Ambiguity destroys morale; establish clear milestones

## Emergency Succession Checklist

- [ ] Is there a documented succession plan accessible to the board chair?
- [ ] Has the successor operated in the CEO's absence before (vacations, sabbaticals)?
- [ ] Are key relationships (investors, clients, regulators) held by multiple leaders?
- [ ] Does the board have independent access to management below the CEO?
- [ ] Is there a communication template ready for sudden leadership changes?
- [ ] Has legal counsel reviewed the employment agreements for transition provisions?

## Advisory Talking Points

- "Every company is one phone call away from a leadership crisis"
- "The best time to plan succession is when you don't need it"
- "Succession is not about replacing a person — it is about transferring capability, relationships, and institutional knowledge"
