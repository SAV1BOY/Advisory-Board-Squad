# Thiel Secrets Framework

## Purpose

Peter Thiel defines a "secret" as an important truth that very few people agree with you on. Secrets are the raw material of transformative businesses. This framework provides a method for finding secrets — the non-obvious truths about technology, markets, and human nature that can be the basis for building valuable companies. The framework distinguishes between secrets of nature (things about the physical world) and secrets about people (things about human behavior and society that are hidden in plain sight).

## When to Use

- During ideation and opportunity identification
- When evaluating the depth of a startup's insight
- When challenging "there are no good ideas left" thinking
- When a market seems fully explored (it usually isn't)
- During strategic planning to identify non-obvious opportunities

## Core Model

### Why Secrets Exist
Secrets exist because:
1. **Incrementalism** — People focus on small improvements, missing big truths
2. **Risk aversion** — People avoid exploring unconventional ideas
3. **Complacency** — People assume the important things are already known
4. **Flatness** — In a "flat" world, people assume everything is visible to everyone

### Two Types of Secrets
- **Secrets about nature:** Undiscovered aspects of the physical or technological world. These require scientific or technical investigation.
- **Secrets about people:** Things that people don't know about themselves, or things that people hide because they are unfashionable. These require observation and social insight.

### Where to Look for Secrets
1. **Where others aren't looking** — unfashionable fields, overlooked problems
2. **At the intersection of fields** — where one domain's secret is another's obvious truth
3. **In plain sight** — things everyone sees but nobody articulates
4. **In contrarian data** — where data contradicts popular narrative

## Steps

1. **Ask: What important truth do very few people agree with you on?** If you can answer this with evidence, you may have a secret.
2. **Explore the edges.** What industries, technologies, or problems are unfashionable? What has been dismissed as impossible or unprofitable?
3. **Look for secrets about people.** What do customers say they want vs. what they actually do? What needs are unspoken or taboo?
4. **Test the secret.** Can you find evidence that supports your secret? Can you find others who independently discovered the same secret?
5. **Build on the secret.** If the secret is valid, it becomes the foundation of a strategy, product, or company.
6. **Share carefully.** A secret shared with everyone is no longer a secret. Share with those who need to know to help you act on it.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- The best founders have a genuine secret about their market. Ask them to articulate it. If they can, evaluate the evidence. If they can't, they may not have a true insight.
- Secrets about people are more common and often more valuable than secrets about nature. "People will pay for convenience even when they say they want to save money" is a secret about people.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing secrets with opinions.** A secret requires evidence. An opinion is just a belief.
2. **Assuming all secrets are technological.** Many of the best business secrets are about human behavior.
3. **Not looking because you assume everything is known.** This is the complacency trap.
4. **Sharing secrets too broadly.** Secrets lose value when they become common knowledge.

## Related Frameworks

- **Thiel Contrarian Truth** — Secrets are contrarian truths with evidence
- **Thiel Zero to One Thinking** — Zero-to-one companies are built on secrets
- **Naval Specific Knowledge** — Specific knowledge often comes from discovered secrets
- **Munger Latticework of Models** — Cross-disciplinary thinking finds secrets at intersections
- **Base Rates and Reference Class** — Base rates can reveal secrets hidden by narrative
