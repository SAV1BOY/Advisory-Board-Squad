# Pricing Strategy Memos — Swipe File

## Overview
Pricing is one of the highest-leverage decisions a company makes. A 1% improvement in pricing typically produces a larger profit impact than a 1% improvement in volume or cost. Yet most boards underinvest in pricing strategy, treating it as a marketing detail rather than a strategic decision. These memo examples illustrate rigorous pricing analysis.

## Pricing Strategy Memo Template

### Section 1: Pricing Objective
- **What are we optimizing for?** Market share, revenue growth, margin expansion, competitive positioning, or customer segmentation?
- **Time horizon:** Short-term revenue vs. long-term market position. These often conflict.
- **Constraints:** Competitive pricing, customer willingness to pay, contractual obligations, regulatory limits.

### Section 2: Current State Analysis
- **Current pricing model:** Per-seat, usage-based, tiered, freemium, flat rate, or hybrid
- **Revenue composition:** What percentage comes from each tier, plan, or pricing mechanism?
- **Price elasticity evidence:** What do we know about how customers respond to price changes? (churn data, win/loss analysis, survey data)
- **Competitive benchmarking:** Where do we sit relative to alternatives? Are we premium, parity, or discount?

### Section 3: Proposed Change
- **The change:** Specific pricing structure, levels, packaging, or model change
- **Rationale:** Why this change, why now?
- **Expected impact:** Revenue, margin, customer acquisition, retention, and competitive positioning
- **Sensitivity analysis:** What happens if adoption is 50% lower than expected? What if churn increases?

### Section 4: Implementation Plan
- **Grandfathering policy:** How are existing customers handled? For how long?
- **Communication plan:** How will changes be announced to customers, sales team, and partners?
- **Timeline:** Phased rollout with measurement gates, or big-bang launch?
- **Rollback plan:** If the change produces negative results, how quickly can we reverse?

## Example: SaaS Company Moving from Per-Seat to Usage-Based Pricing

**Objective:** Align revenue with customer value delivered. Current per-seat model penalizes heavy users and undercharges light users, creating adverse selection.

**Analysis:** 40% of customers use less than 20% of their licensed capacity. 15% of customers are heavy users generating 60% of support costs but paying the same per-seat rate. Net retention is 105% under per-seat; modeling suggests 120%+ under usage-based due to natural expansion.

**Proposal:** Hybrid model — base platform fee + usage-based component tied to API calls. Entry price drops 30% (lower barrier), but expansion revenue increases as usage grows.

**Risks:** Revenue may dip in Q1-Q2 as customers adjust. Sales team must learn value-selling instead of seat-counting. Need robust metering infrastructure before launch.

**Kill criteria:** If net retention drops below 100% for two consecutive quarters post-launch, revert to per-seat with lessons applied.

## Example: B2B Marketplace Introducing Take Rate Increase

**Objective:** Increase marketplace take rate from 8% to 12% to fund supply-side quality investments.

**Analysis:** Competitive take rates range from 10% to 20% in our category. Our NPS from buyers is 72 (strong), indicating willingness to pay for quality. Supplier churn at 8% is 5% annually; modeling suggests 12% would increase churn to 8% — manageable if quality investments improve buyer retention.

**Proposal:** Phased increase: 10% in Q1, 12% in Q3. Communicate the increase alongside visible quality improvements (verified suppliers, faster dispute resolution, insurance).

**Key principle:** Never raise price without simultaneously increasing visible value. The value increase should be announced before or alongside the price increase.

## Pricing Anti-Patterns

| Anti-Pattern | Why It Fails |
|-------------|-------------|
| Cost-plus pricing | Ignores customer willingness to pay and competitive dynamics |
| Matching competitor prices | Races to the bottom; assumes competitors have it right |
| One price for all segments | Leaves money on the table from high-value segments |
| Frequent price changes | Destroys trust and trains customers to wait for discounts |
| Hiding the price | B2B "contact us for pricing" signals the company is not confident in its value |
| Discounting to close deals | Trains the sales team and customers that list price is fictional |

## Board Application
- Pricing should be reviewed at the board level at least annually — it is too important to delegate entirely
- Challenge management: what is the evidence for customer willingness to pay at the proposed price point?
- Insist on a grandfathering policy for existing customers — surprise price increases destroy trust
- Monitor discount rates as a board metric: rising discounts signal pricing misalignment or sales discipline problems
- Ask: "When was the last time we raised prices?" If the answer is "never," the company is likely underpriced
