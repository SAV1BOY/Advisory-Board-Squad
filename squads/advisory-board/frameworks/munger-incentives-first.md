# Munger Incentives First

## Purpose

"Show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome." Charlie Munger's Incentives First principle is the most powerful single diagnostic tool in business analysis. Before analyzing strategy, execution, culture, or capability, analyze incentives. When outcomes are bad, the first question is not "What went wrong?" but "What were the incentives?" This framework provides a systematic method for making incentives the first lens through which you analyze any organization, decision, or dysfunctional pattern.

## When to Use

- As the FIRST analytical step in any organizational diagnosis
- When puzzling over why a team, company, or partner behaves in ways that seem irrational
- When designing compensation, bonus structures, or performance metrics
- When evaluating vendor, partner, or advisor relationships
- Before designing any system that involves human behavior
- When a well-designed strategy is producing poor execution

## Core Model

### Munger's Hierarchy of Explanatory Power
When something goes wrong, investigate in this order:
1. **Incentives** (explains ~70% of all organizational behavior)
2. **Cognitive biases** (explains ~20% of what incentives don't)
3. **Genuine incompetence** (explains the remaining ~10%)

Most people investigate in the reverse order: they assume incompetence first, biases second, and rarely check incentives. Munger inverts this.

### The Incentive Diagnostic

**Direct incentives**: What is the person explicitly rewarded for? Compensation, bonus triggers, promotion criteria, commission structures.

**Indirect incentives**: What is the person implicitly rewarded for? Social status, career advancement, avoiding conflict, looking good to the boss.

**Perverse incentives**: Where does the incentive structure reward behavior that is harmful to the organization? This is where the gold is.

**Missing incentives**: What desired behaviors have no associated reward? These behaviors will be systematically under-produced.

### Munger's Incentive Principles

1. **Never think about something else when you should be thinking about incentives.** Incentive analysis comes first, not last.

2. **The iron law of incentives: People will optimize for whatever you measure and reward.** If you measure hours, you get hours. If you measure output, you get output. If you measure nothing, you get nothing.

3. **Incentives work in both directions.** Positive incentives attract desired behavior. Removing perverse incentives often works better than adding positive ones.

4. **People are not consciously gaming incentives.** Most incentive-driven behavior is unconscious. The salesperson who oversells is not malicious — they are responding to a commission structure that rewards bookings over retention.

5. **The most dangerous incentives are the ones nobody talks about.** Career incentives, social incentives, and political incentives are rarely documented but profoundly shape behavior.

## Steps

1. **Identify the behavior you want to understand or change.** Be specific: "Sales team is discounting aggressively" not "Sales isn't performing."

2. **Map all incentives the actor faces.** Financial, career, social, intrinsic, negative. Include undocumented incentives (what the culture rewards).

3. **Find the misalignment.** Which incentive is driving the unwanted behavior? Almost always, the behavior makes perfect sense once you see the incentive.

4. **Design the incentive fix.** Options:
   - Realign the incentive (change the reward structure)
   - Remove the perverse incentive
   - Add a counter-incentive
   - Change the measurement system
   - Increase transparency (making the incentive visible changes behavior)

5. **Predict second-order effects.** Every incentive change creates new incentives. What new behaviors will the change produce? (See: Cobra Effect)

6. **Test before full rollout.** Pilot the incentive change with a small group. Observe behavior. Adjust. Then scale.

7. **Monitor for gaming.** After implementation, actively look for people optimizing for the letter of the incentive rather than the spirit. Adjust when found.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Before every board recommendation, ask yourself: "What are my incentives for giving this advice?" Advisory fees, equity upside, reputation, desire to be liked — all of these shape advice. Be honest about your own incentives.
- When a company is underperforming, start with incentive analysis before strategy analysis. The strategy might be sound; the incentives might be broken.
- Evaluate CEO compensation through Munger's lens: "What behavior does this package incentivize?" If the answer is "short-term stock price manipulation," the package is wrong regardless of market benchmarks.

### For Organizational Diagnosis
- When two departments are in conflict, map their respective incentives. Conflict between sales and customer success is almost always an incentive misalignment, not a personality clash.
- When employees are leaving, exit interviews often cite culture or management. Dig deeper into incentives: were they paid fairly? Were the right behaviors rewarded? Was there a path to advancement?

## Common Mistakes

1. **Assuming good intentions override bad incentives.** They don't. Good people in bad incentive structures produce bad outcomes. Always.

2. **Fixing people instead of incentives.** Replacing a "problem employee" without fixing the incentive that produced the problem will produce the same problem with the replacement.

3. **Ignoring your own incentives.** Everyone has incentives, including you. The failure to examine your own incentives is the blind spot Munger warns about most.

4. **Over-relying on intrinsic motivation.** Intrinsic motivation is real but fragile. It is destroyed by misaligned extrinsic incentives. Get the extrinsic incentives right first; intrinsic motivation flourishes on that foundation.

5. **Complex incentive structures.** If the incentive system requires a spreadsheet to understand, people will not respond to it predictably. Simplicity is essential.

6. **Delayed incentives.** Incentives that pay out annually lose motivational power monthly. Shorter feedback loops are more effective than larger, delayed rewards.
