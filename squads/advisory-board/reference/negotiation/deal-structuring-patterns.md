# Deal Structuring Patterns

## Overview
How a deal is structured often matters more than the headline terms. Structure determines risk allocation, incentive alignment, and the probability of a successful outcome. This file catalogs common deal structures and their implications for advisory board decision-making.

## Equity Deal Structures

### Stock Purchase
- Buyer acquires 100% of seller's equity (all assets and liabilities)
- **Buyer risk:** Inherits all known and unknown liabilities
- **Seller advantage:** Clean exit, typically favorable tax treatment
- **When to use:** Seller is clean, buyer wants full control, speed matters

### Asset Purchase
- Buyer selectively acquires assets (IP, contracts, equipment) and assumes specified liabilities
- **Buyer advantage:** Choose what to acquire, leave problematic liabilities behind
- **Seller disadvantage:** May face double taxation, residual liabilities remain
- **When to use:** Buyer wants specific assets without legacy risk

### Merger
- Two entities combine into one; can be structured as forward, reverse, or triangular merger
- **Reverse merger:** Target survives; useful when target has valuable licenses or contracts
- **When to use:** Strategic combination of equals or near-equals

## Payment Structures

### All Cash
- Seller receives 100% cash at closing
- **Seller advantage:** Certainty, liquidity, no ongoing risk
- **Buyer cost:** Maximum cash outflow, no risk sharing

### Cash + Earnout
- Portion of purchase price contingent on future performance metrics
- **Seller risk:** Performance may be affected by buyer's post-acquisition decisions
- **Buyer advantage:** Reduces overpayment risk, aligns seller's incentives during transition
- **Key design:** Clear metrics, realistic targets, seller maintains operational control over earnout period

### Stock Swap
- Buyer pays with its own equity
- **Seller risk:** Exposed to buyer's future performance; liquidity may be limited
- **Buyer advantage:** Preserves cash, aligns seller with combined entity
- **When to use:** High-growth buyer whose stock may appreciate

### Seller Financing
- Seller effectively lends buyer part of the purchase price
- **Seller risk:** Dependent on buyer's operational success for full payment
- **Buyer advantage:** Reduces cash requirements; seller's ongoing stake aligns interests
- **When to use:** Buyer has limited cash, seller has high confidence in the business's stability

## Risk Allocation Mechanisms

### Representations and Warranties
- Seller makes factual statements about the business; buyer relies on them
- Breaches can trigger indemnification claims
- **Scope:** Financial statements, IP ownership, contracts, litigation, compliance, employee matters

### Escrow / Holdback
- Portion of purchase price held in escrow for 12-24 months to cover potential claims
- **Typical range:** 5-15% of purchase price
- **Purpose:** Creates a readily accessible fund for indemnification without litigation

### Indemnification
- Seller agrees to compensate buyer for specified losses post-closing
- **Caps:** Usually 10-25% of purchase price for general reps; may be uncapped for fundamental reps (fraud, title, taxes)
- **Baskets:** Minimum threshold before indemnification applies

### Material Adverse Change (MAC) Clause
- Allows buyer to walk away if a material adverse change occurs between signing and closing
- **Heavily negotiated:** Definition of "material" is the key battleground

## Board Application
- For any proposed deal, ensure the board understands the structure, not just the headline price
- Challenge whether the structure properly aligns incentives between buyer and seller
- Pay special attention to earnout structures: poorly designed earnouts create post-acquisition conflict
- Ensure adequate indemnification and escrow protection for the company's position (whether buying or selling)
