# What You Do Is Who You Are — Ben Horowitz

## Summary

Horowitz examines how leaders build organizational culture by studying four unlikely cultural architects: Toussaint Louverture (Haitian revolution leader), the Bushido samurai code, Genghis Khan, and Shaka Senghor (prison gang leader turned author). The central argument: culture is not what you say—it's what you do. It's not your values poster—it's who you hire, fire, and promote. It's not your mission statement—it's how you behave when no one is watching. The book provides practical techniques for encoding desired behaviors into the daily fabric of an organization.

## Key Principles

- **Culture is action, not words** — What you do is who you are. If you say you value innovation but fire people who fail, your real culture is risk-aversion.
- **Shocking rules create cultural clarity** — Rules that seem extreme or counterintuitive force people to think about why the rule exists, which embeds the underlying value. Example: Amazon's door desks signal frugality.
- **Who you hire and fire defines culture** — Every hiring and firing decision is a cultural statement. Tolerating brilliant jerks tells the organization that results matter more than values.
- **Virtues over values** — Values are beliefs. Virtues are practices. Culture must be built on what people do, not what they believe. "We value integrity" means nothing. "We return customer money within 24 hours if dissatisfied, no questions asked" is a virtue.
- **Include people from the culture you want** — Louverture included former enemies and people from different backgrounds. Inclusive cultures are more resilient than homogeneous ones.
- **Walk the talk, especially when it's costly** — Culture is defined in the moments when following your values costs you something. Easy adherence proves nothing.

## Frameworks Extracted

1. **The Culture Design Canvas** — (1) Identify 3-5 behaviors that define the culture you want, (2) Create "shocking rules" that make those behaviors vivid, (3) Design hiring screens that test for those behaviors, (4) Ensure promotion criteria reinforce them, (5) Fire people who violate them regardless of their results.
2. **The Virtues Translation** — For each stated value: (1) What specific behavior does this require? (2) What specific behavior does this prohibit? (3) What would it look like at its most extreme? (4) What does violation look like? If you can't answer these, the value is too vague to be useful.
3. **The Costly Moment Test** — Culture is tested when following your values costs revenue, speed, or convenience. Track these moments and how they're handled. A pattern of exceptions reveals the real culture.
4. **The New Employee Test** — Within their first week, new employees learn the real culture through observation (not orientation decks). Audit what new employees are actually seeing and concluding about "how things work here."

- "You can't fix culture with a poster. You can only fix it with action."

## Application to Board Decisions

- **Culture governance** — The board should assess culture through behavior audits (who was hired, fired, promoted, and why) rather than through employee surveys or values statements.
- **CEO evaluation** — Include culture stewardship in CEO evaluation. Apply the Costly Moment Test: when did the CEO sacrifice short-term results to uphold values? When did they compromise values for results?
- **M&A culture integration** — Before acquisitions, apply the Virtues Translation to both cultures. If the behaviors conflict, integration will fail regardless of operational synergies.
- **Crisis response** — In crisis, culture is revealed. The board should observe how the organization behaves under pressure and compare it to stated values.
- **Succession planning** — The next CEO must embody the culture. Use the Culture Design Canvas to define the behavioral requirements for leadership succession.
