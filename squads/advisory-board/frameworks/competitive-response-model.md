# Competitive Response Model

## Purpose

This framework provides a structured method for predicting how competitors will react to your strategic moves. Most strategy is built as if competitors are static — they are not. Every move you make triggers a response. The Competitive Response Model maps likely competitor reactions before you commit to a strategy, enabling you to choose moves that create the most advantage even after competitive response is factored in.

## When to Use

- Before launching a new product, entering a new market, or changing pricing
- When evaluating competitive threats and planning counter-moves
- During strategic planning to stress-test strategies against competitor reactions
- Before public announcements that competitors will observe and respond to
- When a competitor has made a move and you need to decide how to respond
- When evaluating acquisition targets (how will competitors react to the acquisition?)

## Core Model

### The Response Chain
Every strategic action triggers a chain: Your move → Competitor detection → Competitor analysis → Competitor decision → Competitor response → Your counter-response. Strategy is not a single move but a sequence.

### Three Determinants of Competitive Response

**1. Awareness**: Will the competitor notice your move?
- High visibility moves (pricing changes, public launches) get noticed immediately
- Low visibility moves (operational improvements, talent acquisition) may go unnoticed for months
- Stealth is a strategic advantage — the best moves are the ones competitors don't see until it's too late

**2. Motivation**: Will the competitor care enough to respond?
- Threat to core business → strong motivation to respond
- Threat to peripheral business → weak motivation
- The "attacker's advantage": incumbents are slow to respond to threats in their marginal segments
- Asymmetric stakes: If your move threatens their #1 revenue stream, they will respond aggressively

**3. Capability**: Can the competitor effectively respond?
- Financial resources to fund a response
- Organizational agility to execute quickly
- Technical capability to match or counter
- Strategic flexibility (are they locked into a different path?)

### Four Response Types

| Response | Speed | Intensity | When Used |
|----------|-------|-----------|-----------|
| **Ignore** | N/A | None | Threat is peripheral or competitor is overconfident |
| **Accommodate** | Slow | Low | Competitor adjusts but doesn't counter-attack |
| **Match** | Medium | Medium | Competitor copies your move (fast-follower) |
| **Retaliate** | Fast | High | Competitor counter-attacks aggressively (price war, feature war) |

## Steps

1. **Map the competitive landscape.** Identify all relevant competitors — direct, indirect, potential entrants, and substitutes.

2. **Define your planned move.** State it clearly: what you will do, when, and what the expected impact on the market is.

3. **For each competitor, assess Awareness-Motivation-Capability (AMC):**

   | Competitor | Awareness (H/M/L) | Motivation (H/M/L) | Capability (H/M/L) | Likely Response |
   |------------|-------------------|--------------------|--------------------|-----------------|
   | A          |                   |                    |                    |                 |

4. **Predict the most likely response for each competitor.** Use the AMC scores to predict: Ignore, Accommodate, Match, or Retaliate.

5. **Simulate the response chain.** For each likely response, plan your counter-response. Think 2-3 moves ahead.

6. **Evaluate your move considering the full chain.** Does your strategy still create advantage after competitor response? If not, choose a different move.

7. **Identify moves that are hard to respond to.** The best strategies exploit structural advantages that competitors cannot easily match: network effects, proprietary data, regulatory moats, two-sided markets.

8. **Build monitoring.** Track competitor actions weekly. The sooner you detect a response, the sooner you can adapt.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- When founders present competitive strategy, ask: "How will your top three competitors respond to this?" If they haven't thought about it, the strategy is incomplete.
- Help founders distinguish between competitive paranoia (wasteful) and competitive intelligence (essential).
- Use this framework to evaluate defensibility claims. "We'll win because we're first" is not a competitive response plan.

### For Pricing Decisions
- Pricing changes are the moves most likely to trigger immediate retaliation. Model the response before changing prices.
- Downward pricing is especially dangerous — it is easy for competitors to match and hard to reverse. Ensure you have a structural cost advantage before competing on price.

### For Market Entry
- The incumbent's response to market entry depends on how threatened they are. Enter through the segment the incumbent cares about least.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Assuming competitors are static.** The #1 strategy error. Your plan vs. their current state is not a strategy — your plan vs. their response is.

2. **Overestimating competitor rationality.** Competitors don't always respond rationally. Ego, politics, and organizational dysfunction all affect competitive response.

3. **Underestimating competitor speed.** Large companies can move fast when their core is threatened. Do not assume incumbents are slow.

4. **Ignoring indirect competitors.** The biggest competitive threat is often not the company that looks like you — it is the company solving the same customer problem in a completely different way.

5. **Triggering unnecessary competition.** Some moves provoke responses that destroy value for everyone. Price wars, feature wars, and talent wars often have no winners.

6. **Confusing competitors with enemies.** Competition is structural, not personal. Sometimes the best move is cooperation (expanding the market) rather than direct competition (dividing the market).

## Related Frameworks

- **Moat Map** — Moats are what make competitive responses difficult
- **Strategy Choice Cascade** — Competitive response should inform "How to Win"
- **Inversion Framework** — "How would a competitor destroy our advantage?"
- **Scenario Planning** — Model competitive responses as scenarios
- **Second-Order Thinking** — Competitive responses are second-order effects of your moves
- **Thiel Monopoly Building** — The goal is to build a position where competitive response is irrelevant
