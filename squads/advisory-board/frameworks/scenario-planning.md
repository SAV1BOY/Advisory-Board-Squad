# Scenario Planning Framework

## Purpose

Scenario planning is a structured method for exploring multiple plausible futures rather than betting on a single forecast. Unlike prediction (which seeks the right answer), scenario planning maps the range of possibilities and prepares the organization to act decisively in each. Originally developed at Royal Dutch Shell in the 1970s — enabling them to navigate the oil crisis better than any competitor — this framework helps leaders make robust decisions that perform well across multiple futures rather than optimal decisions that only work if one specific future materializes.

## When to Use

- During annual or quarterly strategic planning
- When facing high uncertainty in markets, regulation, technology, or competition
- Before major capital allocation or irreversible commitments
- When the leadership team has a single, implicit model of the future
- During fundraising to stress-test milestones against different macro conditions
- When board members and founders have divergent assumptions about the future
- For crisis preparedness and business continuity planning

## Core Model

### The Scenario Matrix
Scenarios are built on the intersection of two key uncertainties — the two factors that matter most and are hardest to predict. This creates a 2x2 matrix with four distinct, plausible futures.

```
                    Uncertainty A: High
                         |
        Scenario 2       |       Scenario 1
        "Storm"          |       "Boom"
                         |
 Uncertainty B: ————————————————————— Uncertainty B:
    Low                  |              High
                         |
        Scenario 3       |       Scenario 4
        "Stagnation"     |       "Disruption"
                         |
                    Uncertainty A: Low
```

### Principles of Good Scenarios
1. **Plausible, not probable.** Each scenario should be believable, even if unlikely.
2. **Distinct.** Scenarios should represent genuinely different futures, not variations on a theme.
3. **Relevant.** They should address the decision or strategy you are trying to make robust.
4. **Challenging.** At least one scenario should feel uncomfortable. If all scenarios are rosy, you are not stress-testing.
5. **Named.** Give each scenario a memorable name. Names make scenarios sticky and referenceable.

### Three Layers of Scenario Analysis
- **Environment scenarios**: What could happen in the world around us?
- **Industry scenarios**: What could happen in our specific market?
- **Strategy scenarios**: What could we do, and what would be the result in each environment?

## Steps

1. **Define the focal question.** What decision or strategy are you trying to make robust? Be specific. "Should we expand into Europe in 2026?" is better than "What does the future look like?"

2. **Identify driving forces.** Brainstorm all factors that could influence the outcome: technology, regulation, competition, customer behavior, macro-economy, team dynamics, funding environment. List 15-20 forces.

3. **Rank by impact and uncertainty.** Map each force on a 2x2 of impact (high/low) and uncertainty (high/low). High-impact, high-uncertainty forces are your scenario axes.

4. **Select two critical uncertainties.** Choose the two forces that are highest impact and highest uncertainty. These form the axes of your scenario matrix.

5. **Build four scenarios.** For each quadrant of the matrix, construct a narrative:
   - Give it a name
   - Describe what the world looks like in this scenario
   - Explain how it came to be (the path from today)
   - Identify what signals would indicate this scenario is emerging

6. **Test your strategy against each scenario.** For each scenario, ask:
   - Does our current strategy work here?
   - What would we do differently?
   - What capabilities do we need?
   - What are the risks and opportunities?

7. **Identify robust moves.** Find actions that perform well across multiple scenarios (or all four). These are your "no-regret" moves. Prioritize them.

8. **Identify contingent moves.** Find actions that are right for specific scenarios. Define the trigger signals that would activate each contingent move.

9. **Build an early warning system.** For each scenario, define 3-5 leading indicators. Monitor them regularly. When indicators cluster toward one scenario, activate the contingent moves for that scenario.

10. **Review and refresh scenarios quarterly.** The value of scenarios is not the documents but the organizational muscle of thinking in ranges.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Insist that management present strategies that have been tested against multiple scenarios, not just the base case.
- Use scenarios to challenge implicit assumptions. "This plan works if the market grows 30% — what if it grows 10%? What if it shrinks?"
- Scenarios are an excellent tool for aligning board members who have different views of the future. The framework makes disagreement productive.

### For Fundraising
- Build three financial scenarios: conservative, base, and aggressive. Map each to specific external conditions.
- Raise enough capital to survive the conservative scenario while capturing the upside in the base and aggressive scenarios.
- Use scenarios to communicate sophistication to investors. A founder who presents one plan looks naive; a founder who presents three scenarios and a robust strategy looks prepared.

### For Product Strategy
- Define product roadmap priorities by identifying features that matter in all scenarios (build first) vs. features that matter in specific scenarios (build contingently).
- Use scenarios to evaluate build-vs-buy-vs-partner decisions. The optimal choice often depends on which future materializes.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Building scenarios around a single uncertainty.** This produces optimistic/pessimistic variants, not true scenarios. Use two independent uncertainties for genuine diversity.

2. **Making scenarios too extreme.** Scenarios should be plausible, not dramatic. "AI replaces all workers" is not a useful scenario. "AI automates 30% of mid-level tasks within 3 years" is useful.

3. **Treating the base case as the plan.** Organizations default to the middle scenario. The value of scenario planning is in the extremes — that is where you are least prepared.

4. **One-and-done scenario exercises.** A single offsite that produces scenarios is theater. Ongoing monitoring of scenario indicators is strategy.

5. **Too many scenarios.** Four is the sweet spot. More than four creates analysis paralysis. Fewer than three doesn't provide enough contrast.

6. **Ignoring second-order effects.** In each scenario, trace the downstream implications. "Interest rates rise" doesn't just affect your cost of capital — it affects your customers' willingness to spend, your competitors' ability to fund operations, and the talent market.

7. **Failing to assign early warning indicators.** Scenarios without monitoring are just stories. Each scenario needs measurable signals that indicate it is becoming reality.

## Related Frameworks

- **Base Rates and Reference Class** — Use base rates to calibrate scenario probabilities
- **Second-Order Thinking** — Trace downstream effects within each scenario
- **Expected Value and Asymmetry** — Assign probabilities and values to scenarios for EV analysis
- **Pre-Mortem Framework** — Run pre-mortems within each scenario to find scenario-specific risks
- **Inversion Framework** — Invert scenarios: "What would make each scenario the worst possible outcome?"
- **Strategy Choice Cascade** — Test your cascade against each scenario
