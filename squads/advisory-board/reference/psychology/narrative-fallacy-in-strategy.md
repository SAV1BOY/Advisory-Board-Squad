# Narrative Fallacy in Strategy

## Overview
Humans are compulsive story-tellers. We create coherent narratives to explain the past, making random events seem inevitable and causal. In strategy, this leads to false pattern recognition, overconfident extrapolation, and the dangerous belief that success was caused by our actions rather than by luck, timing, or favorable conditions.

## How Narrative Fallacy Distorts Strategy

### Post-Hoc Strategy Narratives
- "We succeeded because of our strategic vision" — But was it really the strategy, or market timing, competitor mistakes, or luck?
- **Danger:** The narrative becomes the basis for future strategy, even when the causal mechanism is wrong

### Competitor Narratives
- "Competitor X failed because of poor execution" — But maybe they made the same strategic choices and just had worse luck
- **Danger:** We learn the wrong lesson and apply it to our own strategy

### Founder Mythology
- "The founder had a vision from day one" — But the reality was probably a series of pivots, accidents, and adaptations
- **Danger:** New founders try to follow the mythology instead of staying responsive to reality

### Pattern Matching Without Causation
- "The last three companies that entered this market failed, so we shouldn't enter" — But the three failures may have different causes
- **Danger:** We treat correlated events as if they share a single cause

## Detection

### Signs of Narrative Fallacy in Board Discussions
- The explanation for past events is too neat and too simple
- The story has a clear hero, villain, and lesson—like a movie script
- Alternative explanations are dismissed rather than explored
- The narrative conveniently supports the presenter's preferred action

### Questions That Break Narratives
- "What role did luck or timing play?"
- "Could there be a completely different explanation for the same outcome?"
- "What would the narrative be if the outcome had been different?"
- "Are we confusing 'what happened after' with 'what happened because of'?"

## Countermeasures

1. **Multiple narrative generation** — For any past event, generate at least three plausible explanations before settling on one
2. **Base rate analysis** — Before accepting a narrative, check what base rates suggest about the probability of the outcome
3. **Controlled comparison** — Find cases where the same actions led to different outcomes to test the narrative's causal claims
4. **Pre-registration** — Before a strategy is executed, document what you expect to happen and why. Compare to what actually happens.
5. **Probabilistic thinking** — Replace "X caused Y" with "X made Y more likely by Z%"

## Board Application
- When management presents a strategic narrative, apply the detection questions
- Require strategy presentations to include the role of luck and external factors
- Use probabilistic language in board discussions: "likely," "more probable," "one factor among several"
- Maintain a decision journal that enables fair comparison of predictions to outcomes
