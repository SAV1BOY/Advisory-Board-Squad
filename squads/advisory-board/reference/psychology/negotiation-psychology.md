# Negotiation Psychology

## Overview
The psychological dynamics underlying every negotiation, from M&A deals to executive compensation to board-level conflict resolution. Understanding these dynamics allows board members to negotiate more effectively and recognize when psychological tactics are being used against them.

## Core Psychological Principles in Negotiation

### Loss Aversion (Kahneman)
- People feel losses ~2x more intensely than equivalent gains
- **Application:** Frame proposals in terms of what the other side will lose by NOT agreeing, rather than what they'll gain by agreeing
- **Defense:** Notice when someone is framing your options as losses. Reframe in gain terms.

### Anchoring Effect
- The first number mentioned in a negotiation disproportionately influences the outcome
- **Application:** Make the first offer when you have good information about the range. Set an ambitious anchor.
- **Defense:** Ignore their anchor. Present your own independent analysis. Counter-anchor aggressively.

### Reciprocity
- People feel obligated to return favors and concessions
- **Application:** Make small concessions early to create obligation. Give something inexpensive to you but valuable to them.
- **Defense:** Accept gifts and concessions graciously but evaluate proposals on merit, not obligation.

### Commitment and Consistency
- Once people commit publicly, they feel pressure to behave consistently
- **Application:** Get small agreements early. "Do you agree that speed is important here?" sets up later requests.
- **Defense:** Recognize when small yeses are building toward a large commitment you haven't independently evaluated.

### Social Proof
- In uncertainty, people look to what others have done
- **Application:** "Most companies in your position have accepted these terms" or "Standard market terms are..."
- **Defense:** What's standard for others may not be right for you. Evaluate terms independently.

### Scarcity and Urgency
- Things seem more valuable when scarce or time-limited
- **Application:** Create genuine (not artificial) scarcity: limited allocation, competing offers, deadline-driven decisions
- **Defense:** Artificial urgency is a red flag. If the deal is good today, it's usually good next week.

## Emotional Dynamics

### The Emotional Sequence of Negotiation
1. **Anxiety** (before and early) — Managed through preparation and BATNA clarity
2. **Excitement** (when progress is made) — Dangerous: excitement leads to concessions
3. **Frustration** (when stuck) — The most common negotiation-killer. Requires patience.
4. **Relief** (at agreement) — Also dangerous: the urge to close can lead to last-minute concessions

### Managing Your Own Emotions
- Prepare extensively: anxiety decreases with preparation
- Take breaks when frustrated: "Let's pause and reconvene tomorrow"
- Separate the person from the problem: anger at the person pollutes substantive judgment
- Label your own emotions (Voss technique): awareness reduces their influence

## Board Application
- Before any negotiation, brief the board on the psychological dynamics likely to be at play
- Assign different board members to watch for different psychological tactics during negotiations
- Always define the BATNA before entering negotiations—it's the foundation of psychological strength
- Debrief after major negotiations to identify which psychological dynamics influenced the outcome
