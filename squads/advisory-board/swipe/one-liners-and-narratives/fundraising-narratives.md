# Fundraising Narrative Examples — Swipe File

## Purpose
The best fundraising narratives do not describe a company — they describe a future and position the investment as participation in an inevitable transformation. This swipe file contains narrative structures, framing techniques, and example language distilled from successful raises across stages.

## Narrative Structures

### The "Inevitable Future" Narrative
Used when the market shift is clear but most incumbents are too slow to respond.

- **Structure:** The world is changing in X way. Incumbents cannot adapt because of Y structural constraint. We are building the native solution for the new world.
- **Example:** "Enterprise software was built for a world where employees stayed at one company for a decade and used one tool per job. That world is gone. The modern workforce uses 15+ tools, changes roles every 2-3 years, and expects consumer-grade UX. We are building the integration layer that makes this fragmented stack work as one system."
- **Why it works:** Positions the investment as inevitable, not speculative. Reduces perceived risk.
- **Best for:** Series A and B raises where the market thesis is the core of the pitch. Infrastructure companies, platform plays, category creators.

### The "Inflection Point" Narrative
Used when traction data shows a step-change in growth or engagement.

- **Structure:** We have been building for X months/years. In the last Y period, something changed. Here is the data. Here is why it happened. Here is what it means.
- **Example:** "For 18 months, we grew 8% month-over-month. Solid, but not spectacular. Then in Q3, we launched self-serve onboarding and growth accelerated to 22% MoM. Three things changed: activation time dropped from 3 days to 20 minutes, viral coefficient went from 0.3 to 1.1, and enterprise inbound doubled. We are raising to pour fuel on this inflection."
- **Why it works:** Shows a before/after that is verifiable. Investors can see the slope change.
- **Best for:** Any stage where you can point to a clear inflection in the data.

### The "Secret" Narrative
Used when you have a proprietary insight or advantage that competitors do not.

- **Structure:** Everyone in this market believes X. We discovered that Y is actually true. This insight gives us Z advantage.
- **Example:** "Every competitor in our space sells to the CIO. We discovered that the actual buying decision happens with the VP of Engineering, who has a separate budget and faster procurement cycle. This insight gives us a 3x shorter sales cycle and 60% lower CAC than anyone else in the category."
- **Why it works:** Creates intellectual excitement. Positions the company as seeing what others miss.
- **Best for:** Seed and Series A, especially when competing against better-funded players.

### The "Wedge" Narrative
Used when a small, focused product opens a door to a much larger opportunity.

- **Structure:** We started by solving a specific, painful problem for a specific segment. This gave us a foothold. From here we expand to adjacent products and eventually the platform vision.
- **Example:** "We started by automating expense reports for 50-person startups. That gave us 400 customers who trust us with their financial data. Now we are expanding into bill pay, corporate cards, and spend management. Our wedge gave us distribution and trust; our platform vision gives us scale."
- **Why it works:** Demonstrates traction and a credible expansion path. Balances ambition with evidence.
- **Best for:** SaaS companies, vertical platforms, land-and-expand models.

### The "Compounding Advantage" Narrative
Used when your business has a flywheel or network effect that strengthens with scale.

- **Structure:** Every unit of growth makes the next unit easier/cheaper/faster. Here is the mechanism. Here is the evidence it is working.
- **Example:** "Every merchant on our platform adds data to our fraud model. Better fraud detection attracts more merchants. More merchants generate more data. Our false positive rate is now 40% lower than the industry average, and the gap widens with every transaction. Competitors cannot replicate this with engineering alone — they need the transaction volume we already have."
- **Why it works:** Shows that competitive advantage compounds, making the business harder to displace over time.
- **Best for:** Series B and beyond, when you can demonstrate the flywheel with data.

### The "Capital Efficiency" Narrative
Used to differentiate from competitors who burn cash aggressively.

- **Structure:** We have achieved X with Y amount of capital. Our efficiency metrics are in the top percentile. More capital at this efficiency produces outsized returns.
- **Example:** "We reached $5M ARR on $2M of total funding. Our burn multiple is 0.8x — for every dollar we burn, we generate $1.25 in new ARR. The median Series B company at our stage has burned $15M to reach the same revenue. We are raising $20M not because we need it to survive, but because deploying capital at this efficiency is a compounding advantage."
- **Why it works:** Especially powerful in down markets when capital efficiency is valued over growth-at-all-costs.

## One-Line Pitches by Category

### Marketplace
- "We connect [supply] with [demand] in a $[X]B market that still runs on phone calls and spreadsheets."

### SaaS
- "We help [customer type] do [thing] [X]% faster than [current solution], saving [Y] per month."

### Fintech
- "We are rebuilding [financial infrastructure] for [new economy / underserved segment]."

### Platform
- "We are building the operating system for [industry]. Every [company type] needs what we provide."

## Narrative Anti-Patterns

| Anti-Pattern | Why It Fails |
|-------------|-------------|
| "We are the Uber of X" | Signals you cannot articulate your own value proposition |
| "Our TAM is $100B" | Every startup claims a massive TAM. It signals nothing. |
| "We have no competitors" | Means you have not done the research or the market does not exist |
| "We just need 1% of the market" | The 1% fallacy — getting 1% of a large market is not a strategy |
| Leading with the team before the problem | Investors fund solutions to problems, not resumes |
| Burying the ask | State what you are raising and why in the first 5 minutes |

## Structuring the Full Fundraising Narrative

1. **Opening hook** (30 seconds): The single most compelling fact about your business
2. **Problem** (2 minutes): Why this matters and why now
3. **Insight** (1 minute): What you understand that others do not
4. **Solution** (2 minutes): What you have built and why it works
5. **Traction** (2 minutes): Revenue, growth, engagement, retention
6. **Business model** (1 minute): How you make money and why the economics work
7. **Market** (1 minute): Size grounded in bottom-up analysis
8. **Team** (1 minute): Why this team can execute this plan
9. **Ask** (30 seconds): Amount, use of funds, milestones it enables

## Board Application
- Help founders craft their fundraising narrative before they enter the market
- Test the narrative with the board: does it resonate? Is it credible? Is it differentiated?
- Ensure the narrative is consistent with the company's actual strategy and metrics
- The narrative should evolve as the company matures — do not stay frozen at the seed pitch
