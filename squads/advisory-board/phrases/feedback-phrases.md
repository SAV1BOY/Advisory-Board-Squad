# Feedback Phrases

Curated phrases for delivering and receiving feedback in board contexts — positive reinforcement, constructive direction, and growth-oriented coaching.

## Positive Reinforcement

1. **"The way you structured that memo — leading with the decision, keeping it to two pages — is exactly the standard we want. Thank you."**
   Use to reinforce specific, replicable behavior. Name what was good so it gets repeated.

2. **"Your willingness to challenge the consensus in that discussion improved the decision. That takes courage."**
   Use to reinforce dissent behavior. When dissent is praised, it becomes safer for everyone.

3. **"I noticed you invited [name] into the conversation when they were quiet. That kind of facilitation makes the board stronger."**
   Use to reinforce inclusive behavior. Call it out so others model it.

4. **"The data you brought to support your position was compelling. Evidence-based arguments like that raise the quality of our discussions."**
   Use to reinforce rigorous preparation. Preparation is often invisible work that deserves recognition.

5. **"You received that feedback with grace and asked good follow-up questions. That is the standard for how we handle constructive input here."**
   Use to reinforce feedback-receiving behavior. The way someone receives feedback influences whether others will give it.

## Constructive Direction

6. **"I want to help you improve [specific thing]. Here is what I observed: [behavior]. The impact was [impact]. Here is what I'd suggest: [specific alternative]."**
   Use the SBI-A framework for clear, actionable feedback. Always pair the observation with a suggestion.

7. **"Your analysis was thorough, but I lost the thread at [specific point]. Can we work through the logic chain together?"**
   Use to improve communication quality without dismissing the work. Shows respect for the effort while addressing the gap.

8. **"I noticed that in the last two meetings, the pre-reads arrived after the deadline. This puts the board in a difficult position. What's blocking timely delivery?"**
   Use to address a pattern, not a single instance. Two data points make it a pattern worth discussing.

9. **"The recommendation was clear, but it didn't address the downside scenario. For the board to decide, we need to see the risk alongside the opportunity."**
   Use to improve the completeness of analysis without rejecting it. Frames the gap as a requirement, not a criticism.

10. **"You spoke for about 60 % of the meeting. I want to hear your views, but I also need space for other voices. Can we work on that balance?"**
    Use to address over-participation directly but constructively. Quantifying it makes it objective.

## Coaching and Development

11. **"You are strongest when you [specific behavior]. I'd like to see you apply that same approach to [area where it's missing]."**
    Use to leverage an existing strength as a bridge to a growth area. More motivating than focusing solely on the gap.

12. **"I think you have more to contribute on [topic] than you're currently offering. What would make it easier for you to speak up?"**
    Use to encourage a quiet but capable advisor. Frames participation as a contribution the board needs, not a personal deficit.

13. **"The decision you made on [topic] didn't produce the outcome we expected. What's your assessment of what happened, and what would you do differently?"**
    Use for reflective coaching after a poor outcome. Leads with the person's own assessment rather than imposing one.

14. **"I'd like to give you a stretch opportunity: lead the discussion on [topic] at the next meeting. I'll support you in preparation."**
    Use to develop an advisor's capabilities. Stretch assignments with support are more effective than feedback alone.

15. **"Your instincts are strong but your data game needs work. For the next pre-read, I'd like to see every claim backed by a specific number or example."**
    Use when the advisor relies on intuition over evidence. Specific, actionable, and framed as development.

## Receiving Feedback

16. **"Thank you for telling me that. I want to sit with it before I respond. Can I come back to you tomorrow?"**
    Use when you need time to process. Buying time is better than reacting defensively.

17. **"Can you give me a specific example? I want to make sure I'm addressing the right thing."**
    Use to deepen vague feedback into actionable specifics. Most feedback becomes more useful with an example.

18. **"I hear you, and I want to understand: is this about [specific instance], or are you seeing a broader pattern?"**
    Use to assess scope. Single-instance feedback is different from pattern feedback.

19. **"That's hard to hear, and I appreciate your directness. Here's what I'm going to do about it."**
    Use to demonstrate that feedback leads to action. Closing the loop builds trust in the feedback process.

20. **"I see it differently, but I want to understand your perspective before I share mine."**
    Use when you disagree with the feedback. Curiosity before defense.

## Board-to-CEO Feedback

21. **"The board is consistently impressed by [specific strength]. The area where we think the biggest growth opportunity exists is [specific area]. Here is a concrete example."**
    Use for balanced annual CEO feedback. Lead with genuine strength, then focus the development area with evidence.

22. **"I've heard this concern from multiple advisors independently, which tells me it's a pattern worth addressing."**
    Use to convey multi-source feedback. Triangulated feedback is harder to dismiss.

23. **"This isn't about changing who you are. It's about adding one capability that would multiply your effectiveness."**
    Use to frame development as growth, not remediation. Identity-safe framing produces better uptake.

24. **"How can the board better support you on this? We're not just pointing out the gap — we want to help you close it."**
    Use to convert feedback into a collaborative partnership. The board's role is not just to evaluate but to develop.
