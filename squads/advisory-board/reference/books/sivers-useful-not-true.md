# Useful Not True — Derek Sivers

## Summary

Sivers challenges the idea that beliefs need to be true to be valuable. Instead, he argues beliefs should be evaluated by their utility—do they help you act effectively in the world? The book encourages treating beliefs as tools rather than identities: pick them up when useful, put them down when not. This philosophical framework has profound implications for leadership and board governance, where deeply held assumptions about markets, competitors, and strategy often go unexamined because they feel true, even when they're no longer useful.

## Key Principles

- **Beliefs are tools, not identities** — When you identify with a belief, challenging it feels like an attack on your personhood. When you treat it as a tool, you can swap it freely.
- **Useful is the only test that matters** — A belief doesn't need to be objectively true to serve you. "I can handle anything" may not be literally true, but it's a more useful operating belief than "everything is going to go wrong."
- **Multiple contradictory beliefs can coexist** — Hold "this could be the biggest opportunity ever" and "this could fail completely" simultaneously. The tension is productive.
- **Question the source, not just the claim** — Before adopting a belief, ask: who benefits from me believing this? Advisors, investors, and competitors all have incentive-shaped perspectives.
- **Reframe, don't refute** — When a belief isn't serving you, don't argue with it. Simply adopt a more useful one. The old belief isn't wrong—it's just not helping right now.
- **All advice is autobiographical** — Including this book. People give advice that would have helped them in their situation. Your situation is different.

## Frameworks Extracted

1. **The Utility Test** — For any belief or assumption: (1) Is this belief helping me make better decisions? (2) Is it motivating useful action? (3) Would a different belief serve me better in this situation? If the answer to #3 is yes, switch.
2. **The Belief Audit** — List core beliefs about your business, market, and capabilities. For each: (1) Where did this belief come from? (2) Is it still useful? (3) What would I do differently if I believed the opposite? This exercise surfaces hidden assumptions.
3. **The Reframe Menu** — For any situation, generate three alternative frames. Example: "We lost the deal" → (a) "We learned what doesn't resonate," (b) "We freed up resources for better opportunities," (c) "We now know exactly what the competitor offers." Choose the frame that produces the most useful action.
4. **The Source Audit** — For any advice or information: (1) Who is telling me this? (2) What's their incentive? (3) What's their experience base? (4) How different is their context from mine?

## Application to Board Decisions

- **Assumption surfacing** — Before any major strategic decision, run a Belief Audit on the assumptions underlying the proposal. "Our market is growing" and "our moat is defensible" are beliefs, not facts. Test their utility.
- **Reframing setbacks** — When the company faces a failure, use the Reframe Menu to generate multiple interpretations before settling on a narrative. The first story the board tells itself about a failure often isn't the most useful one.
- **Advisor skepticism** — Apply the Source Audit to all external advice—from consultants, bankers, and fellow board members. Everyone's advice is shaped by their experience and incentives.
- **Strategic flexibility** — Encourage the leadership team to hold contradictory scenarios simultaneously. "We should invest heavily in growth" and "we should preserve cash for a downturn" are not mutually exclusive beliefs—they can inform a balanced strategy.
- **Cultural dogma** — Identify beliefs that have calcified into organizational dogma. "We're a premium brand" or "we don't compete on price" may have been useful once but may be limiting now. Test utility, not truth.
