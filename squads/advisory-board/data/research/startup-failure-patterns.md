# Common Startup Failure Patterns

## Key Findings

1. **Product-market fit failure remains the leading cause of startup death, but it is often misdiagnosed.** 38% of startups cite "no market need" as their primary failure reason, but deeper analysis reveals that most of these companies had a market need — they failed to find the right product for the need, the right positioning, or the right customer segment. The distinction matters because it changes the intervention.
2. **Cash management failures are the most preventable cause of death.** 29% of failures cite "ran out of cash" as the primary cause. In nearly all cases, the cash crisis was visible 6-12 months before death but was not acted on due to optimism bias, delayed fundraising, or refusal to cut costs. Board-level cash monitoring prevents this failure mode.
3. **Team and co-founder conflict is underreported.** Co-founder conflict is cited as the primary cause in only 13% of postmortems, but is a contributing factor in an estimated 40-50% of failures. Boards often lack visibility into co-founder dynamics until the relationship has irreparably deteriorated.
4. **Premature scaling is the fastest way to die.** Startups that scale sales and marketing before achieving product-market fit burn cash 3-5x faster than those that wait. The signal that scaling is premature: high logo churn (>5% monthly), low NPS (<30), and sales cycles requiring heavy customization.
5. **Ignoring competition is more dangerous than reacting to it.** Startups that fail due to competitive dynamics typically do not lose in a head-to-head feature war — they are outflanked by a competitor with a better business model, distribution advantage, or ecosystem strategy that the startup never monitored.
6. **Failure to adapt business model kills more startups than failure to build product.** 70% of successful startups pivoted at least once. Companies that rigidly pursued their original business model without evidence-based adaptation had 4x higher failure rates.

## Implications for Board

- **PMF verification.** The board should not accept management's claim of product-market fit at face value. Require evidence: retention cohorts, organic growth rate, NPS >40, willingness to pay without discounts, and inbound demand exceeding capacity.
- **Cash monitoring discipline.** Implement a standing board agenda item: cash balance, monthly burn rate, and runway in months. Trigger a strategic discussion at 12 months runway. At 6 months, treat it as a crisis. See burn-rate-and-runway.md.
- **Co-founder health check.** The board should conduct a private, individual check-in with each co-founder at least semi-annually. Ask directly about the co-founder relationship. Do not wait for conflict to surface publicly.
- **Premature scaling detection.** Before approving a significant increase in sales or marketing spend, the board should verify that the company has achieved repeatable sales (consistent close rates, declining sales cycle length, customer expansion without heavy services involvement).
- **Competitive monitoring.** The board should ensure competitive intelligence is a standing quarterly agenda item. Use competitive-intelligence-template.md to structure the review. Focus on business model and distribution threats, not just product features.
- **Adaptability assessment.** Annually, the board should evaluate whether the company is adapting based on evidence or persisting based on conviction. Ask: "What have we learned in the last 12 months that changed our strategy?"
