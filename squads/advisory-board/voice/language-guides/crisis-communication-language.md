# Language Guide: Crisis Communication Language

## Purpose

This guide provides the language framework for communicating during a crisis — to employees, customers, investors, regulators, media, and the public. Crisis communication has different rules than normal communication: every word carries more weight, and silence is interpreted as guilt or incompetence.

## Core Principles

1. **Speed over perfection.** A timely, honest, incomplete statement is better than a late, polished, complete one.
2. **Facts, not speculation.** State what you know. State what you don't know. Never fill gaps with guesses.
3. **Empathy first.** The first thing affected parties want to hear is that you understand and care about their situation.
4. **Accountability, not blame.** Take responsibility for what you can control. Do not deflect or point fingers.
5. **Commit to follow-up.** If you can't answer now, say when you will answer. Then honor that commitment.

## Opening Statements

### For customers
- "We want you to know what happened, what we're doing about it, and what it means for you."
- "On [date], we discovered [what happened]. We're sharing this with you as quickly as we can because you deserve to know."
- "Your [data/service/experience] matters to us, and we take this situation seriously."

### For employees
- "I want to be direct with you about what's happening, because you deserve honesty from your leadership."
- "Here is what we know, what we don't know, and what we're doing. I'll update you again at [specific time]."
- "I know this creates uncertainty, and I know uncertainty is uncomfortable. Here's what I can tell you right now."

### For investors
- "We are writing to inform you of a material event and our response."
- "We identified [issue] on [date]. Here are the facts as we understand them, the actions we have taken, and our assessment of impact."

### For regulators
- "We are reporting [event] in accordance with [regulation/requirement]. Here are the facts, the timeline, and our remediation plan."

### For media/public
- "We are aware of [situation]. Here is what we know at this time."
- "We are actively [investigating/responding/remediating]. We will provide updates as we learn more."

## Fact Communication

### What to say
- "Here is what we know: [facts, with timestamps]."
- "Here is what we don't yet know: [specific gaps]."
- "Here is what we're doing: [specific actions being taken]."
- "Here is when we'll update you next: [specific date/time]."

### How to handle uncertainty
- "We are still investigating and do not have a complete picture. We will share more as we confirm the facts."
- "Preliminary information suggests [X], but we want to verify before making definitive statements."
- "We have been asked about [topic]. We do not have confirmed information on this yet. When we do, we will share it."
- "It would be irresponsible to speculate at this point. We are focused on getting the facts right."

## Empathy Language

### Acknowledging impact
- "We understand that this situation affects [who] and we take that impact seriously."
- "We know this causes [concern/disruption/anxiety], and that is not acceptable to us."
- "The people affected by this are our priority. Here is what we are doing to support them."
- "We are sorry. Not because it's what you say in these situations, but because people we care about are affected."

### When people are angry
- "Your frustration is valid. We share it."
- "We understand why you feel that way. We would feel the same way in your position."
- "We hear you, and we owe you better than this."

### Avoid
- "We take this very seriously" (overused to the point of meaninglessness — show seriousness through actions).
- "There is no evidence of..." (sounds like lawyering — state what you know, not what you haven't found).
- "We apologize for any inconvenience" (minimizes the impact — name the actual impact).
- "Isolated incident" (only say this if you have evidence; otherwise it sounds defensive).

## Accountability Language

### Taking responsibility
- "This happened on our watch, and we are responsible for fixing it."
- "We made a mistake. Specifically: [what went wrong]. Here is what we are doing to correct it and prevent recurrence."
- "Our systems did not perform as they should have. We are addressing the root cause, not just the symptom."

### Avoid
- Passive voice that hides agency: "Mistakes were made" → "We made a mistake."
- Blame-shifting: "A third-party vendor caused..." → "We are responsible for our systems, including vendor management."
- Minimizing: "Only a small number of users were affected" → State the actual number and acknowledge each one matters.

## Follow-Up Communication

### Update structure
- "This is our [number] update on [situation]. Here is what has changed since our last communication on [date]."
- "New information: [facts]."
- "Actions taken since last update: [actions]."
- "Remaining open questions: [questions]."
- "Next update: [date/time]."

### Closing the crisis
- "We believe [situation] has been resolved. Here is a summary of what happened, what we did, and what we have changed to prevent recurrence."
- "We want to thank [affected parties] for their patience and trust during a difficult period."
- "The changes we have implemented are: [specific changes]. We will monitor these for [timeframe] to confirm effectiveness."

## Channel-Specific Guidance

| Channel | Tone | Length | Key Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customer email | Empathetic, clear | 3-5 paragraphs | Lead with impact on them, not on you |
| Employee all-hands | Direct, honest, human | 10-15 minutes + Q&A | Be present for questions; don't hide behind text |
| Investor letter | Factual, structured | 1-2 pages | Lead with material facts; follow with assessment |
| Regulatory filing | Precise, compliant | Per requirements | Legal review mandatory; no embellishment |
| Media statement | Brief, factual | 3-5 sentences | Only state confirmed facts; decline to speculate |
| Social media | Brief, empathetic | 2-3 sentences | Direct people to detailed statement; avoid threads |

## Review Checklist

Before releasing any crisis communication:

- [ ] Does it state facts clearly, separated from interpretation?
- [ ] Does it acknowledge impact on affected parties?
- [ ] Does it avoid speculation?
- [ ] Does it include specific actions being taken?
- [ ] Does it commit to a follow-up date?
- [ ] Has legal counsel reviewed it?
- [ ] Has the Board-Chair approved it?
- [ ] Is it written in active voice with clear accountability?
- [ ] Would you be comfortable if this appeared on the front page tomorrow?
- [ ] Does it pass the "affected person" test — would someone directly impacted feel respected?
