# Moat Types Taxonomy

## Types

1. **Network Effects** — The product or service becomes more valuable as more people use it.
2. **Switching Costs** — Customers face significant cost, effort, or risk in moving to an alternative.
3. **Cost Advantages** — The company can produce at a structurally lower cost than competitors.
4. **Intangible Assets** — Patents, brands, regulatory licenses, or proprietary data that competitors cannot legally replicate.
5. **Efficient Scale** — The market is only large enough to profitably support a limited number of competitors.
6. **Data Moat** — Proprietary data that improves the product and is difficult to replicate independently.
7. **Ecosystem Lock-in** — Customers are embedded in an ecosystem of complementary products and integrations.
8. **Distribution Advantage** — Structural access to customers that competitors cannot easily replicate.
9. **Talent Density** — Concentration of exceptional talent that self-reinforces through culture and reputation.
10. **Mission Moat** — A purpose-driven brand and culture that attracts customers, talent, and partners who share the mission.

## Definitions

| Type | Definition | Strength Mechanism | Decay Risk |
|------|-----------|-------------------|------------|
| Network Effects | Each additional user increases value for all existing users. Can be direct (user-to-user) or indirect (user-to-creator). | Value compounds exponentially with user growth | Multi-homing (users join competing networks simultaneously); network collapse if critical mass is lost |
| Switching Costs | Customers have invested time, data, integrations, training, or workflow habits that make switching painful. | Pain of switching exceeds dissatisfaction with current provider | Technology standardization that reduces migration cost; competitors offering migration tools |
| Cost Advantages | Structural cost position that does not depend on management skill — scale economies, process IP, geographic advantage, or resource access. | Price competition destroys less efficient competitors | Competitors achieving similar scale; technology disruption changing cost structure |
| Intangible Assets | Legal or perceptual barriers — patents (time-limited), brand (trust-based), regulatory approvals (barrier-based), trade secrets. | Legal or market enforcement prevents replication | Patent expiration; brand erosion through poor execution; regulatory change |
| Efficient Scale | Total addressable market is small enough that additional competitors would make the market unprofitable for all. | New entrants face negative unit economics | Market expansion that creates room for new entrants |
| Data Moat | Proprietary dataset that feeds product quality, and product usage generates more data. Self-reinforcing loop. | Competitors cannot shortcut years of data accumulation | Open-source datasets; synthetic data; regulatory data-sharing requirements |
| Ecosystem Lock-in | Product is the hub of an ecosystem — third-party integrations, extensions, and complementary products create collective switching costs. | Switching requires replacing not just the product but the entire ecosystem | Platform risk if ecosystem owner changes terms; open standards reducing ecosystem dependency |
| Distribution Advantage | Structural access to customers — owned channels, partnerships, regulatory position, or embedded relationships. | Competitors must build distribution from scratch, which takes time and capital | Channel disruption; disintermediation; partner defection |
| Talent Density | The best people in the field want to work here, and their presence attracts more top talent. | A-players attract A-players; mediocre competitors cannot compete for talent | Culture degradation; loss of key leaders; compensation arms race |
| Mission Moat | Customers buy because they believe in what the company stands for, not just what it sells. Talent joins for purpose, not just pay. | Emotional and identity-based loyalty is harder to disrupt than rational loyalty | Mission drift; hypocrisy between stated and actual values; competitor with a more compelling mission |

## Examples

| Moat Type | Company Example | How It Works |
|-----------|----------------|-------------|
| Network Effects | Slack, LinkedIn | Each new team/user on Slack makes it more useful for existing teams. LinkedIn's value scales with professional connections. |
| Switching Costs | Salesforce, Workday | Years of customization, training, data, and workflow integration make switching a multi-year project. |
| Cost Advantages | Walmart, Amazon | Scale enables procurement leverage, logistics efficiency, and fixed-cost amortization that smaller competitors cannot match. |
| Intangible Assets | Coca-Cola (brand), Pfizer (patents) | Coca-Cola's brand commands premium pricing. Pfizer's patents provide temporary monopoly on approved drugs. |
| Efficient Scale | Waste management in a single metro area | Market supports 2-3 haulers; a fourth entrant would make the economics unworkable for all. |
| Data Moat | Google Search, Waze | Every search query improves Google's results. Every Waze driver improves traffic data for all users. |
| Ecosystem Lock-in | Apple, Shopify | Apple users are embedded in iCloud, AirDrop, AirPods, Apple Watch. Shopify merchants rely on thousands of apps and integrations. |
| Distribution Advantage | Berkshire Hathaway (insurance float), Bank branches | Berkshire uses insurance float as zero-cost capital. Bank branch networks provide customer access competitors must build. |
| Talent Density | Renaissance Technologies, early Google | RenTech attracts the best quants because the best quants already work there. Early Google attracted top engineers through engineering culture. |
| Mission Moat | Patagonia, Tesla | Patagonia's environmental mission creates customer loyalty that survives product parity. Tesla's mission attracted early adopters and talent. |

## When to Use Each

**During strategic planning:** Identify which moats the company currently possesses and score them using `lib/utilities/moat-strength-rubric.md`. Determine which moats to invest in.

**During competitive analysis:** Map competitor moats to understand where they are strong and where they are vulnerable.

**During M&A evaluation:** Assess whether the target's moats are real and whether they would be preserved post-acquisition.

**During fundraising:** Articulate moats to investors — sophisticated investors evaluate moat quality as a primary valuation driver.

**During product strategy:** Design product decisions to strengthen existing moats or build new ones. Every feature decision should be evaluated against: "Does this strengthen our moat?"

**Moat stacking principle:** The most durable businesses have 2-3 reinforcing moats. A single moat, no matter how strong, creates fragility. Prioritize investments that strengthen multiple moats simultaneously (e.g., a data moat that feeds network effects that create switching costs).
