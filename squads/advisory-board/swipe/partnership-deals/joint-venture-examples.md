# Joint Venture Structure Examples

## Overview

Joint ventures allow companies to combine complementary capabilities, share risk on large investments, and enter markets neither could access alone. These examples illustrate structural patterns, governance models, and common failure modes.

## Structure 1: 50/50 Strategic JV

### Example: Hulu (Original Structure)

**Partners:** NBC Universal, Fox, Disney (later joined)
**Purpose:** Create a streaming platform to compete with YouTube and emerging digital distributors.

**Structure:**
- Equal ownership among founding partners
- Each partner contributed content licenses and capital
- Independent management team with CEO reporting to JV board
- Board composed of representatives from each partner
- Content licensing agreements at arm's length terms

**What worked:**
- Combined content libraries created compelling consumer offering
- Shared development costs reduced individual partner risk
- Independent management prevented day-to-day partner interference

**What failed:**
- Conflicting strategic interests among partners (each also had competing streaming ambitions)
- Decision paralysis on pricing, international expansion, and original content investment
- Partners eventually pursued individual streaming strategies, leading to Disney's acquisition of full control

**Lesson:** 50/50 JVs work for defined, time-limited objectives. They fail when partners' strategic interests diverge over time.

## Structure 2: Majority/Minority JV for Market Entry

### Example: International Market Entry JV

**Structure:** Foreign company takes 51-70% ownership; local partner holds 30-49%.

**Typical terms:**
- Foreign partner contributes technology, brand, and capital
- Local partner contributes market knowledge, regulatory relationships, and distribution
- Management appointed by majority partner; key local roles filled by minority partner
- Put/call options allowing majority partner to buy out minority after 5-7 years
- Non-compete provisions preventing local partner from working with competitors

**Governance model:**
- Board of 5: 3 seats to majority partner, 2 to minority partner
- Reserved matters requiring unanimous consent: annual budget, key hires, related-party transactions, capital calls above threshold
- Deadlock resolution through escalation, mediation, then buy/sell provisions

**Advisory insight:** The put/call option is the most critical term. It determines whether this is a true partnership or a structured acquisition with a delayed closing.

## Structure 3: Capability-Based JV

### Example: Sony Ericsson (2001-2012)

**Partners:** Sony (consumer electronics and brand) + Ericsson (telecommunications technology)
**Purpose:** Combine mobile phone hardware expertise with consumer electronics design.

**Structure:**
- 50/50 ownership with independent management
- Each partner contributed relevant IP, patents, and engineering teams
- Separate P&L with its own manufacturing and distribution
- Clear IP licensing terms for technology contributed by each partner

**Lifecycle:**
- Early success in feature phone era with distinctive designs
- Struggled during smartphone transition as decision-making slowed
- Sony acquired Ericsson's stake in 2012 for $1.5B when strategic alignment broke down
- JV served its purpose during a specific technology era, then was appropriately dissolved

**Lesson:** Capability-based JVs should include sunset provisions that acknowledge the partnership may outlive its strategic rationale.

### Deadlock Resolution Ladder
1. Escalation to partner CEOs (14-day window)
2. Non-binding mediation (30-day window)
3. If unresolved: buy/sell mechanism triggered (one partner names a price; other partner chooses to buy or sell at that price)

## JV Failure Patterns

1. **Misaligned time horizons** — One partner wants a 3-year project; the other wants a permanent entity
2. **Governance without teeth** — Deadlock provisions that never actually resolve deadlocks
3. **Capability hoarding** — Partners withhold their best people and technology from the JV
4. **Transfer pricing disputes** — Arguments over the fair price of inputs from each partner
5. **Strategic drift** — Partners' core businesses evolve, making the JV less relevant to one or both

## Advisory Principles

- "Define the exit before you define the entrance. How this ends matters more than how it begins."
- "The best JVs have a clear, bounded mission. The worst try to be permanent companies."
- "If you cannot explain what each partner uniquely contributes, you do not need a JV — you need a vendor."
