# WeWork: Governance Failure

## Context

WeWork was founded in 2010 by Adam Neumann and Miguel McKelvey. By 2019, the company had grown to a $47 billion private valuation, raised over $12 billion, and was preparing for an IPO. The business model was simple: lease office space long-term, renovate it, and sublease it short-term at a premium. The company positioned itself as a "technology company" to justify tech-like valuations despite being fundamentally a real estate business with thin margins and massive lease obligations.

## Decision/Event

WeWork's governance failures were systemic and multi-layered:
- **Dual-class stock structure** gave Neumann 20 votes per share, effectively making him unaccountable to any other shareholder.
- **Board composition** was dominated by insiders and investors who had financial incentives to support the IPO rather than challenge the valuation or governance.
- **Self-dealing transactions:** Neumann owned buildings that WeWork leased, purchased the "We" trademark and licensed it back to the company for $5.9M, and took hundreds of millions in personal loans secured by company stock.
- **No financial discipline:** The company burned $1.37 for every $1 of revenue. Losses were growing faster than revenue.
- **S-1 filing (August 2019):** The IPO prospectus revealed the full extent of governance problems to the public for the first time, including the self-dealing, the losses, and Neumann's outsized control.

## What Happened

The S-1 filing triggered a rapid collapse. Public scrutiny revealed what the private board had failed to address. The IPO valuation was slashed from $47B to $10-15B, then the IPO was cancelled entirely. Neumann was forced out as CEO. SoftBank, the largest investor, took control and wrote down its investment by billions. Thousands of employees were laid off. WeWork eventually went public via SPAC at a $9B valuation in 2021 and filed for bankruptcy in 2023.

## Lessons

1. **Governance structures determine accountability.** Dual-class stock structures without sunset provisions create monarchies, not companies. The board could not hold Neumann accountable because the governance structure made him immune.

2. **The board failed its most basic function: oversight.** Every self-dealing transaction should have been flagged and either blocked or disclosed. The board's failure was not ignorance but acquiescence.

3. **Investor incentives can misalign with governance.** SoftBank and other investors were incentivized to support ever-higher valuations (which increased the value of their existing stakes). This created a structural conflict with the oversight function.

4. **Private company governance matters.** The assumption that "we will fix governance before the IPO" is the equivalent of planning to install brakes after driving downhill. The habits formed in private company governance persist.

5. **Revenue growth without unit economics is not a business — it is a bonfire.** Burning $1.37 for every $1 of revenue is not investment in growth; it is destruction of capital.

## What Board Would Have Caught

- Incentive alignment scan would have identified self-dealing as a critical governance risk at the first transaction.
- Board effectiveness rubric would have scored the board as dysfunctional, triggering composition changes.
- Capital allocation review would have flagged the negative unit economics as unsustainable.
- Risk register would have identified the dual-class structure as an existential governance risk.
- Culture health assessment would have identified the personality cult around Neumann as a cultural liability.
