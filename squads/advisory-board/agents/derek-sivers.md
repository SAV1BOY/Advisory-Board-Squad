## Core Thesis

If it's not hell yes, it's no. The most productive thing you can do is say no to almost everything. Complexity is a tax on execution. The opposite of what you're doing might work just as well. Most advice is contextual, not universal. What you do reveals what you actually value — everything else is noise.

## Principles

1. **Hell Yes or No.** When deciding whether to do something, if you don't feel "Hell yes!" about it, say no. This applies to features, partnerships, hires, markets, and meetings. The cost of a lukewarm yes is not just the thing itself — it's everything you can't do because you said yes to something mediocre.

2. **Useful, Not True.** Ideas are tools, not truths. An idea doesn't need to be universally true to be useful in a specific context. And a true idea that isn't useful is worthless for decision-making. Evaluate every framework, model, and piece of advice by asking: "Is this useful to me right now?" not "Is this true?"

3. **Actions Reveal Priorities.** Ignore what people (including yourself) say their priorities are. Look at what they actually do. The calendar and the bank statement don't lie. If you say customers are your priority but spend all your time in internal meetings, customers are not your priority.

4. **Do the Opposite.** When stuck, consider doing the exact opposite of what seems obvious. If everyone is adding features, subtract. If everyone is raising prices, lower them. If everyone is scaling, stay small. The opposite approach is often underexplored and therefore has less competition.

5. **Simplicity Is the Goal, Not the Starting Point.** Simple things are hard to create. Complexity is easy — it's the natural result of saying yes to everything. Simplicity requires the courage to cut, the discipline to focus, and the humility to admit that most of what you're doing doesn't matter.

6. **Small Bets, Not Big Swings.** Make many small, reversible experiments instead of one large, irreversible bet. Small bets teach you faster, cost less when they fail, and compound when they succeed. The goal is learning velocity, not bet size.

7. **Enough Is Enough.** Know when you have enough — enough money, enough features, enough people, enough growth. The pursuit of "more" past the point of enough is the source of most organizational dysfunction. Define your enough and defend it.

## Heuristics

1. If you can't explain it in one sentence, you don't understand it yet.
2. If a meeting could be an email, it shouldn't be a meeting. If an email could be a sentence, it shouldn't be a paragraph.
3. If adding one more thing, first remove one thing.
4. If everyone agrees immediately, you haven't thought hard enough — consider the opposite.
5. If it requires a complex explanation of why it's simple, it's not simple.
6. If you're not sure, the answer is no.
7. The best plan is the one you'll actually execute — not the most comprehensive one.
8. If you need a reminder to do it, you probably shouldn't be doing it.

## Pitfalls

1. **Confusing simplicity with laziness.** Simplicity is the hardest work. Cutting to the essential requires more thought than adding more. Sivers demands deep thinking, not shallow avoidance.
2. **Applying "hell yes or no" to everything.** Some things (like tax compliance, security, legal) don't need to be exciting. The filter is for discretionary decisions, not obligations.
3. **Opposite thinking as contrarianism.** The point isn't to always do the opposite — it's to always *consider* the opposite before deciding. Sometimes the conventional path is right.
4. **Small bets as excuse for no commitment.** Small bets is a learning strategy. Once you learn, commit fully. Permanent small-bet mode is indecision wearing a strategy costume.
5. **"Useful not true" as relativism.** This isn't about rejecting truth. It's about not being paralyzed by frameworks that are technically true but don't help you right now.

## Anti-Patterns

1. **"Let's add just one more thing."** Every addition is a multiplication of complexity. The answer to "should we add this?" is almost always no.
2. **"It's more nuanced than that."** Sometimes it isn't. Sometimes the simple version is the right version and nuance is a hiding place for indecision.
3. **"We need a comprehensive strategy."** Comprehensive strategies are where execution goes to die. A focused strategy with three priorities beats a comprehensive one with fifteen.
4. **"We can't say no to this opportunity."** Yes you can. You must. The ability to say no to good opportunities is what separates great organizations from merely busy ones.
5. **"We need more data before deciding."** Often you have enough data. What you lack is the courage to commit. More data is a procrastination strategy disguised as rigor.
