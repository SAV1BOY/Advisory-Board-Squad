# Good Strategy Bad Strategy — Richard Rumelt

## Summary

Rumelt argues that most of what passes for strategy is actually bad strategy: a combination of fluff, failure to face the real challenge, mistaking goals for strategy, and bad strategic objectives. Good strategy, by contrast, is simple and rigorous: it identifies the critical challenge, creates a guiding policy to address it, and specifies coherent actions to execute the policy. The book provides the most rigorous framework for distinguishing genuine strategic thinking from the feel-good goal-setting that masquerades as strategy in most organizations.

## Key Principles

- **The kernel of good strategy** — Three elements: (1) Diagnosis (what's the critical challenge?), (2) Guiding policy (what's the overall approach to the challenge?), (3) Coherent actions (what specific, coordinated steps will execute the policy?).
- **Bad strategy is not the absence of strategy** — It's actively harmful: it creates the illusion of progress while failing to address the real problem. Four hallmarks: fluff, failure to face the challenge, mistaking goals for strategy, bad strategic objectives.
- **Strategy is about focus** — Trying to do everything is the same as having no strategy. Good strategy requires saying no to many reasonable things in order to concentrate force on the critical few.
- **Proximate objectives** — Objectives must be feasible. Setting a goal you don't know how to achieve is not strategy—it's wishful thinking. Good strategy sets objectives that are achievable given current capabilities.
- **Sources of power** — Strategy creates advantage through leverage, proximate objectives, chain-link systems, design, focus, growth via advantage, dynamics (anticipating change), and inertia/entropy.
- **Coherence is the multiplier** — Actions must reinforce each other. Incoherent actions (pursuing contradictory goals simultaneously) cancel out.

## Frameworks Extracted

1. **The Strategy Kernel** — For any strategic situation: (1) What is the critical challenge? (not the symptom, the underlying challenge), (2) What is the guiding policy? (the high-level approach, not the detailed plan), (3) What are the coherent actions? (specific, coordinated moves that implement the policy).
2. **The Bad Strategy Detector** — Check for: (1) Fluff (restatement of the obvious dressed up as strategy), (2) Failure to identify the challenge, (3) Confusing goals with strategy ("our strategy is to grow 30%"), (4) Incoherent objectives that conflict with each other.
3. **The Focus Test** — If you can add more strategic priorities without removing any, your strategy has no focus. Real strategy requires tradeoffs.
4. **The Coherence Audit** — List all major initiatives. For each pair, ask: do these reinforce each other, conflict with each other, or have no relationship? Reinforcement = coherence. Conflict = incoherence.

## Quotable Lines

- "The core of strategy work is always the same: discovering the critical factors in a situation and designing a way of coordinating and focusing actions to deal with those factors."
- "A hallmark of true expertise and insight is making a complex subject understandable."
- "Strategy is a hypothesis about what will work. Being strategic largely means being less myopic."
- "Good strategy works by focusing energy and resources on one, or a very few, pivotal objectives."

## Application to Board Decisions

- **Strategy review** — Apply the Strategy Kernel to every strategy presentation. Can the CEO articulate the diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent actions in one page? If not, the strategy isn't clear.
- **Bad strategy detection** — When reviewing strategic plans, use the Bad Strategy Detector. Board members should specifically look for goals masquerading as strategy.
- **Resource allocation** — Apply the Focus Test. If the company has more than 3-5 strategic priorities, it has none. The board should push for fewer, more focused bets.
- **Coherence check** — Use the Coherence Audit on the company's initiative portfolio. Incoherent initiatives waste resources and create organizational confusion.
- **Challenge identification** — The board's most valuable contribution to strategy may be helping the CEO correctly diagnose the critical challenge, which is the most intellectually demanding part of strategy.
