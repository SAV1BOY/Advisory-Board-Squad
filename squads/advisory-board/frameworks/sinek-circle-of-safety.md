# Sinek Circle of Safety

## Purpose

Simon Sinek's Circle of Safety describes the conditions under which teams perform at their best: when members feel safe from internal threats (politics, backstabbing, blame) and can focus their energy on external challenges (competition, market shifts, innovation). Leaders create the Circle of Safety by protecting their people from internal dangers, creating trust, and fostering belonging. When the circle is strong, teams are resilient, creative, and loyal. When it breaks, politics replaces productivity.

## When to Use

- When building or repairing team culture after conflict or layoffs
- When diagnosing why a team is underperforming despite having talented people
- When internal politics are consuming energy that should go to external challenges
- When employee engagement or retention is declining
- When merging teams or integrating acquisitions
- When evaluating organizational health as a board advisor

## Core Model

### The Circle Concept
- Inside the circle: trust, belonging, psychological safety, cooperation
- Outside the circle: competition, threats, uncertainty, market forces
- The leader's primary job: maintain the circle's boundary so people feel safe inside
- When people feel safe, they cooperate. When they feel threatened, they self-protect.

### What Breaks the Circle
- Layoffs that feel arbitrary or politically motivated
- Leaders who prioritize their own interests over team welfare
- Metrics and incentives that create internal competition
- Blame culture where mistakes are punished, not learned from
- Opacity about decisions that affect people's livelihoods

### What Builds the Circle
- Consistent, fair treatment of all team members
- Leaders who sacrifice personal benefit for team welfare
- Transparency about challenges and decisions
- Recognition of contribution and effort, not just results
- Investment in people's growth and development

## Steps

1. **Assess the current circle.** Survey the team: Do people feel safe speaking up? Do they trust their colleagues? Do they believe leadership has their back?
2. **Identify circle breakers.** What specific behaviors, policies, or dynamics are eroding safety?
3. **Remove the threats.** Address toxic individuals, unfair policies, or perverse incentives that undermine safety.
4. **Model vulnerability.** Leaders must go first — admitting mistakes, asking for help, showing humanity.
5. **Build rituals of belonging.** Regular team activities, recognition practices, shared experiences that reinforce "we are in this together."
6. **Protect the circle actively.** When external pressures mount, resist the temptation to turn inward. Protect your people from internal threats even when resources are scarce.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Evaluate the Circle of Safety as a leading indicator of organizational health. Teams with strong circles outperform, retain talent, and innovate.
- When a company is struggling, check for broken circles before assuming strategic failure.
- Model circle-building in board dynamics: create safety for dissent, protect whistleblowers, support honest reporting.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Claiming safety while punishing honesty.** Saying "we want candor" while penalizing those who deliver bad news.
2. **Treating layoffs as routine.** Every layoff breaks the circle. If layoffs are necessary, handle them with maximum care and transparency.
3. **Confusing perks with safety.** Free lunch and ping pong tables are not a Circle of Safety. Safety is about trust, not amenities.
4. **Excluding people.** Circles that protect some but not others create resentment and division.
