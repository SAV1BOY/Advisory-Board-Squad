# Dalio Radical Transparency Protocol

## Purpose

Radical transparency, as practiced at Bridgewater Associates, is the principle that almost all information should be shared with almost all people in the organization. This framework provides a practical protocol for implementing radical transparency — deciding what to share, how to share it, and how to handle the discomfort that transparency creates. The premise: people make better decisions with more information, organizations build more trust through openness, and hidden information creates hidden agency problems.

## When to Use

- When building or reforming organizational communication norms
- When trust is low and needs to be rebuilt through demonstrated openness
- When information asymmetry is causing poor decisions or misalignment
- When designing governance structures for boards and advisory relationships
- When a team suspects decisions are being made without relevant context
- When evaluating whether secrecy is protecting the organization or protecting individuals

## Core Model

### The Transparency Spectrum

| Level | Description | Example |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| 0 | Opaque | Decisions made behind closed doors with no explanation |
| 1 | Justified | Decisions explained after the fact with rationale |
| 2 | Participatory | Input solicited before decisions are made |
| 3 | Observable | Decision-making process is visible in real-time |
| 4 | Radical | Nearly all information, including performance assessments and meeting recordings, is available to everyone |

### What Radical Transparency Includes
- Meeting recordings and notes available to all employees
- Performance reviews shared openly (Bridgewater records and shares all meetings)
- Financial information shared broadly (not just with executives)
- Strategic reasoning shared, including the uncertainties and disagreements
- Mistakes and failures shared openly as learning opportunities
- Compensation philosophy and ranges (if not exact numbers) shared

### What Radical Transparency Excludes
- Personal medical or family information
- Legally privileged communications
- Information that would violate another party's confidentiality
- Pre-announcement market-sensitive information (insider trading risk)
- Individual compensation details (without consent)

### The Trust Equation
Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) / Self-Interest

Radical transparency increases credibility (people see your reasoning), reliability (commitments are public), and reduces perceived self-interest (hidden agendas are impossible). The net effect is a dramatic increase in organizational trust.

## Steps

1. **Audit current transparency levels.** For each major information category (financial, strategic, personnel, operational), rate the current transparency level (0-4).

2. **Identify the gaps.** Where is the organization less transparent than it should be? Where is information hoarded? Where do people feel out of the loop?

3. **Define the target transparency level for each category.** Not everything needs to be level 4. Financial performance might be level 3; personnel matters might be level 2. Set intentional targets.

4. **Establish the "default open" principle.** Information should be shared unless there is a specific, articulable reason not to. The burden of proof is on secrecy, not transparency.

5. **Create sharing mechanisms.** How will information flow? All-hands meetings, shared dashboards, open document repositories, recorded meetings, written decision logs.

6. **Train people to receive transparency.** Radical transparency is uncomfortable. People will see things that upset them — messy debates, negative feedback, uncertain strategies. Train people to engage constructively with uncomfortable information.

7. **Handle the exceptions carefully.** When information must be restricted, explain that it is restricted and why (without revealing the content). Silent restriction breeds conspiracy theories.

8. **Measure trust.** Survey organizational trust quarterly. Transparency should correlate with increasing trust over time. If it doesn't, the implementation needs adjustment.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Model transparency with founders: share your reasoning, your doubts, and your mistakes. If you expect transparency from them, you must offer it first.
- Push for transparent board communication. Board materials should be shared with the full leadership team (where appropriate), not hoarded by the CEO.
- When a founder is being selectively transparent — sharing good news broadly and bad news narrowly — flag it. This pattern erodes trust.

### For Founders
- Financial transparency with your team builds loyalty and alignment. People who understand the numbers make better decisions.
- Transparent strategy communication reduces the "rumor mill." When people don't have information, they make it up — and their fiction is usually worse than reality.
- Be transparent about what you don't know. Admitting uncertainty is not weakness; pretending to certainty is dishonesty.

### For Organizations
- Start with transparency about decisions: what was decided, why, and what was considered. This is the highest-leverage transparency improvement.
- Build transparency into tools: shared dashboards, open Slack channels, document repositories with default-public permissions.
- Celebrate transparency acts. When someone shares uncomfortable truth, recognize it publicly.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Transparency without context.** Sharing raw data without explanation causes misinterpretation and panic. Always pair information with context.

2. **Selective transparency.** Sharing only good news or only information that supports the leader's narrative. This is worse than opacity — it is manipulation.

3. **Forcing radical transparency overnight.** Moving from level 0 to level 4 in a week creates chaos. Increase transparency incrementally, allowing the organization to build capacity.

4. **Confusing transparency with democracy.** Transparent decision-making does not mean everyone votes. It means everyone understands the process, reasoning, and outcome. The decision-maker still decides.

5. **Using transparency as a weapon.** Sharing someone's mistake publicly to humiliate them is not transparency — it is cruelty. Transparency serves learning, not punishment.

6. **Ignoring cultural readiness.** Some cultures (organizational and national) are not ready for radical transparency. Assess readiness and adapt the approach.
