# Commitment Escalation

## Overview
Escalation of commitment occurs when decision-makers increase their investment in a failing course of action because of prior investment. Also known as the sunk cost fallacy at organizational scale. It is one of the most expensive biases in corporate decision-making.

## Why Escalation Happens

### Psychological Drivers
- **Sunk cost fallacy** — "We've already invested $10M; we can't walk away now"
- **Self-justification** — Admitting failure threatens the decision-maker's self-concept
- **Completion bias** — The desire to finish what was started, regardless of whether it should be finished
- **Loss aversion** — Stopping feels like crystallizing a loss; continuing preserves hope

### Social Drivers
- **Public commitment** — Decisions made publicly (board votes, announcements) are harder to reverse
- **Career risk** — The person who championed the initiative fears being blamed for its failure
- **Group reinforcement** — Teams that have been working on a project don't want to hear it's being killed

### Structural Drivers
- **No kill criteria** — If success criteria weren't defined upfront, there's no objective trigger for stopping
- **Incremental framing** — Each additional investment seems small relative to total spend ("just one more quarter")
- **Budgetary momentum** — Once a line item exists in the budget, it tends to persist

## Prevention Mechanisms

1. **Define kill criteria upfront** — Before starting any major initiative, define the conditions under which it will be terminated
2. **Separate the decision from the decider** — Have someone other than the initiative's champion evaluate continuation
3. **Fresh eyes review** — Periodically bring in someone unfamiliar with the project to evaluate it without historical context
4. **Sunset clauses** — Every initiative has an expiration date. Continuation requires active justification, not just momentum.
5. **The "Would we start this today?" test** — If the answer is no, the only reason to continue is sunk cost bias

## Board Application
- For every major initiative approved, require explicit kill criteria documented in the board minutes
- Conduct annual portfolio reviews where each initiative must justify its continued existence
- Assign a "fresh eyes" board member to major initiatives—someone not involved in the original approval
- Normalize project termination as good governance, not failure
