# Poor Charlie's Almanack — Charlie Munger

## Summary

A collection of Charlie Munger's speeches, talks, and wisdom compiled over decades. The book reveals Munger's multidisciplinary approach to thinking—drawing from psychology, physics, biology, economics, and history to build a "latticework of mental models." Munger argues that most errors in judgment come from using too few models from too few disciplines. The book covers his investment philosophy, his emphasis on avoiding stupidity over seeking brilliance, and his legendary ability to invert problems. It is less a how-to and more a how-to-think—a manual for building judgment through continuous learning and intellectual humility.

## Key Principles

- **Latticework of mental models** — Build a toolkit of roughly 100 models from multiple disciplines. Reality doesn't respect academic boundaries, and neither should your thinking.
- **Inversion** — Instead of asking how to succeed, ask how to fail, then avoid those things. "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there."
- **Circle of competence** — Know what you know and what you don't. The edges of your competence are where the most expensive mistakes live.
- **Avoid stupidity rather than seek brilliance** — Consistently not doing dumb things beats occasionally doing brilliant things. Filter for downside first.
- **Incentive-caused bias is the most powerful force** — "Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome." Never analyze behavior without analyzing the incentive structure.
- **The Lollapalooza effect** — When multiple psychological biases align in the same direction, the result is an extreme outcome—good or bad.
- **Continuous learning** — Munger reads 500+ pages per day. Compound your knowledge the way you compound capital.

## Frameworks Extracted

1. **The Munger Mental Models Checklist** — Before any major decision, run it through models from: psychology (25+ biases), economics (opportunity cost, comparative advantage), mathematics (compounding, probability), biology (evolution, adaptation), physics (critical mass, tipping points), and engineering (redundancy, margin of safety).
2. **Two-Track Analysis** — First, assess the rational factors (economics, incentives, data). Second, assess the psychological factors (biases, emotions, social proof). Most people only do track one.
3. **Inversion Framework** — State your goal. Then list every way you could fail to achieve it. Design your strategy around avoiding those failure modes first, then pursuing upside.
4. **The Checklist Method** — For repeatable decisions, convert your mental models into a literal checklist. Munger credits checklists with preventing more errors than any other tool.

- "Spend each day trying to be a little wiser than you were when you woke up."
- "You don't have to be brilliant, only a little bit wiser than the other guys, on average, for a long, long time."
- "The best thing a human can do is to help another human know more."

## Application to Board Decisions

- **Investment evaluation** — Apply the Two-Track Analysis to every investment decision. What do the numbers say? What biases might be distorting the board's perception of those numbers?
- **Incentive design** — Before approving any compensation plan or partnership structure, map the incentive-caused biases it might produce. Munger would say this is the single most important board function.
- **Risk management** — Use inversion. Ask: "What would destroy this company?" Then audit whether those failure modes are being actively prevented.
- **Board dynamics** — Watch for the Lollapalooza effect in board consensus. When everyone agrees too quickly, multiple biases may be reinforcing each other. Require a devil's advocate process.
- **Due diligence** — Use the mental models checklist as a literal tool in M&A and strategic reviews. Ensure at least five disciplines are represented in the analysis.
