# Reputational Risk Cases

## Overview
Reputational risk is the potential for negative publicity, public perception, or events to damage an organization's standing. Unlike financial risk, reputational damage is asymmetric: it takes decades to build and minutes to destroy. These cases illustrate patterns boards should recognize and prevent.

## Case Patterns

### The Cover-Up Escalation
- **Pattern:** Initial problem is manageable. Leadership attempts to cover up or minimize. Cover-up is discovered. The cover-up becomes bigger news than the original problem.
- **Example archetype:** Product defect discovered internally. Management decides not to recall. Media investigation reveals internal emails showing knowledge. CEO credibility destroyed.
- **Lesson:** The cover-up is always worse than the crime. Transparency early, even when painful, preserves trust.
- **Board role:** Create a culture where bad news flows upward fast. When bad news arrives, default to transparency.

### The Values-Action Gap
- **Pattern:** Company publicly espouses values (sustainability, inclusion, integrity) but internal practices contradict them.
- **Example archetype:** Company runs sustainability marketing campaign while its supply chain uses child labor. Investigative report exposes the gap.
- **Lesson:** Never claim values you can't substantiate. Aspirational marketing creates liability.
- **Board role:** Audit whether marketing claims match operational reality. If they don't, fix operations or change messaging.

### The Tone-Deaf Response
- **Pattern:** A genuine crisis occurs. Leadership responds with legalistic, dismissive, or self-serving communication.
- **Example archetype:** Data breach affects millions of customers. CEO's initial statement focuses on how the company is also a victim. No empathy, no concrete remediation.
- **Lesson:** In crisis, lead with empathy. Acknowledge impact on affected people before discussing your own situation.
- **Board role:** Pre-approve crisis communication frameworks that lead with empathy and accountability.

### The Leadership Scandal
- **Pattern:** A senior leader's personal behavior (fraud, harassment, discrimination) becomes public.
- **Example archetype:** CEO's pattern of workplace harassment is exposed. Board initially defends the CEO, then reverses course. Both the CEO and the board lose credibility.
- **Lesson:** Act swiftly on credible allegations. Defending a leader against credible evidence damages the board's own reputation.
- **Board role:** Investigate immediately. Take decisive action. Prioritize the organization's integrity over any individual.

## Prevention Framework

1. **Reputation risk register** — Maintain a list of scenarios that could damage reputation, with probability and impact assessments
2. **Crisis communication playbook** — Pre-written templates and processes for the most likely crisis types
3. **Values audit** — Annual review of whether external claims match internal practices
4. **Stakeholder listening** — Monitor social media, employee forums, customer feedback, and press for early warning signals
5. **Red team exercises** — Periodically stress-test the organization's response to hypothetical crises

## Board Application
- Include reputational risk in the quarterly risk review alongside financial and operational risk
- Approve a crisis communication playbook and review it annually
- When a reputational event occurs, convene immediately. Speed and tone in the first 24-48 hours determine the trajectory.
- Never prioritize protecting an individual (including the CEO) over protecting the organization's reputation
