# M&A Success and Failure Rates

## Key Findings

1. **The majority of acquisitions destroy value for the acquirer.** 60-70% of acquisitions fail to deliver projected synergies within 3 years. For technology acquisitions specifically, the failure rate is even higher at 65-75%, primarily due to integration challenges and talent attrition.
2. **Acqui-hires have the highest success rate among acquisition types.** Small team acquisitions (<20 people) where the primary asset is talent succeed at 70-80% rates when the team is integrated into existing product lines. Success drops to 40% when the acquired team is asked to continue working on their original product independently.
3. **Cultural incompatibility is the leading cause of integration failure.** 55% of failed acquisitions cite cultural integration as the primary failure factor. This is particularly acute when a large, process-driven company acquires a small, entrepreneurial team. The acquiring company's antibodies reject the acquired team's working style.
4. **Speed of integration correlates with success.** Acquisitions where integration is completed within 6 months succeed at 2x the rate of those that take 12+ months. Prolonged "integration limbo" creates uncertainty that drives talent departure and customer confusion.
5. **Overpayment is the second most common failure mode.** Acquisitions made at >15x revenue multiples succeed at roughly half the rate of those at 5-10x multiples. The pressure to justify a high price leads to aggressive synergy assumptions that rarely materialize.
6. **Serial acquirers outperform one-time acquirers by a wide margin.** Companies that have completed 5+ acquisitions succeed at 68% rates vs. 41% for first-time acquirers. The advantage comes from institutional knowledge about integration, not deal-making skill.

## Implications for Board

- **Default skepticism on acquisitions.** Given a 60-70% failure rate, the board should approach every acquisition proposal with constructive skepticism. The burden of proof should be on the acquisition thesis, not on the objectors.
- **Pre-mortem requirement.** Before approving any acquisition, require a structured pre-mortem (see pre-mortem-pattern.md): "Assume this acquisition failed. Why?" This surfaces integration risks, talent retention concerns, and synergy assumptions that are typically glossed over in acquisition excitement.
- **Integration plan before approval.** The board should not approve an acquisition without a detailed 90-day integration plan. If the team cannot articulate how integration will work, the acquisition should be deferred.
- **Talent retention incentives.** For talent-driven acquisitions, verify that key personnel have retention incentives (typically 2-4 year vesting schedules) and that their roles post-acquisition are clearly defined and genuinely attractive.
- **Synergy discount.** Apply a 50% haircut to all projected revenue synergies and a 25% haircut to projected cost synergies when evaluating an acquisition's financial model. This reflects historical realization rates.
- **Kill criteria.** Establish pre-defined conditions under which the board would walk away from a deal. Without explicit kill criteria, momentum and sunk cost bias drive completion of bad deals.
