# Getting to Yes — Roger Fisher & William Ury

## Summary

The foundational text on principled negotiation from the Harvard Negotiation Project. Fisher and Ury argue that positional bargaining (each side stakes a position and makes concessions) is inefficient and damages relationships. Instead, they propose negotiating on the merits: separating people from the problem, focusing on interests rather than positions, inventing options for mutual gain, and insisting on objective criteria. The method works because most negotiations are not zero-sum—there's almost always a creative solution that serves both parties' underlying interests better than a compromise between positions.

## Key Principles

- **Separate the people from the problem** — Negotiate the substance without damaging the relationship. Address emotions directly rather than letting them contaminate the substantive discussion.
- **Focus on interests, not positions** — Positions are what people say they want. Interests are why they want it. Multiple positions can satisfy the same interest.
- **Invent options for mutual gain** — Before narrowing to a solution, brainstorm widely. Most negotiations leave value on the table because parties rush to compromise.
- **Insist on objective criteria** — Use external standards (market rates, precedent, expert opinion) to evaluate proposals. This prevents the negotiation from becoming a contest of will.
- **BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement)** — Know your walkaway option before you negotiate. Your BATNA is your source of power; the better it is, the more leverage you have.

## Frameworks Extracted

1. **The Four Principles** — (1) Separate people from problem, (2) Focus on interests, (3) Generate options, (4) Use objective criteria. Apply in sequence for any negotiation.
2. **BATNA Analysis** — Before any negotiation: (1) What is your best alternative if no deal is reached? (2) How can you improve your BATNA? (3) What is the other side's BATNA? (4) Can you worsen their BATNA ethically?
3. **The Interest Map** — List each party's positions, then dig for underlying interests. For each interest, generate 3+ options that could satisfy it. Overlapping interests are the foundation of creative deals.
4. **The One-Text Procedure** — A mediator (or one party) drafts a single text, then iteratively improves it based on both sides' input. Prevents positional anchoring.

## Quotable Lines

- "The reason you negotiate is to produce something better than the results you can obtain without negotiating."
- "Be hard on the problem, soft on the people."
- "The most powerful interests are basic human needs: security, economic well-being, belonging, recognition, control over one's life."
- "In the absence of a good BATNA, you're negotiating with your hands tied."

## Application to Board Decisions

- **M&A negotiation** — Apply interest-based negotiation rather than positional haggling on price. Understanding the seller's underlying interests (legacy, team retention, speed of close) often unlocks creative deal structures.
- **Executive compensation** — Use the Interest Map to negotiate executive packages. The candidate's underlying interests (security, autonomy, growth) often differ from their stated positions (salary, title).
- **Stakeholder conflicts** — When board members disagree, apply "separate people from problem." Address the emotional dynamics directly, then negotiate the substance.
- **Partnership deals** — Use the Four Principles framework for any strategic partnership negotiation. Generate multiple options before committing to a structure.
- **BATNA as power** — Before any critical negotiation, the board should explicitly assess both sides' BATNAs. The side with the better alternative has more leverage.
