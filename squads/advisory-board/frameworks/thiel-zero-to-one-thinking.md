# Thiel Zero to One Thinking

## Purpose

Peter Thiel's "Zero to One" concept distinguishes between two types of progress: horizontal/extensive (copying things that work — going from 1 to n) and vertical/intensive (creating something genuinely new — going from 0 to 1). This framework helps leaders identify whether they are building something truly new or merely iterating on what exists, and provides the strategic principles for creating zero-to-one breakthroughs.

## When to Use

- When evaluating whether a startup idea is truly novel or incremental
- When strategic planning risks defaulting to competitive imitation
- When innovation efforts feel stuck in iteration rather than creation
- When distinguishing between transformative and sustaining innovation
- During brainstorming to push beyond incremental improvements

## Core Model

### Zero to One vs. One to N
- **Zero to One (Vertical):** Creating something that has never existed. The first smartphone. The first search engine. The first social network. Technology in the Thiel sense.
- **One to N (Horizontal):** Copying and scaling what already works. Opening 100 more coffee shops. Entering a new geography with an existing product. Globalization in the Thiel sense.

### Zero-to-One Thinking Requires
1. **Definite optimism:** Believing the future can be shaped, not just predicted, and having a specific plan
2. **First-principles reasoning:** Building from fundamental truths, not analogies to what already exists
3. **Contrarian insight:** Seeing something others miss
4. **The courage to be different:** Zero-to-one companies look weird at first. If everyone agrees it's a good idea, it's probably 1-to-n.

### The Four Views of the Future
| | Optimistic | Pessimistic |
|---|-----------|-------------|
| **Definite** | Best: build a specific, better future (US 1950-1970) | Prepare for specific challenges (China today) |
| **Indefinite** | Worst for innovation: expect good things without a plan (US today) | Prepare for unknown bad things |

Zero-to-one companies require definite optimism: a specific vision of a better future and the plan to build it.

## Steps

1. **Ask the Zero-to-One question.** "Is what we're building genuinely new, or a variation of something that exists?" Be ruthlessly honest.
2. **Apply first-principles reasoning.** Strip away analogies and assumptions. What are the fundamental truths? What can be built from them?
3. **Identify the contrarian truth.** What do you believe that most people don't? This is the foundation of zero-to-one thinking.
4. **Design for 10x, not 10%.** If you can only improve by 10%, you're in one-to-n territory. Zero-to-one requires an order-of-magnitude leap.
5. **Build a definite plan.** Not "let's iterate and see" but "here is our specific vision and how we will achieve it."
6. **Commit fully.** Zero-to-one creation requires concentrated effort, not diversified hedging.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Evaluate whether portfolio companies are building zero-to-one or one-to-n businesses. Both can succeed, but the strategies are fundamentally different.
- Challenge founders who use analogies: "We're the Uber of X" is one-to-n thinking. Push for first-principles descriptions.
- The highest-value advisory contribution is helping founders develop definite, optimistic visions rather than incremental plans.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing novelty with value.** Not everything new is valuable. Zero-to-one must also be useful.
2. **Indefinite optimism.** Expecting good things without a specific plan. "We'll figure it out" is not zero-to-one thinking.
3. **Using analogies instead of first principles.** "It's like X but for Y" is one-to-n.
4. **Seeking permission.** Zero-to-one creation requires acting before consensus forms. Waiting for validation is waiting too long.
