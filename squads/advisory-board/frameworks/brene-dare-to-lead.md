# Brene Brown Dare to Lead

## Purpose

Brene Brown's Dare to Lead framework operationalizes the research finding that courage is the most important leadership capability — and that courage requires vulnerability. Daring leadership is not about having all the answers; it is about having the courage to show up when you cannot predict or control the outcome. This framework provides the four skill sets of courageous leadership and practical tools for developing them.

## When to Use

- When leadership avoidance is causing organizational problems (tough conversations not happening, difficult decisions not being made)
- When building leadership development programs
- When a founder is struggling with imposter syndrome or fear of failure
- When team conflict is being avoided rather than addressed productively
- When organizational culture rewards armor (perfectionism, cynicism) over authenticity

## Core Model

### Four Skill Sets of Courageous Leadership

**1. Rumbling with Vulnerability:** The willingness to show up when you can't control the outcome. Not weakness but the most accurate measure of courage.
- Showing up for hard conversations
- Admitting "I don't know"
- Asking for help
- Taking smart risks where failure is possible

**2. Living into Values:** Identifying your core values (maximum two) and practicing them daily rather than just professing them.
- Name your two core values
- Define 3-4 behaviors that demonstrate each value
- Define 3-4 behaviors that violate each value ("slippery behaviors")
- Hold yourself accountable to observable behaviors, not intentions

**3. Braving Trust:** Building trust through seven specific behaviors (see BRAVING Trust framework). Trust is not built in grand gestures but in small moments over time.

**4. Learning to Rise:** The process of getting back up after a fall — recognizing emotions, owning the story, rewriting the ending.
- The reckoning: noticing you've been triggered emotionally
- The rumble: getting curious about the story you're telling yourself
- The revolution: writing a new, more accurate ending

## Steps

1. **Self-assess on the four skill sets.** Where are you strong? Where do you armor up?
2. **Identify your two core values.** From a list of values, choose the two that are most essential to who you are.
3. **Map your armor patterns.** When you feel vulnerable, what do you do? Perfectionism? Cynicism? Numbing? Controlling?
4. **Practice vulnerability in low-stakes settings.** Start small: admit a mistake in a team meeting, ask for feedback.
5. **Build trust deliberately.** Use the BRAVING inventory to identify where trust is strong and where it needs work.
6. **Develop a "Learning to Rise" practice.** When you fall (and you will), use the three-step process: reckon, rumble, revolution.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Model daring leadership in board settings. Admit when you don't know. Change your mind publicly when presented with better evidence.
- Help founders develop vulnerability as a leadership skill, not a liability. Founders who can't be vulnerable create cultures of hiding.
- When you see a founder armoring up (perfectionism, deflection, over-control), name it with care.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing vulnerability with oversharing.** Vulnerability is appropriate openness with trusted people, not emotional dumping.
2. **Listing values without living them.** Values are behaviors, not posters.
3. **Skipping the rumble.** Going straight from emotional reaction to action without getting curious about the story.
4. **Using vulnerability as manipulation.** Sharing vulnerability to get sympathy or leverage is the opposite of daring leadership.
