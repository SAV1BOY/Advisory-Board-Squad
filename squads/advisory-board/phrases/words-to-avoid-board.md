# Words to Avoid — Board Communication

A catalog of words, phrases, and patterns that weaken board communication. Each entry explains why it fails and what to use instead.

## Corporate Filler

1. **"Synergize" / "Leverage" / "Move the needle"**
   Why avoid: Empty calories. They sound strategic but communicate nothing specific.
   Instead: Name the specific action. "Integrate the sales teams" instead of "synergize." "Use our distribution network" instead of "leverage."

2. **"Align" / "Alignment" (as a standalone goal)**
   Why avoid: Alignment is not an outcome. It is a precondition for an outcome.
   Instead: State what the aligned action produces. "Agree on Q2 priorities so both teams execute the same plan" instead of "get aligned."

3. **"Circle back" / "Take offline" / "Table this"**
   Why avoid: These are polite ways to avoid a decision or dodge a topic. Use them only when genuinely deferring, and state when the conversation will happen.
   Instead: "Let's schedule 30 minutes on Thursday to resolve this." With a date, it's a deferral. Without a date, it's avoidance.

4. **"Pivot" (when you mean "we failed and changed direction")**
   Why avoid: Pivot has been sanitized to hide failure. Be honest about what happened.
   Instead: "We tried X, it didn't work because Y, and we changed direction to Z."

5. **"At the end of the day"**
   Why avoid: Filler that adds nothing. Every sentence happens "at the end of the day."
   Instead: Delete it. Start with the point itself.

## Hedging and Weakening

6. **"I feel like..." / "I kind of think..."**
   Why avoid: Undermines your own position. If you think something, state it.
   Instead: "My assessment is..." or "I believe..." or simply state the claim directly.

7. **"With all due respect..."**
   Why avoid: Universally understood as a prelude to disrespect.
   Instead: Just state your point respectfully. The respect should be in the content, not in a disclaimer.

8. **"Just" (as a minimizer)**
   Why avoid: "I just think we should..." diminishes the statement. "I'm just following up..." apologizes for existing.
   Instead: Remove the word. "I think we should..." "I'm following up..."

9. **"Basically" / "Essentially"**
   Why avoid: Signals that you're about to simplify something you haven't fully thought through.
   Instead: State the thing. If it's already simple, you don't need a disclaimer.

10. **"We should probably..."**
    Why avoid: If it's worth doing, commit. "Probably" signals you haven't decided.
    Instead: "I recommend we..." or "We should..." or "I'm not sure whether we should — here's why."

## Blame Avoidance

11. **"Mistakes were made"**
    Why avoid: Passive voice that removes accountability. Who made the mistakes?
    Instead: "We made a mistake. Specifically, [person or team] [action]."

12. **"It was a team decision"**
    Why avoid: When used to diffuse accountability, it's dishonest. Every decision has an owner.
    Instead: "I made this decision with input from the team. I'm accountable for the outcome."

13. **"We inherited this problem"**
    Why avoid: Even if true, it signals avoidance. You may not have caused it, but you own it now.
    Instead: "This problem predates the current team. Here's our plan to fix it."

14. **"The market made it difficult"**
    Why avoid: Externalizes blame. Market conditions affect everyone; the question is how you responded.
    Instead: "Market conditions were challenging. We [did / didn't] adapt fast enough. Here's why."

## False Comfort

15. **"Everything is on track"**
    Why avoid: Almost never entirely true. And when it is true, it doesn't need to be said.
    Instead: Provide specific status. "Revenue is 2 % above plan. Hiring is one week behind. Product launch is on schedule."

16. **"We're cautiously optimistic"**
    Why avoid: Means nothing. You cannot be both cautious and optimistic — pick one and explain why.
    Instead: "We expect [X] outcome with [Y] confidence. The risk is [Z]."

17. **"It's under control"**
    Why avoid: Often said when it isn't, to prevent further questions.
    Instead: "Here is the situation, the actions we've taken, and the outstanding risks."

18. **"This is a best practice"**
    Why avoid: Best practices are averages. They describe what most companies do, not what the best companies do.
    Instead: "This is what [specific company] does, and here's why it works for our situation." Or: "This is our approach, and here's the evidence it works."

## Vagueness

19. **"Significant" / "Substantial" / "Meaningful"**
    Why avoid: Adjectives that substitute for numbers. What is "significant" growth? 5 %? 50 %?
    Instead: Use the number. "Revenue grew 14 %." "We reduced churn by 3 percentage points."

20. **"Going forward" / "Moving forward"**
    Why avoid: Implies the past is being swept under the rug.
    Instead: If you're changing direction, state what happened and what's changing. "We tried X. It didn't work. We're switching to Y."

21. **"Various" / "Several" / "Multiple"**
    Why avoid: Imprecise. How many is "several"?
    Instead: State the number. "Three customers churned." "We evaluated five options."

22. **"A lot" / "Very" / "Really"**
    Why avoid: Intensifiers that weaken rather than strengthen. "Very good results" is weaker than "14 % above target."
    Instead: Replace the adjective + intensifier with a fact.

## Performative Language

23. **"We take this very seriously"**
    Why avoid: Overused to the point of meaninglessness. Seriousness is demonstrated through actions, not declarations.
    Instead: Describe the actions you're taking. The actions demonstrate the seriousness.

24. **"We're committed to..." (without specific actions)**
    Why avoid: Commitment without action items is a press release, not a plan.
    Instead: "We're committed to [X]. Specifically, we are doing [Y] by [date]."

25. **"Thought leadership" / "World-class" / "Best-in-class"**
    Why avoid: Self-congratulatory labels that the market assigns, not the company.
    Instead: State the evidence. "Our NPS is in the top 5 % of our industry" is credible. "We're world-class" is not.

## The Rule

When in doubt, apply this test: **Would you say this to a smart friend over coffee?** If the phrase sounds unnatural in that setting, it's probably corporate filler and should be replaced with plain language.
