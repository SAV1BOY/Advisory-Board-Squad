# Taxonomy of Innovation Types

## Overview

Not all innovation is the same. Different types of innovation require different strategies, risk tolerances, organizational structures, and time horizons. Misclassifying the type of innovation leads to misallocated resources — applying startup methods to sustaining innovations, or incumbent methods to disruptive ones.

## The Innovation Type Matrix

### Type 1: Incremental Innovation
**Definition:** Continuous improvement to existing products, services, or processes.

**Characteristics:**
- Low risk, predictable returns
- Executed by existing teams within existing business units
- Measured by efficiency gains, margin improvement, or customer satisfaction
- Timeline: weeks to months

**Examples:**
- Improving checkout flow conversion rate by 15%
- Reducing manufacturing defect rate from 2% to 0.5%
- Adding requested features to an existing product based on customer feedback
- Streamlining internal processes to reduce cycle time

**Organizational fit:** Core engineering and product teams. Standard project management. Regular release cycles.

**Common mistake:** Calling incremental work "innovation" to satisfy board expectations for transformation.

### Type 2: Sustaining Innovation
**Definition:** Significant improvements that strengthen the existing business model for current customers.

**Characteristics:**
- Moderate risk with high confidence in customer demand
- Strengthens competitive position in existing market
- Requires meaningful investment but within established budgeting frameworks
- Timeline: 6-18 months

**Examples:**
- Apple's annual iPhone improvements (better camera, faster processor)
- Tesla's over-the-air software updates that add new capabilities
- Enterprise software platforms adding AI-powered features
- Banks launching mobile banking applications

**Organizational fit:** Dedicated project teams within existing business units. Cross-functional collaboration. Executive sponsorship for resource allocation.

**Common mistake:** Confusing sustaining innovation with disruption. Better products for existing customers are valuable but do not create new markets.

### Type 3: Adjacent Innovation
**Definition:** Expanding into new markets or customer segments using existing capabilities.

**Characteristics:**
- Moderate to high risk depending on market familiarity
- Leverages existing assets (technology, brand, distribution) in new contexts
- Requires new go-to-market capabilities even when product is familiar
- Timeline: 12-24 months

**Examples:**
- Amazon expanding from books to all e-commerce categories
- Uber moving from ride-sharing to food delivery (Uber Eats)
- Salesforce moving from sales CRM to platform-as-a-service
- Nike entering digital fitness tracking with Nike+

**Organizational fit:** Separate team with access to core assets. Distinct P&L or budget. Different success metrics than the core business.

**Common mistake:** Assuming adjacency is easy because the capability exists. New markets have new rules, competitors, and customer expectations.

### Type 4: Disruptive Innovation
**Definition:** Creating new markets or displacing established competitors by targeting underserved segments with simpler, cheaper, or more accessible offerings.

**Characteristics:**
- High risk, potentially transformative returns
- Initially serves non-consumers or over-served customers
- Inferior on traditional metrics but superior on accessibility, cost, or convenience
- Incumbents rationally ignore it — until they cannot
- Timeline: 2-5+ years

**Examples:**
- Netflix streaming displacing Blockbuster's retail model
- Airbnb creating a peer-to-peer accommodation market
- Personal computers disrupting minicomputers and mainframes
- Low-cost airlines disrupting legacy carriers

**Organizational fit:** Separate entity or autonomous team with independent funding and metrics. Must be protected from core business metrics and culture. Requires patient capital.

**Common mistake:** Trying to manage disruption within the existing organizational structure. The core business will starve it of resources to protect near-term revenue.

### Type 5: Radical / Breakthrough Innovation
**Definition:** Fundamentally new technology or business model that creates entirely new industries.

**Characteristics:**
- Very high risk, uncertain timeline, potentially industry-defining returns
- Requires sustained R&D investment with no guaranteed commercial outcome
- Often emerges from research labs, universities, or deep-tech startups
- May take a decade or more to reach commercial viability
- Timeline: 5-15+ years

**Examples:**
- CRISPR gene editing technology
- Blockchain and decentralized finance
- Commercial space flight (SpaceX)
- mRNA vaccine platform (Moderna/BioNTech)

**Organizational fit:** R&D labs, corporate venture arms, or university partnerships. Must be funded separately from commercial operations. Success is measured by technical milestones, not revenue.

**Common mistake:** Expecting breakthrough innovation to follow predictable project timelines and business case frameworks. It cannot.

## The Innovation Portfolio

Balanced innovation requires investment across multiple types:

| Innovation Type | Recommended Allocation | Expected Return |
|----------------|----------------------|-----------------|
| Incremental | 50-60% of innovation budget | Predictable, steady |
| Sustaining | 20-25% | High confidence, moderate upside |
| Adjacent | 10-15% | Moderate confidence, significant upside |
| Disruptive | 5-10% | Low confidence, transformative upside |
| Radical | 2-5% | Very low confidence, industry-defining upside |

## Advisory Questions for Innovation Assessment

1. What percentage of R&D spend goes to incremental vs. transformative innovation?
2. Is the organization structurally capable of pursuing disruptive innovation, or will antibodies kill it?
3. Does the board understand the difference between sustaining and disruptive innovation?
4. Are innovation efforts measured with the right metrics for their type?
5. Is there a portfolio view of innovation investments, or are all projects evaluated against the same criteria?

## Key Principle

> "The innovator's dilemma is not about intelligence or effort. It is about rational actors making rational decisions within a system that structurally cannot pursue disruptive innovation." — Clayton Christensen (adapted)

The advisor's role is helping leaders see which type of innovation they are actually pursuing, whether it matches their strategic intent, and whether the organization is structured to execute it.
