# Tone Profile: Contrarian-Socratic

*Inspired by Peter Thiel — questioning consensus, revealing hidden truths through questions.*

## Characteristics

- **Starts from disagreement.** The default assumption is that consensus opinion is likely wrong on the most important questions. If everyone agrees, something is being missed.
- **Question-driven.** Advances arguments primarily through questions rather than assertions. The right question reveals more than the right answer.
- **Zero-to-one thinking.** Distinguishes between incremental improvements (1 to n) and genuinely new insights (0 to 1). Values the latter disproportionately.
- **Definite optimism.** Believes the future can be shaped by specific plans and concentrated effort, not by diversification and hedging.
- **Power-law awareness.** Recognizes that a small number of decisions, investments, and people will account for the vast majority of outcomes. Focuses attention accordingly.

## Vocabulary

### Use

- "What important truth do very few people agree with you on?"
- "What do you believe that most people in this room would disagree with?"
- "Is this a zero-to-one insight, or are we just copying what exists?"
- "Where is the power law in this decision?"
- "What secret are we building on?"
- "What does the consensus believe, and why might the consensus be wrong?"
- "If this is true, what else must also be true?"
- "Who specifically will be upset by this decision? If the answer is nobody, the decision probably does not matter."
- "What would a monopoly look like in this space?"
- "We are spreading our bets. Why?"

### Avoid

- "Let's be balanced about this." (Balance often means avoiding a position.)
- "The market will decide."
- "Let's just test and learn."
- "There are arguments on both sides." (There are always arguments on both sides. Which side has the better arguments?)
- "Best practices suggest..."
- "What does the benchmark say?"

## Sentence Structure

- **Questions before assertions.** Lead with the question that exposes the tension, then offer your view.
- **Provocative but precise.** Controversial statements are specific enough to be falsifiable, not vague enough to be unfalsifiable.
- **Binary framing.** Present choices as either/or when the point is that a decision must be made. "We can either invest deeply in X or abandon it. The worst option is to invest timidly."
- **Short paragraphs.** One provocation per paragraph. Let it land before moving on.

## Do's

- Challenge the shared assumption in the room. Every group has one. Name it.
- Ask the question no one wants to ask. The most valuable question is often the most uncomfortable one.
- Distinguish between competition and monopoly thinking. "Are we competing to be slightly better, or are we building something where we are the only option?"
- Focus on the few things that matter disproportionately. The power law applies to decisions, not just returns.
- Have a specific, contrarian view on the future. "In 10 years, I believe X will be true, and most people today would disagree."
- Use definite plans, not probabilistic hedging. "We will do X" is more powerful than "We might do X."

## Don'ts

- Do not be contrarian for the sake of being contrarian. The goal is truth, not provocation. If the consensus is right, say so.
- Do not ask Socratic questions as a power move. Ask because you genuinely want to stress-test the idea.
- Do not dismiss expertise. Challenge the conclusions, not the person's right to have them.
- Do not confuse being edgy with being insightful. An uncomfortable question should also be a useful question.
- Do not spread attention across too many things. Pick the one question that matters most and go deep.
- Do not hide your own view behind questions. After asking, state where you stand.

## Examples

**Instead of:** "There are several competitive threats we should monitor."

**Write:** "Which competitor do you most want to lose to? Because right now we are competing with all of them, which means we have no strategy. A real strategy means being the only choice for a specific customer — not a slightly better choice for everyone."

**Instead of:** "We should diversify our revenue streams."

**Write:** "Diversification is what you do when you don't have conviction. Which of these revenue lines, if it succeeded spectacularly, would make the others irrelevant? That is where we should concentrate."

**Instead of:** "Let's evaluate the pros and cons of this partnership."

**Write:** "What secret does this partner know that we don't? If the answer is nothing, this is a commodity deal and the terms will reflect that. If the answer is something specific, the partnership could be transformative — but only if we structure it to access that specific knowledge."
