# The Innovator's Dilemma — Clayton Christensen

## Summary

Christensen explains why well-managed, customer-focused companies fail when disruptive technologies emerge. The paradox: the very practices that make companies successful (listening to customers, investing in profitable products, pursuing larger markets) are the same practices that blind them to disruption. Disruptive innovations start in small, unattractive markets with inferior products that improve rapidly. Incumbents rationally ignore them because their best customers don't want them and the margins are too small. By the time the disruption matures, it's too late to respond. The book provides frameworks for recognizing and responding to disruption before it becomes fatal.

## Key Principles

- **Sustaining vs. disruptive innovation** — Sustaining innovations improve existing products for existing customers. Disruptive innovations create new markets or enter at the bottom of existing ones with simpler, cheaper, more accessible products.
- **Good management causes failure** — Listening to your best customers, investing in your most profitable products, and targeting large markets are rational decisions that create disruption blind spots.
- **Disruption starts at the bottom** — Disruptive products are initially worse on traditional metrics but better on accessibility, simplicity, or price. They improve along a different trajectory.
- **Value networks shape perception** — Companies evaluate innovations through the lens of their existing value network (customers, margins, cost structures). Disruptions that don't fit the network are dismissed.
- **Autonomous organizations are the response** — To pursue disruption, create a separate organization with its own P&L, culture, and customer set. The parent company's immune system will otherwise kill the disruptive initiative.

## Frameworks Extracted

1. **The Disruption Litmus Test** — Is the new technology: (1) Initially inferior on mainstream metrics? (2) Cheaper or more accessible? (3) Improving rapidly? (4) Targeting non-consumers or the most overserved segment? If all four: disruptive.
2. **The Value Network Map** — Chart your company's value network: who are your customers, what do they value, what are your margin requirements? Then ask: what innovations can't survive in this network but could thrive in a different one?
3. **The RPV Framework** — Resources (what we have), Processes (how we do things), Values (what we prioritize). An organization can only pursue opportunities that fit its RPV profile. Disruptions usually require different processes and values, which is why incumbents must create separate units.
4. **The Autonomous Unit Model** — For pursuing disruptive opportunities: (1) Create a separate organization, (2) Give it different metrics and values, (3) Let it find its own customers, (4) Protect it from the parent company's resource allocation process.

## Quotable Lines

- "The logical, competent decisions of management that are critical to the success of their companies are also the reasons why they lose their positions of leadership."
- "Generally, disruptive technologies underperform established products in mainstream markets. But they have other features that a few fringe customers value."
- "Watching how customers actually use a product provides much more reliable information than can be gleaned from a focus group."
- "Companies stumble not because of bad management, but because good management itself can be a hindrance."

## Application to Board Decisions

- **Disruption monitoring** — The board should regularly ask: what technologies or business models could disrupt our core business? Use the Disruption Litmus Test to evaluate threats.
- **Innovation portfolio** — Ensure the company maintains both sustaining innovation (improving current products) and disruptive experiments (exploring new value networks). Boards that only fund sustaining innovation create disruption risk.
- **Autonomous unit governance** — When the company creates a disruptive unit, the board must protect it from the parent company's metrics and resource allocation pressures.
- **M&A for disruption** — Acquiring disruptive startups and integrating them into the parent destroys the disruption. The board should ensure acquisitions maintain autonomy.
- **Customer over-reliance** — Challenge the leadership team when every decision is justified by "our customers want this." Today's customers are the worst source of insight about tomorrow's disruption.
