# Opportunity Cost Framework

## Purpose

This framework makes the invisible visible by forcing teams to explicitly articulate what they are giving up with every decision. Every resource allocated to one initiative is a resource unavailable for every other initiative. Opportunity cost is the value of the best foregone alternative. Most organizations evaluate decisions in isolation — "Is this a good use of $1M?" The right question is: "Is this the BEST use of $1M compared to everything else we could do with it?"

## When to Use

- During resource allocation and budgeting decisions
- When a team wants to add a new initiative without removing an existing one
- When evaluating whether to continue an underperforming project
- During hiring decisions (every seat filled is a seat unavailable for a different role)
- When founders are spread across too many priorities
- At quarterly planning to force prioritization
- When evaluating "free" opportunities that still consume time and attention

## Core Model

### The Three Scarce Resources
Every opportunity cost analysis must consider three resources, not just money:

1. **Capital**: Money allocated to one thing cannot be spent on another
2. **Time**: The most irreversible resource — time spent cannot be recovered
3. **Attention**: The scarcest leadership resource — cognitive bandwidth is finite and every initiative consumes it

### The Opportunity Cost Equation
True cost of any initiative = Direct cost + Opportunity cost of the best alternative

Example: Building Feature A costs $500K. But if those same engineers could have built Feature B (which would generate $2M in revenue), the true cost of Feature A is $500K + $2M = $2.5M.

### The "Not-Doing" List
For every initiative you choose to pursue, explicitly document what you chose NOT to pursue and why. This creates accountability for prioritization and a record that can be reviewed.

### The Attention Tax
Every active project consumes leadership attention even when not actively being worked on. A CEO with 10 active priorities has ~10% attention for each. A CEO with 3 active priorities has ~33% attention for each. The opportunity cost of adding a 4th priority is not just resources — it is the dilution of attention across all four.

## Steps

1. **List all competing uses of the resource.** For any allocation decision, enumerate all realistic alternatives. Include: the proposed initiative, 2-3 alternative uses, and the "do nothing / save" option.

2. **Estimate the value of each alternative.** Use expected value analysis where possible. For each alternative, estimate the range of outcomes and their probabilities.

3. **Rank alternatives by expected value.** The best foregone alternative is your opportunity cost. If you choose option A, and option B was the next best alternative, then option B's expected value is the opportunity cost of choosing A.

4. **Calculate true cost.** True cost = Direct cost + Opportunity cost (value of the best alternative). A positive decision is one where the value of the chosen option exceeds its true cost.

5. **Document the "Not-Doing" list.** For every approved initiative, record which alternatives were rejected and their estimated value. This becomes a powerful planning artifact.

6. **Review the "Not-Doing" list quarterly.** Has the opportunity cost landscape changed? Are there items on the Not-Doing list that should now be prioritized? Are there active initiatives that should be replaced?

7. **Apply the attention audit.** Count the number of active initiatives consuming leadership attention. If it exceeds 3-5 for a founder or 5-7 for a team, you are over-committed. Something must be killed or delegated.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- The most valuable question a board advisor can ask is: "What are you NOT doing because you're doing this?" If the founder cannot answer, they have not thought about opportunity cost.
- Use opportunity cost to challenge "good" initiatives that are not "best" initiatives. A project can be positive ROI and still be the wrong use of resources.
- Help founders kill projects. Founders are emotionally attached to their initiatives. The board's job is to create permission to stop things.

### For Resource Allocation
- Never approve a new initiative without asking what will be stopped or deprioritized. Adding without subtracting is a fantasy.
- Apply the "hell yes or no" filter: if a new initiative is not dramatically better than current priorities, it should not displace them.

### For Personal Effectiveness
- The opportunity cost of every meeting is the deep work you could have done instead. Audit your calendar through this lens.
- The opportunity cost of saying yes to every advisor, investor, or partnership request is the time you would have spent on your highest-leverage activity.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Ignoring opportunity cost entirely.** The most common error. Evaluating initiatives in isolation without comparing to alternatives.

2. **Treating time as free.** "It'll only take a few hours" ignores the opportunity cost of those hours. Founders' time is the most expensive resource in a startup.

3. **Sunk cost confusion.** Past investment is not relevant to future opportunity cost. The question is always forward-looking: "Given where we are now, what is the best use of our remaining resources?"

4. **Unlimited resource fantasy.** "We'll just hire more people" or "We'll raise more capital" ignores the real constraint, which is usually attention and coordination capacity.

5. **Comparing to zero instead of the alternative.** "This project returns $X" is meaningless without knowing what else could return $X or more.

6. **Ignoring the option to wait.** Sometimes the best alternative is to preserve optionality by doing nothing now and deciding later with more information.

## Related Frameworks

- **Decision Memo Framework** — Every Decision Memo should include an opportunity cost section
- **Expected Value and Asymmetry** — Use EV to compare the proposed initiative against alternatives
- **Strategy Choice Cascade** — Where to Play choices define the opportunity cost landscape
- **Power Law Thinking** — In power law environments, the opportunity cost of not investing in winners is enormous
- **Reversible-Irreversible Decisions** — Irreversible decisions have the highest opportunity cost
- **Sivers Focus Filter** — A practical tool for filtering out low-value opportunities
