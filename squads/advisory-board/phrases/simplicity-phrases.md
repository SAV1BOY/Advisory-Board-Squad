# Simplicity Phrases

Phrases for cutting complexity, eliminating waste, and sharpening focus. Use when the organization is spread too thin, processes are bloated, or priorities have multiplied beyond capacity.

## Challenging Complexity

1. **"If we had to cut our priorities to three, which three would survive?"**
   Use to force-rank when the priority list has grown beyond what the organization can execute.

2. **"What would we stop doing if we were being honest about our capacity?"**
   Use to surface the uncomfortable truth that doing more requires doing less of something else.

3. **"Is this complexity serving the customer, or is it serving us?"**
   Use to distinguish between necessary complexity (multi-product for customer needs) and unnecessary complexity (internal politics, legacy processes).

4. **"Explain this to me like I have no context. If you can't simplify it, you don't understand it well enough."**
   Use when a proposal is buried in jargon and layers. Forced simplification reveals whether the idea is sound.

5. **"What is the simplest version of this that could work?"**
   Use to strip a proposal to its minimum viable form. Add complexity only when the simple version demonstrably fails.

## Cutting What Doesn't Matter

6. **"If this didn't exist today, would we create it? If not, why are we maintaining it?"**
   Use to test legacy products, processes, and teams. The answer reveals whether inertia is driving the decision.

7. **"What is the cost of maintaining this? Not just the budget — the attention, the meetings, the cognitive load."**
   Use to surface the hidden costs of complexity. Budget is the visible cost; attention is the expensive one.

8. **"Hell yes, or no. If this isn't a clear yes, it's a no."**
   Use as a binary filter for new commitments. The middle ground — "sure, why not" — is where capacity dies.

9. **"Every time we add something, we should remove something. What are we removing?"**
   Use to enforce a one-in, one-out discipline. Without it, complexity only grows.

10. **"Name the three things we are NOT going to do this quarter. And mean it."**
    Use to make the anti-priorities explicit and public. The not-doing list is more important than the to-do list.

## Simplifying Communication

11. **"That was a 20-minute explanation for a 2-minute point. What is the one thing you want us to know?"**
    Use to compress verbose communication. Respectful but direct.

12. **"Can you put that in one sentence?"**
    Use to test whether the speaker has clarity. If it can't be said in one sentence, the thinking isn't finished.

13. **"I've read 30 pages and I don't know what you want me to decide. What's the ask?"**
    Use to push back on pre-reads that bury the lead. Documents serve the reader, not the author.

14. **"What would this meeting look like if it were 30 minutes instead of 60?"**
    Use to test whether meeting duration is justified. Most meetings expand to fill the time allotted, not the time needed.

15. **"We have 10 dashboards. Which one would you look at if you could only look at one?"**
    Use to identify the metric that actually matters. If the answer is "I need all 10," the measurement system is too complex.

## Focusing Execution

16. **"Finish something before starting something new."**
    Use to combat the addiction to starting. Starting is exciting; finishing is where value is created.

17. **"What is the one thing that, if we got it right, would make the other five things easier or unnecessary?"**
    Use to identify the leverage point. The highest-leverage action is rarely the most obvious one.

18. **"Are we spreading peanut butter or making a strategic bet?"**
    Use when resources are distributed thinly across many initiatives. Spreading resources evenly is the default, not a strategy.

19. **"Every project we have is 'high priority.' When everything is high priority, nothing is."**
    Use to call out priority inflation. Force the ranking. Someone has to be #4.

20. **"We can do three of these five things well, or all five of them badly. Which three?"**
    Use to make the trade-off explicit. The illusion of doing everything is more dangerous than the discipline of choosing.

## Making Simplicity Stick

21. **"Complexity is the default. Simplicity requires constant, deliberate effort."**
    Use as a reminder that organizations naturally drift toward complexity. Simplification is an active practice, not a one-time event.

22. **"The goal is not to do more. The goal is to do the right things, completely."**
    Use to reframe productivity from volume to value. Fewer things done well beats many things done poorly.

23. **"What would our best customer say if they saw how we spend our time internally?"**
    Use to provide an external perspective on internal complexity. Customers don't care about your org chart.
