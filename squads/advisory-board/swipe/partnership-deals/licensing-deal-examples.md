# Licensing & IP Deal Examples

## Overview

Licensing and intellectual property deals can unlock revenue from existing assets, expand market reach without capital expenditure, and create strategic moats. These examples illustrate common structures, negotiation leverage points, and pitfalls.

## Deal Structure 1: Technology Out-Licensing

### Example: Qualcomm's Patent Licensing Model

**Structure:** Qualcomm licenses its wireless technology patents to device manufacturers on a per-unit royalty basis, typically calculated as a percentage of the device's wholesale price.

**Key terms:**
- Royalty rate: 3-5% of device wholesale price
- Minimum annual guarantees regardless of unit volume
- Cross-licensing provisions with major manufacturers
- Patent portfolio licensed as a bundle, not individual patents

**Why it works:**
- Creates recurring revenue stream independent of Qualcomm's own chip sales
- Licensing revenue carries near-100% gross margins
- Portfolio bundling prevents cherry-picking of individual patents
- Per-device pricing aligns Qualcomm's revenue with market growth

**Advisory insight:** When your IP is foundational to an industry standard, per-unit royalties on the end product capture more value than flat licensing fees.

## Deal Structure 2: Brand Licensing

### Example: Disney Character Licensing Program

**Structure:** Disney licenses characters and brands to manufacturers across categories (toys, apparel, home goods) in exchange for royalties and creative control.

**Key terms:**
- Royalty rates: 8-15% of wholesale depending on category and brand tier
- Strict creative approval process for all licensed products
- Territory and channel exclusivity provisions
- Minimum marketing spend commitments from licensees
- Performance thresholds that trigger termination rights

**Why it works:**
- Extends brand reach without Disney bearing manufacturing or distribution risk
- Creative control provisions protect brand equity
- Exclusivity creates competition among potential licensees, driving better terms
- Licensed products serve as marketing for Disney's core entertainment business

**Advisory insight:** Brand licensing only works when the licensor maintains rigorous quality control. Revenue from licensing must never compromise brand equity.

## Deal Structure 3: Software/SaaS Licensing

### Example: Enterprise Software OEM Licensing

**Structure:** Software company licenses its technology to a larger platform company for embedding in the platform's product suite.

**Typical terms:**
- Upfront license fee: $2-10M depending on scope
- Per-seat or per-transaction royalties for ongoing usage
- Source code escrow for business continuity
- Exclusivity window: 12-24 months in defined market segment
- Joint development commitments for integration features
- Most-favored-nation pricing clauses

**Key negotiation points:**
- **Scope definition** — Precisely define what "the licensed technology" includes and excludes
- **Derivative works** — Who owns improvements made during integration?
- **Competitive restrictions** — How broad is the non-compete, and does it limit the licensor's growth?
- **Audit rights** — Licensor must be able to verify usage-based royalty calculations
- **Termination and wind-down** — What happens to end users if the deal ends?

## Deal Structure 4: University/Research IP Licensing

### Example: Stanford's Google PageRank License

**Structure:** Stanford licensed the PageRank patent to Google in exchange for equity and royalties.

**Terms:**
- 1.8 million shares of Google pre-IPO stock
- Exclusive license for search engine application
- Stanford retained rights for academic and research use
- License reverted if commercialization milestones not met

**Why it was exceptional:**
- Equity component captured upside that fixed royalties would have missed
- Exclusive license gave Google competitive protection during critical growth phase
- Reversion clause protected Stanford if Google failed to commercialize
- Stanford's $336M return on the equity became a model for university tech transfer

**Advisory insight:** When licensing breakthrough technology to a startup, equity participation captures value that royalty rates cannot predict.

## Licensing Deal Red Flags

1. **Overly broad exclusivity** — Locks out future opportunities without adequate compensation
2. **Ambiguous scope** — Undefined boundaries lead to disputes about what is covered
3. **No audit rights** — Royalty-based deals without verification are based on trust alone
4. **Perpetual terms without review** — Market conditions change; deals should include renegotiation triggers
5. **Missing termination provisions** — Every deal needs a clear exit path for both parties

## Advisory Principles

- "License the asset, not the business. If the deal constrains your strategic freedom, it is a sale disguised as a license."
- "Royalty rates matter less than royalty bases. 5% of the right number beats 15% of the wrong one."
- "The best licensing deals make both parties more competitive against shared rivals."
