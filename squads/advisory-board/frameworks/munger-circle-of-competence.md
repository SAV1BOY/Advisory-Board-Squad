# Munger Circle of Competence

## Purpose

The Circle of Competence framework, articulated by Charlie Munger and Warren Buffett, provides a discipline for knowing the boundaries of your knowledge and staying within them for high-stakes decisions. The key insight: knowing what you don't know is more valuable than what you know. Success comes not from having the largest circle of competence but from accurately knowing where your circle ends and never making critical decisions outside it without appropriate help.

## When to Use

- Before giving advice on topics where your expertise may be insufficient
- When deciding whether to invest in, advise, or operate in a new domain
- When building advisory boards (ensuring coverage of critical competence areas)
- When a founder is entering a market or technology space they don't deeply understand
- When evaluating whether to make a decision yourself or defer to someone with deeper expertise
- During personal development planning to identify competence gaps

## Core Model

### The Three Zones

**Inside the Circle**: Topics where you have deep, earned knowledge through years of experience, study, and feedback. You understand the nuances, the exceptions, and the second-order effects. You can identify what you don't know within the domain.

**The Edge of the Circle**: Topics where you have some knowledge but lack the depth for high-stakes decisions. You know enough to ask good questions but not enough to have reliable judgment. This is the most dangerous zone — competence here feels like expertise but isn't.

**Outside the Circle**: Topics where you have little to no earned knowledge. You are aware of your ignorance, which is actually safer than the Edge — because you know to seek help.

### How Competence Is Earned
True competence requires:
- **Years of relevant experience** (not just adjacent experience)
- **Feedback loops** (outcomes you could observe and learn from)
- **Deliberate study** (not just passive exposure)
- **Failure experience** (understanding how things go wrong, not just how they succeed)
- **Current knowledge** (competence erodes if not maintained)

### The Competence Illusion
The most dangerous state is believing your circle is larger than it is. Common causes:
- **Success in one domain** creates false confidence in another (a great investor is not automatically a great operator)
- **Reading about a topic** creates the illusion of experiential knowledge
- **Seniority** creates the expectation (from self and others) of universal competence
- **Past competence** may have decayed as the field evolved

## Steps

1. **Map your competence honestly.** List the domains relevant to your role. For each, rate your competence as Inside, Edge, or Outside. Have trusted colleagues validate your self-assessment.

2. **Define the evidence for each rating.** "Inside" requires: years of experience, demonstrated track record, current knowledge, ability to identify edge cases. If you can't cite specific evidence, you're not Inside.

3. **Identify your critical gaps.** For decisions you regularly make, are there domains where you are at the Edge or Outside? These are your vulnerability points.

4. **Build a competence complement.** Find people whose circles of competence cover your gaps. Advisors, board members, team hires, consultants — build a network that collectively covers all critical domains.

5. **Establish a "check before deciding" rule.** For any decision that touches a domain outside your circle, consult someone who is Inside before committing.

6. **Expand your circle deliberately.** Choose one area at the Edge and invest in moving it Inside through study, mentorship, and small-stakes practice. This takes years, not weeks.

7. **Maintain your circle.** Competence decays without maintenance. Stay current in your core domains through continuous learning and practice.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Be explicit about where your competence lies and where it doesn't. An advisor who says "I don't know enough about this to advise" is more valuable than one who opines on everything.
- Help assemble advisory boards with complementary circles. If all advisors have the same circle, the board has blind spots. Map circles to ensure coverage.
- When advising outside your circle, flag it: "I'm outside my circle of competence here, so take this as a question rather than a recommendation."

### For Founders
- Your circle of competence is usually narrower than you think. First-time founders are especially prone to overestimating their competence in domains they've never operated in.
- Hire for competence gaps. Your first hires should cover the critical domains outside your circle — usually one of: engineering, sales, finance, or operations.
- Knowing your circle is a strategic asset. It tells you where you need advisors, where you need to hire, and where you should not be making decisions alone.

### For Investment Decisions
- Never invest in businesses you don't understand. This is the Buffett-Munger discipline. Expanding your investment circle is fine; investing outside it is not.
- When evaluating startups, assess whether the founding team's collective circle covers all critical domains for the business.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Overestimating the circle.** The most common and most dangerous mistake. Dunning-Kruger effect: the less you know, the more competent you feel.

2. **Static circle assumption.** Circles expand and contract. A competence you had 10 years ago may have eroded. A field you were expert in may have transformed.

3. **Confusing intelligence with competence.** Smart people can reason about domains they lack competence in, but reasoning without domain knowledge produces plausible-sounding wrong answers.

4. **Refusing to act outside the circle ever.** The framework is not about inaction — it's about appropriate humility. You can act outside your circle if you: acknowledge the limitation, seek expert input, size the bet appropriately, and build in learning loops.

5. **Social pressure to opine.** Board members, advisors, and executives face constant pressure to have opinions on everything. Resist. "I don't know" is the most honest and most useful answer when you're outside your circle.

## Related Frameworks

- **Munger Latticework of Models** — The latticework expands your circle; the circle tells you where the latticework is strongest
- **Dalio Believability-Weighted Voting** — Believability is competence applied to decisions
- **Munger Checklist for Rational Thinking** — The checklist compensates for competence gaps
- **Base Rates and Reference Class** — Base rates are especially important outside your circle
- **Board Charter Framework** — Boards should be designed to cover all critical competence areas
- **Stakeholder Mapping Framework** — Identify competence needs across stakeholder domains
