# Authority Bias in Boardrooms

## Overview
Authority bias is the tendency to attribute greater accuracy and weight to the opinions of authority figures, regardless of the substance of their claims. In boardrooms—where power dynamics, status, and expertise hierarchies are pronounced—authority bias can silently distort every decision.

## How It Manifests

### The CEO Effect
- Board members defer to the CEO's assessment of the business because the CEO "knows more"
- **Reality:** The CEO knows more about operations but may be biased about strategy, risk, and their own performance

### The Lead Investor Effect
- When a prominent VC or investor speaks, other board members align, especially on financial matters
- **Reality:** Investment expertise doesn't automatically transfer to operational or strategic judgment

### The Domain Expert Effect
- When one board member has deep domain expertise, others defer on adjacent topics where the expertise doesn't apply
- **Reality:** Being an expert in finance doesn't make you an expert in product strategy

### The Credentials Effect
- Board members from prestigious institutions (Harvard, McKinsey, Goldman) receive unwarranted deference
- **Reality:** Credentials correlate with competence but don't guarantee it in any specific context

## Structural Countermeasures

1. **Reverse seniority speaking order** — Junior or less-experienced members speak first to prevent authority anchoring
2. **Written pre-meeting positions** — Members submit independent views before hearing authority voices
3. **Mandatory challenge** — Require at least one substantive challenge for every major proposal, regardless of who presents it
4. **Anonymous polling** — For sensitive votes, use anonymous ballots to remove social pressure
5. **Believability weighting** — Instead of equal votes or authority-based deference, weight opinions by demonstrated track record in the relevant domain (Dalio model)
6. **External perspectives** — Periodically invite outside experts who have no relationship with board members to present independent analysis

## Board Application
- The board chair is responsible for managing authority bias dynamics in discussions
- Explicitly discuss authority bias during board orientation for new members
- Track speaking time by member: if one person consistently dominates, authority bias (or its cousin, deference) is likely at play
- Create a culture where challenging authority is rewarded, not punished
