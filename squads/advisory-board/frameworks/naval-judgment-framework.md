# Naval Judgment Framework

## Purpose

Naval Ravikant identifies judgment as the most valuable skill in business — the ability to make decisions with incomplete information that produce good long-term outcomes. Judgment cannot be taught directly but can be developed through experience, mental models, and deliberate practice. This framework provides the principles and practices for developing and applying superior judgment in advisory and leadership contexts.

## When to Use

- When facing decisions with incomplete information and high stakes
- When developing decision-making capability in yourself or others
- When evaluating leaders and founders (judgment is the #1 hiring criterion for senior roles)
- When the analytical frameworks produce ambiguous results and a judgment call is needed
- When speed of decision matters and perfect information is unavailable

## Core Model

### What Judgment Is
Judgment is the ability to make correct decisions in novel situations with incomplete information. It is distinct from intelligence (processing speed), knowledge (information stored), and skill (repeatable technique). Judgment integrates all three into applied wisdom.

### Naval's Principles of Good Judgment

**1. Clear thinking requires intellectual honesty.** The biggest barrier to judgment is self-deception. You must want to find the truth more than you want to be right.

**2. The best judgment comes from compounding experience.** Each decision made and reflected upon improves future decisions. This is why experienced founders outperform first-timers even with worse ideas.

**3. Mental models are the tools of judgment.** The more models you have, the more angles you can analyze from, the better your judgment. But models must be deeply understood, not superficially collected.

**4. Judgment means knowing the long-term consequences of actions.** Short-term thinking is easy. Judgment is seeing the second, third, and tenth-order effects.

**5. The best judgment is quiet and confident.** People with good judgment don't need to convince loudly. Their track record speaks.

### Judgment Development Path
1. **Read broadly** across disciplines to build mental model inventory
2. **Decide frequently** with small stakes to build the judgment muscle
3. **Reflect ruthlessly** on outcomes — what did you get right, what did you get wrong, and why?
4. **Seek feedback** from people whose judgment you respect
5. **Track your predictions** to calibrate confidence levels

## Steps

1. **Gather information efficiently.** Don't seek perfect information — seek the minimum information needed for a directionally correct decision. Diminishing returns set in fast.

2. **Apply relevant mental models.** Which 2-3 models from your latticework illuminate this situation? Apply them independently, then synthesize.

3. **Consider the long-term.** Project the decision forward 1 year, 5 years, 10 years. Which option produces the best long-term outcome?

4. **Check for emotional distortion.** Are you afraid? Excited? Angry? Emotions are data but not decisions. Acknowledge them, then set them aside.

5. **Make the call.** Decide. The cost of indecision is often greater than the cost of a wrong decision.

6. **Record and review.** Write down your reasoning, confidence level, and expected outcome. Review against actual outcome to calibrate.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Judgment is the most valuable thing you offer — not connections, not industry knowledge, but the ability to help founders make better decisions. Develop it deliberately.
- When advising, separate information from judgment. Share information freely, but label judgment clearly: "Based on my experience, I believe..."
- Help founders develop their own judgment rather than creating dependency on yours.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing confidence with judgment.** Confident people are often wrong. Good judgment includes knowing when you don't know.
2. **Not tracking decisions.** Without a record, you cannot calibrate. Keep a decision journal.
3. **Overthinking.** Good judgment is often fast. If you're agonizing, you may be optimizing the wrong variable.
4. **Ignoring emotional signals.** Emotions contain information. The key is not ignoring them but not being controlled by them.
