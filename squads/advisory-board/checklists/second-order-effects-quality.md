# Second-Order Effects Quality Checklist

## Purpose
Verify that decisions are evaluated beyond their immediate impact. First-order thinking asks "what happens next?" Second-order thinking asks "and then what?" This checklist ensures the advisory board consistently pushes past surface-level analysis to identify cascading consequences, feedback loops, and unintended outcomes.

## When to Use
- Before approving any strategic initiative, partnership, or organizational change.
- When reviewing decision memos that involve cross-functional impact.
- During post-mortems where outcomes diverged significantly from expectations.
- When a proposed action feels "obvious" and no one is raising concerns.

## Gate Criteria

### Causal Chain Mapping
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The decision memo explicitly traces at least two levels of consequences beyond the immediate outcome.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Each second-order effect identifies who is affected (customers, employees, competitors, regulators, partners).
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Feedback loops are identified where the outcome of an action changes the conditions that created the need for the action.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Time horizons are specified for when second-order effects are expected to materialize (30 days, 90 days, 12 months).

### Stakeholder Impact Analysis
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Impact on employees beyond the directly affected team is assessed (morale, precedent-setting, workload redistribution).
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Customer behavior changes are modeled, not assumed to remain static.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Competitive response is anticipated with at least two plausible scenarios.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Regulatory or legal ripple effects are evaluated where the decision touches compliance boundaries.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Supplier or partner dependency shifts are identified.

### Systemic Risk
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The analysis identifies whether this decision creates concentration risk or single points of failure.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The interaction between this decision and other in-flight initiatives is mapped.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The analysis considers what happens if the decision succeeds wildly (not just if it fails).
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Market-level effects are considered for decisions that affect pricing, supply, or competitive dynamics.

### Mitigation and Monitoring
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Leading indicators for negative second-order effects are identified with monitoring owners.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Tripwires are defined that would trigger a reversal or modification of the decision.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: A review date is set specifically to assess whether second-order effects are materializing as predicted.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Contingency plans exist for the top three most damaging second-order scenarios.

### Intellectual Honesty
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The analysis distinguishes between known second-order effects and speculative ones.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: Assumptions underlying the second-order analysis are stated explicitly.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: At least one person with a different functional perspective has reviewed the analysis.
- [ ] **Pass/Fail**: The analysis avoids optimism bias by giving equal weight to negative second-order effects.

## Red Flags
- The proposal claims there are no significant second-order effects.
- Analysis only considers positive cascading outcomes.
- Second-order effects are described in vague language ("may impact culture") without specifics.
- The same person who proposed the action also conducted the second-order analysis with no independent review.
- Historical precedents with known second-order problems are ignored.
- The analysis treats competitors, customers, and employees as passive actors who will not respond.

## Escalation Triggers
- A material negative outcome occurs that was a predictable second-order effect but was not identified in the original analysis.
- Multiple board members independently flag missing second-order considerations.
- The second-order analysis was skipped or treated as a formality for an irreversible decision.
- Post-mortem reveals a pattern of consistently missing the same category of second-order effects.
