# Sivers Directness Framework

## Purpose

Derek Sivers advocates for radical directness in communication and action. Say what you mean. Do what you say. Don't hint — state. Don't imply — ask. This framework provides practical guidance for replacing indirect communication patterns with direct ones, improving clarity, reducing misunderstandings, and accelerating decision-making.

## When to Use

- When communication is failing due to ambiguity or indirectness
- When cultural norms of politeness are preventing honest feedback
- When a founder needs to deliver difficult messages to team, investors, or customers
- When meetings produce vague commitments instead of clear decisions
- When designing communication norms for teams

## Core Model

### Directness Principles
1. **Say the thing.** If you're thinking it, say it. Unspoken thoughts are unactionable.
2. **Ask for what you need.** Don't hint. Don't hope they'll figure it out. State it plainly.
3. **Mean what you say.** Don't say yes when you mean no. Don't say "maybe" when you mean "never."
4. **Accept direct communication from others.** If you punish directness, you train people to be indirect.
5. **Short is better than long.** Every additional word dilutes the message. Be concise.

### Direct vs. Indirect

| Indirect | Direct |
|----------|--------|
| "We should probably think about..." | "I recommend we do X by Y date." |
| "I'm not sure this is the best approach..." | "This approach will fail because X. I suggest Y instead." |
| "Let me think about it" (meaning no) | "No, and here's why." |
| "That's an interesting idea" (meaning I disagree) | "I disagree because X." |

### The Directness Paradox
Directness feels risky but is actually safer:
- Indirect communication creates ambiguity → misaligned expectations → conflict
- Direct communication creates clarity → aligned expectations → less conflict
- Short-term discomfort from directness prevents long-term damage from misunderstanding

## Steps

1. **Identify where you are indirect.** In what situations do you soften, hint, or avoid saying what you mean?
2. **Practice direct alternatives.** Rewrite your indirect messages as direct ones. Compare.
3. **Start with low-stakes directness.** Practice being direct in routine communications before high-stakes ones.
4. **Create direct communication norms.** Make directness a stated team value.
5. **Model receiving directness well.** When someone is direct with you, thank them. Don't punish honesty.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Be direct with founders. Indirect advisory feedback wastes everyone's time and fails to deliver value.
- Help founders develop directness as a leadership skill. Founders who cannot deliver clear, direct messages create organizational confusion.
- Apply the Brene Brown principle: "Clear is kind. Unclear is unkind."

## Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing directness with rudeness.** Direct communication can be warm and caring. It just doesn't hide the message.
2. **Being direct only downward.** If you're only direct with subordinates but indirect with peers and superiors, it's domination, not communication.
3. **Overcorrecting.** Going from always indirect to always blunt without regard for context. Directness still requires emotional intelligence.
