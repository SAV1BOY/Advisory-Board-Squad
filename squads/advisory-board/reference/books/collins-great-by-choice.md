# Great by Choice — Jim Collins & Morten Hansen

## Summary

Collins and Hansen study companies that thrived in chaotic, uncertain environments—what they call 10Xers (companies that outperformed their industry by 10x over 15+ years). The central question: why do some companies thrive in uncertainty while others don't? The answer is not luck, not bold risk-taking, not visionary genius. It's a combination of fanatical discipline (the 20-Mile March), empirical creativity (fire bullets then cannonballs), and productive paranoia (always preparing for the worst). The book directly challenges the myth that success in turbulent times requires bold, aggressive risk-taking.

## Key Principles

- **The 20-Mile March** — Consistent performance targets hit in good times and bad. Don't overextend in booms or shrink in busts. Discipline trumps brilliance.
- **Fire bullets, then cannonballs** — Test ideas cheaply (bullets) before committing major resources (cannonballs). Empirical validation before big bets.
- **Leading above the death line** — Productive paranoia means always maintaining sufficient reserves (cash, margin, time) to survive worst-case scenarios.
- **SMaC recipe** — Specific, Methodical, and Consistent operating practices that remain stable for years. Change your SMaC recipe only when empirical evidence demands it.
- **Return on Luck (ROL)** — Lucky and unlucky events happen to all companies equally. 10Xers don't get more luck; they extract more value from luck events through preparation and speed of execution.
- **Zoom out, then zoom in** — In crisis: first zoom out to assess the full situation and possible scenarios, then zoom in on the specific actions required. Panic is the enemy.

## Frameworks Extracted

1. **The 20-Mile March Design** — (1) Set a performance floor you will meet regardless of conditions, (2) Set a performance ceiling you won't exceed even in great conditions, (3) The march must be under your control, (4) Must be relevant to your specific context.
2. **Bullets-to-Cannonballs Protocol** — (1) Fire low-cost, low-risk experiments, (2) Evaluate which experiments hit targets, (3) Concentrate resources behind validated successes (cannonballs), (4) Never fire uncalibrated cannonballs (big bets without empirical evidence).
3. **The Death Line Audit** — (1) What are our top three existential risks? (2) What reserves do we maintain against each? (3) Are we above or below the death line? (4) What would push us below it? Conduct quarterly.
4. **The SMaC Recipe Template** — Document 10-15 specific operating practices that define how the company wins. These should be durable (10+ year shelf life), specific (not vague), and empirically validated. Change only with overwhelming evidence.

## Quotable Lines

- "The signature of mediocrity is not an unwillingness to change. The signature of mediocrity is chronic inconsistency."
- "10Xers don't have better luck. They get a better return on luck."
- "The greatest danger is not failure but success without understanding why you succeeded."
- "Discipline, in essence, is consistency of action."
- "Fire bullets first. Cannonballs later. And never fire uncalibrated cannonballs."

## Application to Board Decisions

- **Capital allocation** — Apply the bullets-to-cannonballs framework to investment decisions. Require small-scale validation before approving major capital expenditures.
- **Risk governance** — Conduct the Death Line Audit at every board meeting. Ensure the company maintains sufficient reserves to survive plausible worst cases.
- **Performance expectations** — Set 20-Mile March expectations for the CEO. Reward consistent performance, not heroic surges followed by collapses.
- **Strategic consistency** — Review the company's SMaC recipe annually. If it keeps changing, the company lacks strategic identity. If it never changes despite market shifts, it's becoming rigid.
- **Crisis governance** — When a crisis hits, apply zoom out/zoom in. The board should resist the urge to make dramatic moves before fully assessing the landscape.
