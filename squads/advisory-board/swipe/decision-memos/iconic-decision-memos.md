# Iconic Decision Memos

## Overview
The most consequential business decisions in history were often captured in written memos that crystallized the reasoning, the risks, and the conviction behind the choice. These swipe examples illustrate what great decision documentation looks like.

## Amazon's Decision to Launch AWS (2003-2006)

### The Memo Pattern
- **Context:** Amazon had built massive internal infrastructure to support its e-commerce platform. The insight: other companies need this infrastructure too.
- **The contrarian element:** A retailer becoming an infrastructure company. Wall Street didn't understand it. Internal teams questioned the distraction from retail.
- **Key reasoning:** (1) We've already built it, (2) The market need is enormous, (3) Our operational expertise is transferable, (4) Infrastructure has higher margins than retail, (5) It creates a flywheel—AWS customers become Amazon retail partners.
- **The decision:** Invest hundreds of millions in building a cloud computing platform despite no existing market category.
- **Outcome:** AWS became Amazon's profit engine, generating $80B+ in annual revenue.

### What Makes This Memo Great
- Identified a non-obvious insight from internal capabilities
- Clearly articulated why a retailer should build infrastructure
- Addressed the strongest counterargument (distraction from core business)
- Defined success metrics that were independent of the retail business

## Intel's Decision to Exit Memory Chips (1985)

### The Memo Pattern
- **Context:** Intel was a memory chip company facing devastating competition from Japanese manufacturers with lower costs.
- **The "revolving door" test:** Andy Grove asked Gordon Moore: "If we got kicked out and the board brought in a new CEO, what would he do?" Moore answered: "He would get us out of memories." Grove said: "Then why shouldn't you and I walk out that door, come back in, and do it ourselves?"
- **Key reasoning:** (1) Memory was becoming a commodity, (2) Japanese competitors had structural cost advantages, (3) Microprocessors were high-margin and defensible, (4) Emotional attachment to memory was the only reason to stay.
- **The decision:** Exit the business that defined Intel and bet entirely on microprocessors.
- **Outcome:** Intel became the world's most valuable semiconductor company.

### What Makes This Decision Great
- Used a mental model (the outsider test) to overcome emotional attachment
- Correctly identified the structural dynamics making the current business unwinnable
- Made a single, focused bet rather than hedging across both businesses

## Netflix's Decision to Split Streaming and DVD (2007-2011)

### The Memo Pattern
- **Context:** Netflix's DVD-by-mail business was profitable and growing. Streaming was nascent, expensive, and cannibalizing the DVD business.
- **Key reasoning:** (1) Physical media delivery is a declining technology, (2) Streaming is the future but requires different economics, (3) Maintaining both creates conflicting incentives, (4) If we don't cannibalize ourselves, someone else will.
- **The decision:** Invest aggressively in streaming even though it compressed margins and confused the market. Later attempted to separate the businesses entirely (Qwikster), which failed in execution but was correct in strategic intent.
- **Outcome:** Netflix became the dominant streaming platform globally.

### What Makes This Decision Great
- Willingness to cannibalize a profitable business for a better future position
- Recognition that the disruption would happen regardless—better to self-disrupt
- The Qwikster failure showed that even great strategic decisions can be poorly executed

## Swipe Elements for Board Memos

### The Structure of a Great Decision Memo
1. **What are we deciding?** — One sentence, unambiguous.
2. **Why now?** — What has changed that makes this decision urgent?
3. **The case for** — The strongest argument for the proposed action.
4. **The case against** — The strongest counterargument, stated fairly.
5. **What must be true** — The key assumptions underlying the decision.
6. **The decision** — Clear, specific, actionable.
7. **Reversibility** — Can we undo this if we're wrong? How quickly?
8. **Success metrics** — How will we know this worked?
