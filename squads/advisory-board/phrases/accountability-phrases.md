# Accountability Phrases

Phrases for establishing, maintaining, and enforcing accountability — without crossing into blame or micromanagement. Accountability is about ownership and follow-through, not punishment.

## Setting Expectations

1. **"To confirm: you own this. The deliverable is [X], the deadline is [Y], and you will report back to [Z]. Correct?"**
   Use to close every commitment with explicit terms. Ambiguous ownership is no ownership.

2. **"What does 'done' look like for this? I want us to agree on the definition before we start."**
   Use to prevent the "I thought you meant..." conversation. Shared definition of done is the foundation of accountability.

3. **"Is this deadline realistic? I'd rather hear 'no' now than 'it's delayed' in three weeks."**
   Use to give permission for honest timeline assessment. Unrealistic deadlines create a culture of routine failure.

4. **"What could prevent you from delivering on this? Let's name the risks now so we can mitigate them."**
   Use to surface blockers proactively. Accountability includes anticipating obstacles, not just promising outcomes.

5. **"If you need help or resources to deliver, ask now. Don't wait until the deadline to tell me you were stuck."**
   Use to establish that asking for help is a sign of accountability, not weakness.

## Checking In

6. **"Where are we on [commitment]? Not the story — the status."**
   Use when you need a direct update without narrative padding. Separates status from explanation.

7. **"You committed to [X] by [date]. It's [date]. What happened?"**
   Use when a deadline was missed. Direct, factual, not accusatory. The question creates space for an honest answer.

8. **"I see the action tracker shows this as 'in progress' for the third consecutive review. What specifically is blocking completion?"**
   Use to address chronic non-completion. Patterns deserve attention.

9. **"Are you on track to deliver by [date]? If not, I need to know today so we can adjust."**
   Use as a proactive checkpoint. Early warning is always better than late surprise.

10. **"I notice this was delegated downward. That's fine, but you are still the accountable party to this board."**
    Use when delegation is confused with abdication. Delegation transfers tasks; accountability stays.

## Addressing Misses

11. **"The commitment was [X]. The result was [Y]. Help me understand the gap."**
    Use after a miss. Open-ended enough to invite an honest explanation. Specific enough to prevent deflection.

12. **"I'm not interested in blame. I'm interested in what went wrong and what we change so it doesn't happen again."**
    Use to set the tone for a post-miss conversation. Learning over punishment.

13. **"This is the second time this commitment has slipped. What needs to change — the commitment, the resources, or the owner?"**
    Use to force a structural conversation. Repeated misses mean the system is broken, not just the execution.

14. **"I want to separate two things: the miss itself, and the fact that I wasn't informed it was going to miss. The second one concerns me more."**
    Use when the lack of early warning is the bigger issue than the miss. Transparency failures erode trust faster than execution failures.

15. **"Accountability means owning the outcome, not just the effort. I appreciate the work that went in, and the result still fell short."**
    Use to honor effort while holding the outcome standard. Both things can be true simultaneously.

## Reinforcing Accountability Culture

16. **"I want to publicly acknowledge that [name] committed to [X], delivered [X], on time. This is the standard."**
    Use to reinforce follow-through with positive recognition. Accountability culture is built more by celebrating delivery than by punishing misses.

17. **"In this board, a commitment is a commitment. If you're not sure you can deliver, say 'I'll try' or 'I'll investigate.' Don't say 'I will' unless you mean it."**
    Use to calibrate the weight of verbal commitments. In high-accountability cultures, words matter.

18. **"Who is accountable for this? Not who is involved — who is the single person whose name goes next to this outcome?"**
    Use to prevent shared accountability, which is no accountability. One name per outcome.

19. **"Our action item completion rate is [X]%. That's [above/below] our target. Here's what we're going to do about it."**
    Use to make accountability systemic, not personal. Track it, report it, act on it.

20. **"The best way to build trust on this board is to do what you said you would do, when you said you would do it."**
    Use as a cultural statement. Simple, memorable, and true.

## Self-Accountability

21. **"I committed to [X] and I didn't deliver. Here's what happened, and here's what I'm doing to fix it."**
    Use to model accountability from the Chair or any leader. Accountability flows from the top.

22. **"I owe you an apology. I said [X] and I did [Y]. That's not the standard I hold myself to."**
    Use when your own behavior didn't match your words. Self-accountability builds the right to hold others accountable.

23. **"Let me update my commitment: the original timeline was [X], the new timeline is [Y], and here's why."**
    Use to revise commitments openly rather than silently sliding deadlines. Transparent revision is accountability; silent extension is not.
