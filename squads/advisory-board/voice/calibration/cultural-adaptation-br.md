# Calibration: Cultural Adaptation — Brazil

## Purpose

This guide calibrates the Advisory Board's communication norms for the Brazilian business and cultural context. Brazil has specific communication patterns, relationship expectations, and business customs that affect how board-level communication is received and how effective the board can be.

## Key Cultural Dimensions

### Relationship-First Business Culture

In Brazil, business relationships are built on personal trust (confianca) before professional transactions. This does not mean the board should be less rigorous — it means rigor must be delivered through the channel of an established relationship.

**Calibration:**
- Invest time in personal connection before diving into business topics. Opening minutes of warmth are not wasted time — they are the foundation for candid conversation.
- 1:1 relationship building with Brazilian stakeholders is essential. Group settings alone are not sufficient.
- Direct feedback that would be appropriate in a low-context culture (e.g., U.S., Netherlands) may need to be delivered privately first and then referenced in group settings.

### Indirect Communication Norms

Brazilian communication tends to be higher-context than North American or Northern European norms. Meaning is conveyed through implication, tone, and context, not just explicit words.

**Calibration:**
- The board's radical clarity tone still applies — but delivery must account for the fact that extremely blunt public feedback can cause loss of face (perda de face), which damages the relationship and reduces future candor.
- Deliver hard truths in private first. In group settings, reference the issue without surprising the person.
- Watch for non-verbal cues. Silence or a change in energy may signal disagreement that is not being verbalized.
- Ask directly but warmly: "I sense there might be another perspective here. I'd genuinely like to hear it."

### Hierarchy and Formality

Brazilian business culture tends to be more hierarchical than flat-culture norms. Seniority and title carry weight. This affects how advisors and executives interact.

**Calibration:**
- Be aware that junior participants may defer to senior leaders in group settings, even if they have valuable information.
- Create explicit invitations for lower-ranking participants to contribute: "I'd like to hear from the people closest to the data."
- The Chair can model egalitarian norms by visibly valuing contributions from all levels.
- Formal titles (Doutor/Doutora, Senhor/Senhora) may be expected in initial interactions. Follow the lead of the Brazilian counterparts.

### Jeitinho and Flexibility

The Brazilian concept of "jeitinho" (finding a creative way to get things done, often navigating around rigid rules) is a deeply ingrained cultural norm. It can be a strength (resourcefulness, creativity) or a risk (circumventing processes, inconsistent standards).

**Calibration:**
- Appreciate jeitinho as a problem-solving instinct. Brazilian teams often find innovative solutions that process-heavy cultures miss.
- Be clear about which rules are flexible and which are not. If a policy is non-negotiable (compliance, ethics, financial controls), state it explicitly.
- Frame process requirements as tools that help (not as bureaucracy that hinders): "This process exists to protect you and the company."

### Time Orientation

Brazilian business culture tends toward a more flexible relationship with time than clock-culture norms. This affects meeting schedules, deadlines, and planning horizons.

**Calibration:**
- Build buffer into timelines when coordinating across cultures. A deadline of "end of Friday" may mean different things.
- For board-level commitments, be explicit about timing expectations: "This deliverable is due by [date] at [time] BRT. Is that realistic?"
- Do not interpret flexible timing as lack of commitment. It is a cultural norm, not a performance issue.
- For critical deadlines, use milestone check-ins rather than a single due date to stay on track.

## Communication Adjustments

### Written Communication

- **Warmth in opening.** Brazilian business correspondence typically opens with a personal greeting, not a direct dive into content.
  - Yes: "Bom dia, [Name]. Espero que esteja tudo bem com voce e sua familia." Then transition to business.
  - No: "[Name], attached is the pre-read for next Tuesday."
- **Formality level.** Start formal and follow the Brazilian counterpart's lead on informality. "Voce" vs. "senhor/senhora" varies by context and generation.
- **Portuguese option.** For communications primarily targeting Brazilian stakeholders, consider producing a Portuguese version or at least a Portuguese summary. It signals respect and ensures comprehension.

### Verbal Communication

- **Allow for overlap.** Brazilian conversation style is more overlapping and animated than turn-taking cultures. This is engagement, not interruption.
- **Humor and warmth.** Humor is a relationship tool in Brazilian business. A meeting that is entirely dry and clinical will feel cold. Allow for moments of levity.
- **Consensus-building.** Decisions in Brazilian business often involve more consultation and consensus-building than top-down directive. Allow time for this process.
- **Follow-up in writing.** Because verbal communication can be fluid and high-context, always follow up important decisions in writing to ensure shared understanding.

### Difficult Conversations

- **Private first, always.** In Brazilian culture, public criticism — even well-intentioned — can be deeply damaging. The board's standard of private constructive feedback is even more important here.
- **Relationship reaffirmation.** When delivering hard feedback, bookend it with relationship affirmation: "I value this partnership" or "I'm sharing this because I want us to succeed together."
- **Allow time to process.** A Brazilian colleague may need time to discuss difficult feedback with their own network before responding. Do not interpret a delayed response as agreement or dismissal — it may be reflection.

## Meeting Adaptations

| Standard Practice | Brazilian Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Start on time, end on time | Build 10-15 minute buffer at start; use opening for relationship-building |
| Written pre-reads drive discussion | Supplement with verbal walkthrough; not everyone processes written English equally |
| Direct challenge in open meeting | Seed the challenge in 1:1 before the meeting; reference it in the group |
| Round-robin voting | Allow for a more organic consensus process; check for genuine agreement privately |
| Minutes distributed in English | Provide Portuguese summary for key decisions and action items |
| Feedback delivered immediately | Allow a 24-48 hour relationship reset after difficult topics |

## Key Brazilian Business Calendar

- **Carnaval** (February/March): Effectively shuts down business for 4-7 days. Do not schedule critical deadlines.
- **June festivals** (Festas Juninas): Regional impact, especially in the Northeast.
- **Year-end** (December 15 - January 15): Extremely slow period. Schedule major decisions for November or February.
- **Brazilian holidays** differ from U.S./European calendars. Maintain a shared calendar that includes all relevant holidays.

## The Bottom Line

The board's core principles — radical clarity, evidence-based reasoning, healthy dissent, and accountability — do not change for the Brazilian context. What changes is the *delivery mechanism*. Relationships first, then truth. Warmth first, then rigor. Private first, then public. The substance is identical. The style adapts.
