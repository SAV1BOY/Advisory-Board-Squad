# Case Study: Crisis Leadership

## Title: How a CEO Navigated a Product Safety Crisis Without Losing the Company

## Situation

A consumer hardware company ("DeviceCo") discovered that a component in its flagship product had a defect that could cause overheating under specific conditions. The defect had been present in approximately 180,000 units shipped over the previous 8 months.

**Key facts at the moment of discovery:**
- No injuries had been reported, but internal testing confirmed the risk was real
- The defective component was supplied by a third-party vendor
- A voluntary recall would cost an estimated $12M (representing 40% of the company's cash reserves)
- The company had recently closed a Series B and was in conversations with potential Series C investors
- Two employees had flagged the overheating issue 3 months earlier; their reports had been deprioritized

## The Crisis Response

### Hour 0-6: Assessment and Mobilization

**CEO's first decisions:**
1. Halted all shipments of the affected product immediately
2. Assembled a crisis team: CEO, General Counsel, VP Engineering, VP Operations, Head of Communications
3. Engaged outside counsel specializing in product liability
4. Commissioned an independent engineering assessment of the defect severity

**What the CEO did NOT do:**
- Did not wait for "more data" before stopping shipments
- Did not attempt to determine fault (vendor vs. internal) before taking protective action
- Did not consult investors before acting — safety first, financial implications second

### Day 1-3: Investigation and Planning

**Engineering assessment results:**
- Defect probability of causing thermal event: approximately 0.3% under stress conditions
- Risk category: low probability but potentially high severity
- Root cause: vendor component specification did not match agreed tolerances

**Decision: Voluntary recall**
The CEO decided to issue a voluntary recall despite the low probability, reasoning:
- "If one customer is harmed, the probability was 100% for them"
- A proactive recall would be viewed more favorably by regulators and customers than a reactive one
- The company's brand was built on trust and product quality; concealment would destroy the foundation

**Recall logistics:**
- Free replacement unit shipped to every affected customer
- Prepaid return packaging for defective units
- Dedicated support line with extended hours
- Full refund option for customers who preferred not to wait for replacement

### Day 3-7: Communication

**Stakeholder communication sequence:**

1. **Employees (Day 3, morning):** All-hands meeting. CEO explained the defect, the decision to recall, the financial impact, and asked for everyone's help. Acknowledged the earlier internal reports and committed to fixing the process that deprioritized them.

2. **Board and investors (Day 3, afternoon):** Emergency board call. Presented the facts, the recall decision, the cost estimate, and the plan. No investor pushed back on the recall decision.

3. **Customers (Day 4):** Direct email to every affected customer from the CEO personally. Clear, plain-language explanation of the issue, the risk, and the remedy. No legal hedging, no blame-shifting to the vendor.

4. **Media (Day 4):** Proactive press release timed to go out simultaneously with the customer email. CEO made herself available for interviews.

5. **Regulators (Day 4):** Formal notification to CPSC with full documentation of the defect, affected units, and recall plan.

### Week 2-8: Execution

**Recall execution:**
- 94% of affected units were returned or replaced within 60 days
- Customer support team handled 12,000+ inquiries with an average satisfaction score of 4.6/5
- Zero injuries reported throughout the recall period
- The replacement product included an upgraded component that improved overall product quality

**Vendor accountability:**
- Terminated the relationship with the defective component supplier
- Implemented incoming quality inspection for all safety-critical components
- Negotiated $4M in damages from the vendor to partially offset recall costs

### Month 3-6: Recovery and Rebuilding

**What the CEO did to rebuild:**
- Published a transparent post-mortem on the company blog detailing the root cause, the response, and the process changes implemented
- Implemented a "safety concern escalation" policy requiring that any employee-reported safety issue reach the VP Engineering within 24 hours
- Hired a dedicated quality assurance lead (a new role)
- Made the recall response a case study in new employee onboarding

## The Outcome

### Financial Impact
- Total recall cost: $14M (slightly above the $12M estimate)
- Revenue impact: 2-month sales pause during recall, approximately $6M in delayed revenue
- Net financial impact: approximately $20M over 12 months

### Strategic Impact
- Customer NPS increased by 15 points in the quarter following the recall (trust effect)
- The Series C round closed 4 months later at a higher valuation than pre-crisis projections
- Lead Series C investor cited the crisis response as a positive signal about leadership quality
- Zero product liability lawsuits filed
- Media coverage was overwhelmingly positive, framing DeviceCo as a model for responsible corporate behavior

### Cultural Impact
- The two employees who originally flagged the issue were publicly recognized
- "Escalate safety concerns immediately" became a core operational principle
- The crisis response became a point of pride within the company and a recruiting advantage

## Principles Demonstrated

### 1. Speed Beats Perfection in Crisis
The CEO stopped shipments within hours, before the full investigation was complete. Waiting for perfect information would have meant more defective units in customers' hands.

### 2. Own the Narrative
By communicating proactively — to employees, customers, media, and regulators — the CEO controlled the story. Companies that wait for journalists to break the news lose narrative control permanently.

### 3. Customer Safety is Not a Financial Decision
The CEO explicitly refused to frame the recall as a cost-benefit analysis. "If one customer is harmed, the probability was 100% for them" reframed the decision from financial to ethical.

### 4. Transparency Builds Trust
The CEO's personal email to customers, the public post-mortem, and the media availability all demonstrated that transparency during crisis builds more trust than perfection during normal times.

### 5. Crisis Reveals Culture
The company's response revealed its true values more clearly than any marketing campaign or values statement. The Series C investor recognized this — crisis behavior is the most reliable signal of leadership quality.

### 6. Fix the System, Not Just the Symptom
The CEO did not just replace defective units. She fixed the internal process that deprioritized safety concerns, hired quality assurance capability, and changed vendor management practices.

## Advisory Application

When advising companies facing potential product or safety crises, use this case to demonstrate that the short-term cost of proactive, transparent response is almost always less than the long-term cost of delay, concealment, or minimization. The question is never "can we afford to recall?" — it is "can we afford not to?"
