# Flywheel Framework

## Purpose

The Flywheel Framework, popularized by Jim Collins, provides a method for identifying and building self-reinforcing virtuous cycles within a business. A flywheel is a set of interconnected activities where each step powers the next, creating compounding momentum over time. Unlike linear strategies (do X, get Y), flywheel strategies produce exponential results because each revolution strengthens the next. Amazon's flywheel — lower prices attract more customers, more customers attract more sellers, more sellers increase selection, more selection attracts more customers — is the canonical example.

## When to Use

- When designing or redesigning a business model
- When the company is growing but the growth feels linear (effort in = results out)
- When trying to identify which activities create compounding advantage
- During strategic planning to focus investment on flywheel-accelerating activities
- When explaining a business model to investors, employees, or partners
- When diagnosing why growth has stalled (a broken flywheel link)
- When evaluating whether a new initiative strengthens or weakens the core flywheel

## Core Model

### Anatomy of a Flywheel
A flywheel has 4-6 interconnected steps arranged in a cycle where each step enables and amplifies the next:

```
    Step 1 ——→ Step 2
      ↑              ↓
    Step 4 ←—— Step 3
```

### Properties of Strong Flywheels
1. **Self-reinforcing**: Each step makes the next step easier or more powerful
2. **Compounding**: Each revolution produces more output than the last with the same input
3. **Defensible**: The flywheel itself becomes a moat — competitors must build the entire cycle, not just one step
4. **Measurable**: Each step has a metric that indicates flywheel health
5. **Simple**: If the flywheel has more than 6 steps, it is probably not a flywheel — it is a flowchart

### Classic Flywheel Examples

**Amazon**: Lower prices → More customers → More sellers → More selection → Lower prices (through scale economics)

**Netflix**: More subscribers → More revenue → More content investment → Better content → More subscribers

**Marketplace**: More buyers → More attractive for sellers → More sellers → More selection → More buyers

### The Anti-Flywheel (Doom Loop)
The inverse: a vicious cycle where each deterioration causes the next. Example: Losing customers → Less revenue → Less investment → Worse product → Losing more customers. Recognizing doom loops early is as important as building flywheels.

## Steps

1. **Map your current activities.** List all the major activities your business performs: acquiring customers, delivering product, collecting data, building brand, etc.

2. **Identify causal links.** For each activity, ask: "Which other activity does this enable or amplify?" Draw arrows between activities that have a causal relationship.

3. **Find the cycle.** Look for closed loops — sequences of activities that form a cycle where the last step feeds back into the first. You may find multiple potential flywheels.

4. **Select the primary flywheel.** Choose the flywheel that is most central to your business model and most likely to compound. You can only invest in one primary flywheel.

5. **Define metrics for each step.** Each flywheel step needs a measurable indicator. If a step's metric is declining, that link in the flywheel is weakening.

6. **Identify the bottleneck.** The flywheel's speed is limited by its weakest link. Find the step with the lowest throughput and invest there.

7. **Remove friction.** For each link in the flywheel, ask: "What slows the transition from this step to the next?" Reducing friction at any point accelerates the entire flywheel.

8. **Test every new initiative against the flywheel.** Before approving any investment, ask: "Does this strengthen a step in the flywheel, or is it orthogonal?" Orthogonal investments dilute flywheel momentum.

9. **Communicate the flywheel to every employee.** Everyone should know the flywheel and understand how their work contributes to it. The flywheel is the company strategy made visual.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Ask founders to draw their flywheel on a whiteboard. If they cannot, they may not have one — and a business without a flywheel is a business without compounding advantage.
- Evaluate strategic proposals by asking: "Which step of the flywheel does this strengthen?" If the answer is "none," the proposal is a distraction.
- Look for doom loops in struggling portfolio companies. Turning a doom loop around often requires a single bold intervention at the weakest link.

### For Product Strategy
- Product features that strengthen the flywheel should be prioritized over features that are independent of it.
- Data network effects are the most powerful flywheel for technology companies. If your product generates data that makes the product better, you have the beginning of a flywheel.

### For Go-to-Market
- Customer acquisition is a flywheel step, not a standalone activity. The best GTM strategies have customers generating more customers (referrals, word of mouth, marketplace effects).
- If customer acquisition costs are rising, the flywheel is not working. In a functioning flywheel, CAC should decrease over time.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing a process with a flywheel.** A sequence of steps with no feedback loop is a process, not a flywheel. The defining feature is that the output of the last step feeds back into the first.

2. **Too many steps.** If your flywheel has 8+ steps, it is fragile. Each additional step is a potential breakpoint. Simplify to 4-6 steps.

3. **Ignoring the weakest link.** Investing in the strongest flywheel step while ignoring the weakest is like upgrading the engine on a car with flat tires.

4. **Assuming the flywheel is automatic.** Flywheels require energy to start and maintain. In the early stages, each revolution requires deliberate, manual effort. Automation comes later.

5. **Investing against the flywheel.** Initiatives that do not strengthen the flywheel actively weaken it by consuming resources. Every dollar and hour has an opportunity cost.

6. **Not measuring flywheel speed.** If you cannot measure the time for one full revolution (or the throughput at each step), you cannot manage the flywheel.

## Related Frameworks

- **Moat Map** — Flywheels are the mechanism that builds and widens moats
- **Power Law Thinking** — Flywheels create power-law outcomes through compounding
- **Strategy Choice Cascade** — The flywheel is the operational expression of "How to Win"
- **Network Effects Types (Hoffman)** — Network effects are a common flywheel driver
- **Competitive Response Model** — A strong flywheel is the best defense against competitive response
- **Opportunity Cost Framework** — Every investment should be evaluated against the flywheel
