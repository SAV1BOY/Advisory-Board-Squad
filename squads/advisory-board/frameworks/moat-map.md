# Moat Map Framework

## Purpose

This framework provides a comprehensive system for identifying, evaluating, building, and defending competitive moats. A moat is a durable competitive advantage that protects a business from competitors and sustains above-average returns over time. Warren Buffett popularized the concept; this framework operationalizes it by mapping five distinct moat types, assessing their depth and durability, and creating action plans to widen them.

## When to Use

- During strategic planning to assess and strengthen competitive position
- When evaluating investment opportunities or acquisition targets
- When a founder claims defensibility — test the claim against this map
- Before entering a market to understand what moat is achievable
- When competitors are closing the gap and you need to identify where to reinvest
- During board reviews of long-term strategy

## Core Model

### The Five Moat Types

#### 1. Network Effects
Value increases as more users join. The strongest and most durable moat type.
- **Direct network effects**: Each user makes the product more valuable for every other user (social networks, messaging)
- **Indirect/cross-side effects**: More users on one side attract more on the other (marketplaces, platforms)
- **Data network effects**: More users generate more data, improving the product for everyone (recommendation engines, maps)
- **Strength test**: Would losing 50% of users make the product less than 50% as valuable?

#### 2. Scale Economics
Cost advantages that grow with size, creating barriers for smaller competitors.
- **Supply-side scale**: Lower unit costs through volume (manufacturing, distribution)
- **Demand-side scale**: Better economics from a larger customer base (spreading fixed costs)
- **Learning-curve effects**: Cumulative experience reduces costs and errors
- **Strength test**: Does a 2x increase in volume create a >2% cost advantage per unit?

#### 3. Brand
Customer willingness to pay a premium or choose you by default, based on reputation and trust.
- **Functional brand**: Known for specific, superior functionality (Google for search)
- **Emotional brand**: Represents identity or aspiration (Apple, Nike)
- **Trust brand**: Chosen because the cost of being wrong is high (McKinsey, AWS)
- **Strength test**: Would customers pay 20%+ more for your product over an identical unbranded alternative?

#### 4. Switching Costs
Costs (financial, procedural, relational) that customers incur to change to a competitor.
- **Financial switching costs**: Contractual penalties, retraining costs, data migration
- **Procedural switching costs**: Learning new workflows, rebuilding integrations
- **Relational switching costs**: Losing relationships, institutional knowledge, trust
- **Strength test**: Would it take a customer more than 6 months and significant expense to switch?

#### 5. Intellectual Property
Legal protections that prevent competitors from copying your advantage.
- **Patents**: Time-limited monopoly on specific inventions
- **Trade secrets**: Proprietary processes, algorithms, or formulas
- **Regulatory licenses**: Government-granted exclusivity (spectrum, banking charters)
- **Proprietary data**: Unique datasets that cannot be replicated
- **Strength test**: Could a well-funded competitor replicate your advantage within 2 years even if they wanted to?

### Moat Depth Assessment
Rate each moat type from 0-5:
- **0**: No moat
- **1**: Nascent — early signs, not yet protective
- **2**: Emerging — provides some advantage, easily challenged
- **3**: Moderate — meaningful barrier, requires significant investment to overcome
- **4**: Strong — substantial barrier, would take years and hundreds of millions to challenge
- **5**: Dominant — effectively unassailable in the foreseeable future

## Steps

1. **Map your current moats.** Score each of the five moat types from 0-5 with specific evidence for each score.

2. **Map competitor moats.** Do the same analysis for your top 3 competitors. Compare moat profiles.

3. **Identify your moat advantage.** Where are you strongest relative to competitors? This is your defensive priority — protect it.

4. **Identify your moat gap.** Where are competitors stronger? This is either a vulnerability to address or a segment to concede.

5. **Design moat-widening actions.** For each moat type where you score 2+, identify specific actions to increase the score by 1 point within 12 months.

6. **Prioritize by durability.** Network effects and switching costs tend to compound over time. Brand and IP tend to be more fragile. Prioritize investments in moats that get stronger with time.

7. **Set moat metrics.** Define measurable indicators for each moat type and track them quarterly.

8. **Review annually.** Moats erode. Technology shifts, regulations change, new entrants find novel approaches. Annual reassessment is essential.

## Application Guidelines

### For Board Advisors
- Every strategic review should include a moat assessment. If a company has no moat, its margins will eventually be competed away.
- Help founders distinguish between competitive advantages (temporary) and moats (durable). Being first to market is an advantage, not a moat.
- When evaluating fundraising, assess whether the capital will be used to widen moats or just grow revenue. Growth without moat-building is borrowing from the future.

### For Startups
- At seed stage, you probably have no moat. Your job is to identify which moat type you can build and take early steps toward it.
- At Series A, you should have a nascent moat (score 1-2) in at least one type.
- At Series B+, you should have a moderate moat (score 3+) in at least one type and be actively widening it.

### For Incumbents
- Your moat is your most valuable asset. Invest in maintaining and widening it before investing in new growth.
- Watch for moat erosion signals: declining pricing power, increasing churn, competitors winning deals you used to win automatically.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing growth with moat.** Growing fast is not a moat. Growing fast because of a structural advantage that compounds is a moat.

2. **Overvaluing IP moats.** Patents expire. Trade secrets leak. Regulatory advantages get lobbied away. IP is the weakest moat type for most technology companies.

3. **Ignoring moat erosion.** Every moat erodes over time unless actively maintained. Technology shifts can destroy moats rapidly (see: Kodak's brand moat in film).

4. **Trying to build all five moats simultaneously.** Focus on 1-2 moat types that are natural to your business model. Spreading moat-building efforts too thin builds no moat at all.

5. **Mistaking customer satisfaction for switching costs.** Customers can be happy and have zero switching costs. True switching costs are structural, not emotional.

6. **Assuming network effects exist when they don't.** Not every multi-user product has network effects. A product used by many people but where each user's value is independent of others has no network effects.
